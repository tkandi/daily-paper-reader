Abstract 

Fully decentralized, peer-to-peer collaborative learning is an emerging distributed deep learning paradigm that, unlike its centralized counterparts, eliminates the need for a central ag-gregator and disseminator, avoiding the single-point-of-failure and bottleneck issues they can bring. Many such approaches – including decentralized federated and gossip learning – use 

parameter averaging , requiring clients to have homogeneous model architectures, whereas others use knowledge distilla-tion as an alternative while leaning on shared public data or centralized control infrastructure. Most decentralized learn-ing approaches also make a one-size-fits-all assumption, i.e., they collaboratively train clients towards a single, globally optimal solution for all clients, when in reality, clients’ un-derlying data distributions might differ such that a globally optimal solution may not be locally optimal for all clients. To enable collaborative learning in environments where hetero-geneity is present in both model architecture and in underlying data distribution, we present SPADE-KD, a fully decentralized serverless model distillation framework that optimizes collab-oration towards personalized rather than global performance. SPADE-KD leverages two mechanisms – adaptive distillation 

and utility-driven peer selection . Adaptive distillation mod-ulates the strength of each incoming model transfer by the performance advantage of the incoming model compared to the native model measured by their task losses, such that a client will distill more aggressively from incoming models that outperform it on its own local data. Utility-driven peer selection uses the loss-informed utility derived from adap-tive distillation to rank prospective partners, focusing on ex-changes that measurably improve the local model while sup-pressing low-utility transfers. Neither mechanism relies on any centralized infrastructure or public datasets. Using federated classification tasks, we demonstrate that SPADE-KD consis-tent outperforms naive decentralized distillation with lower loss and faster convergence and we provide ablation studies of the combined and individual effectivenesses of SPADE-KD’s component mechanisms. 

## Introduction 

As more data is generated at the network edge and utilized to train deep learning models, distributed model training over decentralized data has become an attractive structural paradigm for deep learning, mitigating privacy concerns and

providing more autonomy for participating clients and im-proving communication efficiency between servers and/or clients. While many centralized and semi-centralized dis-tributed learning approaches are facilitated or coordinated through centralized servers, raising concerns about servers being singe points of failures or bottlenecks in collaborative learning among networks of clients, and motivating fully decentralized collaborative learning paradigms. However, distributed learning – centralized and decentral-ized – must contend with issues emerging from the hetero-geneity that is especially characteristic in realistic peer-to-peer settings. Heterogeneity within underlying data distribu-tions poses challenges to distributed learning approaches that target consensus towards a global solution, as they may not adequately serve networks of clients whose data distributions show incongruence enough that a globally optimal solution may not be the best solution – or even an adequate one – for all clients. Heterogeneity in model quality as a result of training capacities or quantity and/or quality of clients’ local data poses opportunities to bridge model performance dis-parity through collaboration but also challenges in preserv-ing performance higher-quality models, mitigating training volatility, and convergence of local models. While many fed-erated learning approaches have been proposed to address these two axes of heterogeneity, heterogeneity in model ar-chitectures make it impossible to use the model parameter-averaging mechanisms at the core of many of them, and therefore knowledge must be instead transferred functionally – via knowledge distillation. Yet the scarce but growing landscape of existing dis-tributed distillation methods inherit two assumptions that can fail in heterogeneous settings: (1) that every model has something to offer that is worth distilling from, and (2) that every potential peer client is worth collaborating with. When peers hold heterogeneous data and unequal competence, both assumptions break — and a naive scheme can drag a capable agent down toward a weaker peer. Even distribution distilla-tion methods that confront that challenge these assumptions assume a level of heterogeneity low enough that distribution variance among clients’ data can be overcome with universal collaboration among clients. In the face of more challenging heterogeneity, we must consider both how a client should distill from models shared with it from other clients and how a client should select peers it wants to receive models from. arXiv:2610.24013v1 [cs.LG] 15 Oct 2026 Our key insight is that both problems share a solution – a client can measure the potential utility of a shared model on its own data and use this signal to determine how much to distill from each shared model and which clients it should query next — i.e., one utility can govern both what a client learns and who it should learn from, in the context of collab-orative learning. This insight yields our proposed approach, Serverless Peer-to-Peer Adaptive Decentralized Knowledge Distillation (SPADE-KD), a framework that builds both the distillation mechanism and the peer selection mechanism from a single utility signal. In SPADE-KD, each client com-putes an adaptive coefficient from the relative task loss of a peer’s model versus its own native model, and uses it to modulate that peer’s contribution in the distillation objec-tive, promoting prospective teacher models that outperform its native model locally and surpressing those that do not. Be-cause this coefficient falls out of distillation as a by-product, we reuse it as a stored utility score that drives peer selec-tion, with a neutral default for never-seen peers that yields exploration at no additional cost. Our contributions are: 

• A decentralized adaptive knowledge distillation method that derives a utility signal γ from the comparative loss advantage of an incoming prospective teacher model and the native student model (Eq. 4) and applies it as a co-effecient to modulate distillation. 

• The re-utilization of that same utility signal γ as an indi-cation of collaboration potential in the formulation of a multi-armed bandit problem for each client to select the optimal peers to request prospective teacher models from to best collaboratively train its native model and minimize loss over its local data. 

• SPADE-KD, a fully decentralized collaborative deep learning framework, that utilizes decentralized adaptive knowledge distillation and utility-based peer selection to account for for networks of clients with heterogeneity in data distributions, model architectures, and model quali-ties within the network. 

## Background & Related Work 

Our work builds on the emerging domain of distributed, col-laborative training among decentralized, autonomous clients with private data. In the traditional deep learning paradigm, clients would be required to send some or all their data to a server for the server to then train the model, presenting issues with communication bandwidth and data privacy. 

Federated & Decentralized Learning Federated learning (FL) (McMahan et al. 2017) proposed having a central server disseminate copies of its model to clients and perform param-eter averaging of the returned client-trained model updates to periodically update its global model, making it unnec-essary for clients to send their data directly to the server and providing consequent benefits in privacy and communi-cation bandwidth utilization. Personalized variations on FL have followed, e.g., proposing adaptations to its structure of collaboration and aggregation (Tan et al. 2022) and using the cosine similarity of model progressions to produce dif-ferentiated server models trained by clusters of clients (Sat-tler, Müller, and Samek 2020), among other approaches. To address the single point of failure and performance bottle-neck issues raised by centralized approaches, decentralized FL approaches (Beltrán et al. 2023) have been proposed and proven competitive to centralized FL, including asyn-chronous, peer-to-peer gossip learning (Hegedűs, Danner, and Jelasity 2019). These decentralized adaptations include some approaches that address data and training heterogeneity among clients, proposing mechanisms such as optimal peer selection and weighted aggregation to overcome heterogene-ity and reach consensus on a global optimal solution (Tang et al. 2022). Other approaches target more localized optimal solutions by exploiting observable heterogeneity as clients train (Ma et al. 2024). 

Distributed Knowledge Distillation The utilization of knowledge distillation in distributed, collaborative deep learning aims to overcome some of the detriments of pa-rameter averaging, namely the requirement for homogeneous model architectures and the catastrophic forgetting incurred by the averaging of divergent client models. Some decen-tralized FL approaches leverage knowledge distillation as an intermediary mechanism to discern compatible collaboration partners (Jeong and Kountouris 2023). Other proposals im-plement mutual distillation to optimize information exchange from peer-aggregated models and peer selection (Soltani et al. 2024) in networks of high data heterogeneity, supple-menting but not supplanting the parameter averaging-based aggregation mechanism. FL has also evolved to include fed-erated knowledge distillation approaches (Wu et al. 2022; Kalra et al. 2023) that leverage mutual knowledge distillation to produce compact proxy models that can be sent with better communication efficiency over the network to then be aggre-gated via parameter averaging for better training robustness. These approaches yield the additional benefit that because native client models are distilling into these compact proxy models and only these proxy models are being exchanged, only the proxy models must have identical model architec-tures, enabling the native client models to have heterogeneous architectures. To enable collaborative decentralized learning between heterogeneous model architectures towards a glob-ally optimal solution space, decentralized federated mutual learning (Khalil et al. 2024) has been proposed, in which clients in a decentralized network take turns mutually distill-ing partners’ models alongside their own over their local data, oscillating between local training and distillation. However, this and similar approaches do not consider the possibility of data distributions being heterogeneous to the point of incon-gruence in which training jointly on clients’ data – directly or indirectly via client-trained model updates – can be detri-mental to the training of any single client. To that end, one LLM-specific orchestrated gossip learning via distillation approach (Singh et al. 2026) proposes to have a centralized matchmaker that learns throughout the collaborative train-ing phase to form client pairs based on training performance and client-provided profiles. To our knowledge, our approach is the first to provide a general solution to fully decentral-ized collaborative learning via knowledge distillation among clients that addresses both heterogeneous model architecture as well as data heterogeneity and training disparity. Peer-to-Peer Adaptive Knowledge Distillation 

To facilitate collaboration in decentralized networks with het-erogeneous data, models, and training history, we introduce Serverless Peer-to-Peer Adaptive Decentralized Knowledge Distillation – SPADE-KD – a fully decentralized approach that adaptively modulates distillation from received models using a utility signal derived from local task loss advantage and reuses that same signal to drive utility-based peer selec-tion.

Model & Training Heterogeneity We model deep learn-ing model heterogeneity by implementing size-based tiers within the model family for each task. For the MNIST and 

FEMNIST handwritten digit recognition task scenarios, we employ a family of four CNN classifiers. The largest is the standard LeNet-5 ( ∼61K parameters), while the three more compact variants ( ∼29K, ∼12.6K, and ∼3.4K parameters) share a common single-convolution template – a 3 × 3 con-volutional layer with 4 × 4 max-pooling followed by a single fully-connected hidden layer – with the channel width and hidden dimension scaled down at each tier. At the start of each experiment, model architectures are assigned round-robin to each client and clients pre-train their model on local data at different levels, randomly set.

Results & Discussion 

We monitor loss as our primary performance metric 2. Re-sults are shown for the FEMNIST scenario in Fig. 1, the label-swapped MNIST scenario in Fig. 2, and the Speech-Commands scenario in Fig. 3. Final performance losses and relative performance advantages are shown in Table 3. These results reflect the performance metrics of all clients averaged across 5 unique seeds for the MNIST and FEMNIST scenarios and 10 unique seeds for the SpeechCommands scenario to ac-count for noisiness incurred from scare data availability. Our experiments demonstrate that overall, while both its adaptive distillation and utility-based peer selection components each provide tangible performance advantages, the combination of both in SPADE-KD yields better performance than each component in isolation in virtually all cases. 

Component Effectiveness The impacts of each compo-nent mechanism do vary. Earlier in the collaboration phase (as seen in the FEMNIST or MNIST scenarios) or when deal-ing with scarce local data availability (as seen in the Speech-Commands scenario), adaptive distillation without utility-based peer selection tends to be the more advantageous com-ponent, achieving higher initial performance gains than peer selection without adaptive distillation. However, as collab-orative training continues and native models become more stationary, utility-based peer selection without adaptive dis-tillation seems to provide a greater advantage than adaptive distillation without utility-based peer selection. We can even observe that in the FEMNIST and MNIST scenarios, utility-based peer selection in isolation appears to match or slightly exceed the accuracy of SPADE-KD; however, even in these cases, SPADE-KD still achieves lower overall loss. 

Managing Quality Disparity We observe that our ap-proach yields the lowest loss for both higher-quality models (via P10 losses) and lower-quality models (via P90 losses) in virtually all cases. This implies that our method is effective in suppressing the distillation influence of lower-quality models and preserving the training progress of higher-quality mod-els while still enabling the unidirectional flow of knowledge

We observe a specific excep-tion in the massively distributed SpeechCommands scenario – our approach does not serve lower-quality client models as well (Fig. 3c); we can attribute this to the stochastic noise expected in the weakest models training over the fewest local samples, but this leaves room for future exploration. 

Diminishing Scores We observe that clients’ score values for contacted peers decrease over time. This makes sense – while contribution potential is high in earlier rounds of training, as a client has more rounds to train on its local data, the potential for another client’s model trained on that client’s data diminishes. This implicit decaying of score also acts as a natural defense against stale score values, such that a peer with a stale high score may be selected for knowledge transfer but that interaction would update that stale score. Even then, optimal peer selection still selects the most useful peers to request models from, and as scores diminish, clients are encouraged to contact unexplored peers due to the default neutral contribution potential γ0 = 0 .5.

## Conclusion 

We presented SPADE-KD, a fully decentralized, serverless framework for collaborative learning among clients that are heterogeneous in their data distributions, model architec-tures, and model quality. Its core idea is that a single, locally computed utility signal — the relative task-loss advantage of a received model over a client’s own model on its local data — can act as an adaptive coefficient for how much a client distills from a received model and as a bandit score for which candidate peers it queries next. Across three clas-sification scenarios spanning artificial and natural hetero-geneity, adaptive distillation and utility-based peer selection each yielded tangible advantages, and their combination in SPADE-KD generally achieved the lowest loss — surpassing a the naive decentralized distillation baseline and either com-ponent in isolation. However, even then, a direct comparison between FedKD and the decentralized distillation approaches we present poses various incompatibilities. First, this federated approach assumes a different communication structure altogether, where clients interact only with the server; thus, we im-plement a star topology with the server at the middle instead of the small-world peer-to-peer topology (Watts and Stro-gatz 1998). Second, FedKD uses mutual distillation between its mentor and mentee models to transfer knowledge from the rest of the network, whereas our approaches implement distinct local training and collaborative distillation steps. To attempt to mitigate this, we set αt = αd = 1 to retain both the full task loss and distillation losses. Third, FedKD and other federated knowledge distillation approaches (Kalra et al. 2023) rely on compact proxy models to carry informa-tion between clients through server-orchestrated parameter aggregation, making their performance dependent on the structure and capacity of the proxy model architecture it-self. We consider that in real world scenarios considering a network of heterogeneous model architectures and inferred heterogeneous computational capacities, the proxy model ar-chitecture should be trainable by all clients. Therefore, we select the smallest common model architecture for the in-termediary proxy model. Even then, the existence of these factors, however mitigated, and others make direct compari-son challenging between federated KD and our decentralized KD methods. Future work will compare our methods and ab-lations against state-of-the-art decentralized heterogeneity-aware distillation methods as they continue to emerge.
