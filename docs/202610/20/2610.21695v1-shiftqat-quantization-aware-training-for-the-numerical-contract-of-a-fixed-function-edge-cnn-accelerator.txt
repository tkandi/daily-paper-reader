SHIFTQAT: QUANTIZATION-AWARE TRAINING FOR THE NUMERICAL CONTRACT
OF A FIXED-FUNCTION EDGE CNN ACCELERATOR
Oguzhan Buyuksolak 1 Osman Erman Okman 1 Mehmet Gorkem Ulkar 1
ABSTRACT
Fixed-function CNN accelerators make edge inference fast and energy-eﬀicient, but they execute only networks
that obey a precise numerical contract. On the MAX7800x family, this contract requires power-of-two, per-tensor,
symmetric scales, 8-bit activations and biases, integer-only arithmetic, and one statically configured shift per layer.
We present ShiftQAT, the quantization-aware training (QAT) flow we developed for this family, which produces
networks satisfying the contract by construction. It is the default training path of ai8x-training, the family’s open-
source training toolchain. ShiftQAT resolves residual, concatenation, and weight-sharing dependencies so that
activation scales propagate statically and each layer’s requantization reduces to one offline-configured shift. It
also trains weights and biases on a joint power-of-two grid that fits the 8-bit bias budget. Among the compared
methods, only ShiftQAT satisfies all of the contract’s scaling constraints, including signed 8-bit activation storage.
Even so, on ImageNet it matches the W8A8 accuracy drop of less constrained QAT methods and stays within 2.9
percentage points of FP32 at W4A8. On MAX78002 silicon, ShiftQAT-trained MobileNetV2 and ResNet-18
classify ImageNet images in 14.6 and 8.4 ms at 5.1 and 2.8 mJ per inference.

INTRODUCTION
Deep neural networks are increasingly deployed on battery-
powered edge devices, close to the sensors that produce
their inputs. General-purpose microcontrollers offer only a
few megabytes of memory and limited arithmetic through-
put. This has motivated software stacks for running quan-
tized models on MCUs (Lai et al., 2018; David et al., 2021;
Lin et al., 2020), as well as dedicated low-power CNN accel-
erators integrated alongside the MCU core (Giordano et al.,
2022; Huang et al., 2024). Quantization is the enabling step
for both, because it shrinks the memory footprint and lets in-
ference run in fixed-point arithmetic (Gholami et al., 2022;
Nagel et al., 2021).
The quantization literature usually treats hardware friendli-
ness as a spectrum of desirable properties, such as uniform,
symmetric, power-of-two, and per-tensor scaling. Methods
are then compared by how much accuracy they recover at
a given bit-width. Fixed-function accelerators turn these
properties into hard requirements. We call the complete
set of arithmetic rules that an accelerator’s datapath imple-
ments its numerical contract. A network either satisfies this
contract or cannot be compiled for the device at all. On
Analog Devices’ MAX7800x family (Analog Devices Inc.,
2023), the contract requires power-of-two, per-tensor, sym-

integer-only inference. Each layer has a single shift that is

This paper presents ShiftQAT, a QAT flow that produces

developed ShiftQAT at Analog Devices as the default
quantization-aware training path of ai8x-training, the open-
source training toolchain for the MAX7800x family. This
paper documents its design, the datapath constraint behind
each of its mechanisms, and its evaluation on ImageNet and
on MAX78002 silicon. Each of its mechanisms answers a
specific constraint of the datapath, and two of them carry
the design. The first is static scale propagation. Because
the datapath cannot rescale dynamically, ShiftQAT resolves
all rescaling before deployment. It folds each layer’s activa-
tion scale forward into the next layer’s bias and shift, so that
intermediate layers never recover real-valued scale. This
requires positively homogeneous activations and a scale-
consistent graph. ShiftQAT therefore resolves every resid-
ual addition, concatenation, and shared-weight layer so that
propagation remains exact on real topologies. The second
is a joint power-of-two weight/bias scale, re-estimated at ev-
ery training iteration. Once biases have absorbed upstream
activation scales and must still fit in 8 bits, weights and bi-
ases compete for the same power-of-two grid. Training on

BACKGROUND AND RELATED WORK
Integer-only inference and dyadic requantization.
Ja-
cob et al. (2018) formalized integer-only inference. Each
layer’s real-valued output multiplier is decomposed offline
into a fixed-point mantissa and a power-of-two shift, and
biases are stored as 32-bit integers at the accumulator’s
scale.
HAWQ-V3 (Yao et al., 2021) restricts these mul-
tipliers to dyadic numbers, and TensorRT (Migacz, 2017)
and CMSIS-NN (Lai et al., 2018) provide calibrated or Q-
format fixed-point kernels for GPUs and Cortex-M CPUs.
Figure 1a shows this per-layer recovery structure. On pro-
cessors with a general integer multiplier it is inexpensive. A
fixed-function accelerator changes where the rescaling can
happen. It can only be a statically configured power-of-two
shift per layer, never a multiplication by an arbitrary man-
tissa.
Moving scales across layers. An activation f is positively
homogeneous if f(sz) = s f(z) for all s > 0, as holds
for ReLU and Leaky ReLU. For such activations, a per-
channel or per-tensor scale can be moved through the ac-
tivation into the next layer’s weights. Data-free quantiza-
tion (Nagel et al., 2019) exploits this property for cross-layer
weight equalization. ShiftQAT uses the same identity for a
different purpose. It defers every activation scale to the net-
work output, so that intermediate layers never recover real-
valued scale. It adds the topology handling (Section 4.3)
and the bias treatment (Section 4.5) needed to make this
hold under an 8-bit bias budget on real CNNs.
Hardware-friendly quantizers. PACT (Choi et al., 2018),
FAQ (McKinstry et al., 2019), LSQ (Esser et al., 2020),
and differentiable range/step-size methods (Uhlich et al.,
2020) learn clipping ranges or step sizes during QAT.
TQT (Jain et al., 2020) trains power-of-two per-tensor
thresholds, and APoT (Li et al., 2020) uses sums of pow-
ers of two as non-uniform levels.
Vandersteegen et al.
(2021) combine per-layer power-of-two scales with 4-bit
weights, optionally through a lookup table that maps 4-bit
codes to non-uniform 8-bit weights.
Post-training meth-
ods such as AdaRound (Nagel et al., 2020), BRECQ (Li
et al., 2021), HPTQ (Habi et al., 2021), and EPTQ (Gor-
don et al., 2024) avoid retraining. The strongest of them
rely on per-channel scales, which the MAX7800x datapath
does not provide. HPTQ is closest in spirit to ShiftQAT.
It targets uniform, symmetric, power-of-two quantization,
and ShiftQAT adopts its activation-threshold search (Sec-
tion 4.2). Lower-bit and binary/ternary schemes (Hubara
et al., 2016; Zhou et al., 2018; Zhang et al., 2018; Jung et al.,
2019; Yang et al., 2021) pursue compression beyond the 8/4-
bit regime studied here.
Edge
CNN
accelerators.
The
MAX78000
and
MAX78002 integrate a CNN accelerator with 1/2/4/8-
bit weights and 8-bit activations alongside a Cortex-M4
core. Independent studies have characterized their latency
and energy on small vision and audio workloads (Wang
et al., 2022; Giordano et al., 2022; Moosmann et al., 2023;
Huang et al., 2024; Dabbous et al., 2025).
This paper
describes the training side, namely the QAT flow of the
family’s own toolchain, together with ImageNet-scale
deployments on it.

4.1
Batch-Normalization Folding
Batch normalization (Ioffe, 2015) is folded into the preced-
ing convolution or linear layer before quantization, follow-
ing Jacob et al. (2018). This is required by C6. The acceler-
ator has no normalization stage, so its aﬀine transform must
be absorbed into the weights and biases that the contract
governs.

4.3
Topology Dependency Resolution
Constraint C7 means that wherever two branches meet, their
tensors must already share a scale, because the datapath
cannot rescale one branch at runtime to match the other.
Independently proposed per-layer scales do not have this
property, so they must be reconciled before propagation.
ShiftQAT resolves three cases (Figure 3).
Residual connections. For an element-wise addition with
inputs from L1 and L2 (scales Sx1, Sx2) and output scale
Sx3, both inputs are reassigned Sx3. The two addends then
share a grid, so the accelerator adds them directly and satu-
rates the sum to the 8-bit range, with no runtime alignment.
Concatenation. The output range of a concatenation is set
by its widest input, so all inputs adopt the maximum of their
proposed scales. If Sx1 > Sx2, then Sx2 ←Sx1.
Weight sharing. In FPN-style heads (Lin et al., 2016), one
layer L4 may be invoked on inputs from L1, L2, and L3 at
different points in the network. A single set of weights and
a single shift must then serve every invocation. The scale
Sx4 is proposed from the union of L4’s outputs across invo-
cations, and the inputs L1–L3 are assigned the maximum
of their scales.
Sharing scales at additions and concatenations follows the
practice of TQT (Jain et al., 2020). ShiftQAT additionally
resolves weight sharing and then folds all scales forward
(Section 4.4). In each case the rule favors the larger scale, or
the scale of the merge point. This trades some resolution on
the narrower branch for the guarantee that no branch clips.

EVALUATION
5.1
Setup
We quantize MobileNetV2 and ResNet-18 starting from
the oﬀicial PyTorch FP32 checkpoints (Ansel et al., 2024),
trained on ImageNet at 224×224 (Deng et al., 2009). We
evaluate two settings, W8A8 and W4A8. For a fair compar-
ison with published results, all methods in Tables 2 and 3,
including ShiftQAT, use 32-bit biases (nb = 32), as is stan-
dard in the quantization literature. These tables therefore
isolate the effect of the weight and activation constraints
(C1–C3, C5–C7). The deployed networks in Section 5.3 use
8-bit biases (nb = 8), as the hardware requires (C4). QAT
runs for the last 50 of 200 epochs for MobileNetV2 and for
the last 30 of 120 epochs for ResNet-18. Appendix B gives
the full hyperparameters. All ShiftQAT results come from
a single run per configuration.
Baseline numbers are taken from the cited papers.
Be-
cause the baselines use different FP32 references (e.g.,
71.878% for our MobileNetV2 checkpoint versus 72.62%
in EPTQ’s), we compare accuracy drops (∆) rather than ab-
solute accuracy. We treat differences below about 1 pp as
comparable, given single runs and cross-paper references.
We also mark each method’s regime, post-training (PTQ)
or quantization-aware (QAT), because PTQ methods use far
less compute than a QAT schedule. No baseline can be de-
ployed on MAX78002 without violating part of the contract,
so all comparisons are accuracy-only.

5.3
End-to-End Deployment on MAX78002
MAX78002 supports 1/2/4/8-bit weights with 8-bit acti-
vations. We deploy MobileNetV2 and ResNet-18 trained
with the same ShiftQAT recipe, with mixed 4/8-bit weights
and 8-bit activations. Unlike in Tables 2 and 3, the biases
are quantized to 8 bits, as the hardware requires (C4), so
these networks satisfy the full contract. The trained check-
points are compiled with ai8x-synthesis, the toolchain’s
open-source synthesis tool, with no additional calibration.
Because full-size ImageNet models exceed the accelerator’s
on-chip weight memory, the deployed variants are reduced.
MobileNetV2 uses width multiplier m = 0.75 and ResNet-
18 uses m = 0.5, both with 128×128 inputs. Layers wider
than 1024 channels are capped at 1024, and each stride-
2 convolution is replaced by 2×2 max-pooling followed
by a stride-1 convolution (Appendix B.2).
The mixed-
precision assignment keeps the input and output layers at
8-bit weights and concentrates the 4-bit layers in the inte-
rior of the network. This follows a long-standing conven-
tion in low-bit quantization, where the first and last layers
are kept at higher precision because they are the most sensi-
tive to quantization error (Rastegari et al., 2016; Zhou et al.,
2018; Choi et al., 2018; Esser et al., 2020). TQT’s authors
likewise attribute part of their INT4 accuracy shortfall to
not keeping the first and last layers at higher precision (Jain
et al., 2020). Hessian-based analyses also confirm that sen-
sitivity to quantization varies strongly across layers (Dong


ShiftQAT: Quantization-Aware Training for a Fixed-Function Edge Accelerator
et al., 2019). The input layer sees raw pixels through only
three input channels and holds few parameters, so 8 bits
there cost little memory, while the output layer directly pro-
duces the logits. Latency and energy are measured with
the MAX78002 evaluation kit’s on-board power monitor
in its windowed energy-accumulation (CNN power) mode.
Firmware instrumentation, generated by the synthesis tool,
marks idle and active windows with trigger signals. The
monitor accumulates power on the VCOREA rail, which
supplies the CNN accelerator as well as the MCU logic, and
times the active window. It subtracts the idle power, so that
E = (A −I) T, where A and I are the active and idle
power and T is the active period. Each measured operation
is averaged over 100 repetitions. Weight loading is mea-
sured as a separate stage and is not included in Table 4. The
CNN accelerator runs at 200 MHz. Table 4 reports infer-
ence only, obtained by subtracting the input-loading stage
from the input-loading-plus-inference stage.
Table 4 reports the results. The deployed MobileNetV2 runs
in 14.6 ms (about 68 inferences per second) at 5.1 mJ per
inference, and ResNet-18 runs in 8.4 ms (about 119 infer-
ences per second) at 2.8 mJ. Every layer executes on the
accelerator in integer arithmetic, and only the final output
rescale runs on the Arm core. These are ImageNet-scale
CNNs running on a microcontroller-class device at millisec-
ond latency and millijoule energy. The memory columns
show what drove the model design.
Both variants store
about 2 MB of weights, most of MAX78002’s 2,340 KiB
weight memory, whose effective capacity is further reduced
for layers with more than 64 channels. On average they store
roughly 7 bits per weight for MobileNetV2 and roughly 5.5
bits for ResNet-18, so the 4-bit option was used far more
heavily for ResNet-18. All biases occupy exactly one byte
each, as C4 requires.
Reduced variants. To fit in on-chip weight memory, the
deployed networks are narrower than the full-size models,
take 128×128 inputs, and use mixed 4/8-bit weights. They
also use 8-bit rather than 32-bit biases. Their accuracy is
therefore not directly comparable to the full-size results in
Section 5.2. Table 4 demonstrates what the flow enables on
silicon, not a controlled accuracy comparison.

LESSONS FROM BUILDING AND DEPLOYING
THE FLOW
L1.
The contract, not the bit-width, is the specifica-
tion. “W4A8” underdetermines a deployable network. Two
W4A8 networks can differ in symmetry, scale granularity,
bias width, and where rescaling happens, and only one of
them may compile. Writing the contract down explicitly
(Table 1) before designing the training flow is what made
each mechanism in Section 4 necessary rather than optional.
L2. Constraints interact, so satisfy them jointly. Each
constraint in isolation has a known solution. The diﬀiculty
comes from their interaction. Folding activation scales for-
ward (C6, C7) moves them into biases, which must then fit
in 8 bits (C4) on the same power-of-two grid as the weights
(C2). The joint, per-iteration weight/bias scale exists only
because of this interaction.
L3.
Topology, not arithmetic, is where static scaling
breaks. The propagation identity itself (Eq. 5) is a few lines
of algebra. Making it valid on real networks required re-
solving every point where branches merge or weights are
reused (Section 4.3). Any flow targeting a datapath without
dynamic cross-branch rescaling must solve this before it can
compile ResNet- or FPN-style graphs.
L4. On-chip weight memory shapes the deployed model.
The deployed models were sized to fill the weight memory
(Table 4). Width, input resolution, the 1024-channel limit,
and the 4/8-bit mix were all chosen so that the network fits.
Wider or higher-resolution deployments come from more
weight memory or lower average bits per weight, and a QAT
flow’s leverage lies in making the lower-bit option accurate
enough to use. ShiftQAT’s W4A8 results are what make
4-bit interior layers a practical way to fit larger networks.
L5.
Make training emit the hardware configuration.
The PoT scaler (Section 4.6) computes, at every iteration,
the exact value that each layer’s output-shift register will
hold. A trained checkpoint therefore maps deterministically
to integer weights, 8-bit biases, and shift values. No post-
training calibration step is needed, so the hardware never
runs a configuration that training did not see. In practice,
we found this property more valuable than any single accu-
racy trick, because it makes the path from training to silicon
predictable.

DISCUSSION AND SCOPE
Beyond MAX7800x. The constraints in Table 1 are typical
of shift-based fixed-point datapaths, and the mechanisms of
Section 4 do not depend on MAX7800x specifics beyond
them. On accelerators that lack a free per-layer shifter, static
propagation would additionally remove per-layer rescaling
from the datapath. On MAX78002, whose output shift is
free, its value lies in making the network executable at all.
Closing the 4-bit gap. Learned power-of-two thresholds,
as in TQT (Jain et al., 2020), remain compatible with the
contract, because the learned scales are still powers of two.
Adopting them in ShiftQAT is a direct way to narrow the
W4A8 gap to QAT methods that relax the contract.

CONCLUSION
Fixed-function edge accelerators define a numerical con-
tract, and a QAT flow for them must be designed to that con-
tract rather than to a bit-width. ShiftQAT meets the full con-
tract of the MAX7800x family. It resolves topology depen-
dencies so that activation scales propagate statically, and
it trains weights and biases on a shared power-of-two grid
that fits an 8-bit bias budget. Under the contract’s scaling
constraints, it matches the W8A8 accuracy drop of less con-
strained QAT methods and stays within 2.9 pp of FP32 at
W4A8. With 8-bit biases, ShiftQAT-trained networks run
end-to-end on MAX78002 at millisecond latency and milli-
joule energy per inference. The flow is open source and is
the default training path of the MAX7800x toolchain, and
we expect its constraint-to-mechanism design to carry over
to other fixed-function accelerators.

A
HARDWARE-FRIENDLY QUANTIZATION
PROPERTIES AND THEIR MAX7800X
MANDATE
Uniform quantization.
A uniform quantizer Q(x) =
⌊x/∆⌉+ Z uses a single step size ∆and zero-point Z
across the range, so multiply-accumulate operations need
no lookup tables or branching.
The accelerator stores
weights, biases, and data as fixed-point Q7 integers, which
implies a uniform step; there is no documented non-uniform
or lookup-table dequantization path.
Symmetric quantization. Fixing Z = 0 gives Q(x) =
⌊x/∆⌉and removes the zero-point cross terms from every
product. The accelerator’s accumulate-and-shift datapath
assumes zero-centered operands. The documented number
format is signed two’s complement with no offset term, so
a nonzero zero-point would need an offset correction the
datapath does not describe (C1).
Power-of-two scaling. With ∆= 2s/2n−1 for integer s,
Q(x) = ⌊x · 2n−1/2s⌉, and every rescaling becomes a
shift. On MAX7800x this is not a preference. The per-
layer output shift can only multiply by powers of two, so
a non-power-of-two scale cannot be realized without a gen-
eral multiplier on the output path (C2).
Per-tensor quantization. Per-channel scales improve ac-
curacy (Habi et al., 2021; Li et al., 2021; Gordon et al.,
2024), but they require a scale per output channel at infer-
ence. The accelerator’s output shift is configured per layer,
so per-channel scaling would require per-channel shifts or
a runtime multiply, neither of which is documented for this
hardware generation (C3).

B.1
Hyperparameters
MobileNetV2.
We use SGD with momentum 0.9 and

Scale estimation.
Activation statistics are collected as
2048-bin histograms in one forward pass over the train-
ing set, without backpropagation, immediately before QAT
starts. Activation outliers are removed with a z-score thresh-
old of 8.0. The activation-threshold search evaluates five
power-of-two candidates, the non-clipping threshold and its
first four halvings. The weight/bias range bound uses the
0.999 quantile for 8-bit layers and the 0.9 quantile for 4-bit
layers. Total per-layer shifts are clamped to [−15, 15].
B.2
Hardware Implementation Details
To fit the memory and architectural limits of MAX78002,
we made the following changes for deployment:
• Input resolution was set to 128×128.
• MobileNetV2 used width multiplier 0.75, and ResNet-
18 used width multiplier 0.5.
• Layers wider than 1024 channels were capped at 1024
channels, the hardware’s per-layer channel limit.
• Each stride-2 conv2d was replaced by 2×2 max-
pooling followed by a stride-1 conv2d.
• Biases were quantized to 8 bits, as the hardware re-
quires (C4). The accuracy comparisons in Tables 2
and 3 instead use 32-bit biases for all methods, match-
ing the published baselines.
B.3
Compute Resources
All training ran on two NVIDIA RTX 4090 GPUs with
24 GB each. Runs resumed from a pretrained checkpoint
took approximately 12 hours, and runs from scratch took
approximately 48 hours.
