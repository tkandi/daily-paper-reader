# GeoTrain: Energy-Aware Automatic Parallelism for Foundation Model Training on Heterogeneous Geo-Distributed Resources 

## Bui-Khanh Hoang, TianZhang He, Mohammad Goudarzi ∗

Faculty of Information Technology, Monash University, Melbourne, Australia 

## Abstract 

Training foundation models at the frontier typically requires large-scale dedicated and centralized clusters with fast in-terconnects - infrastructure that is prohibitively expensive and generally accessible only to well-resourced organiza-tions. Decentralized training over pooled, geographically distributed GPUs offers an alternative, but introduces sub-stantial heterogeneity in compute, memory, power, and net-work performance. Existing distributed training systems are primarily designed for clusters with fast, low-latency inter-connects and typically use all available GPUs when configur-ing parallel training. In geo-distributed environments, how-ever, slower devices and weak network links can become bot-tlenecks, while optimizing solely for throughput overlooks significant energy cost. Efficiently selecting and configuring a subset of heterogeneous GPUs therefore requires jointly reasoning about resource characteristics, model partitioning, and pipeline placement. We present GeoTrain, an automatic parallelism system that jointly optimizes training performance and energy con-sumption for heterogeneous geo-distributed GPU environ-ments. GeoTrain simultaneously selects GPUs, partitions model layers, and determines pipeline configurations while accounting for heterogeneous compute, memory, power, and network constraints. To efficiently explore this large com-binatorial search space, GeoTrain employs an optimized memetic algorithm with specialized genetic operators and local search. Experiments with decoder-only Transformer models on real-world and simulated heterogeneous topolo-gies demonstrate that GeoTrain reduces iteration time and energy consumption by up to 61 .4% and 11 .7%, respectively, over the existing state-of-the-art approaches. It also finds the exact optimum on verifiable instances and scales beyond exact methods as the number of GPUs increases, while expos-ing favorable energy-time trade-offs, including cases where its time-optimal distributed training plan is both faster and more energy-efficient than the baselines.

## 1 Introduction 

Training foundation models, such as GPT-3 and LLaMA, requires distributing computation across hundreds or thou-sands of GPUs [2, 29]. As models continue to scale, organi-sations are increasingly pooling all available GPU resources, including heterogeneous hardware spanning multiple gen-erations and geographically distributed clusters linked by wide-area networks with diverse bandwidth and latency characteristics. Making efficient use of such infrastructure is essential to reducing both training time and the considerable energy expenditure of large-scale model training. Systems such as Alpa [35] and AMP [9] have advanced the automation of parallelism planning for distributed train-ing. Also, a separate line of work, DTFM [34], SWARM [22], Sailor [25], Metis [30], and CocktailSGD [32], has begun to address training over heterogeneous or geo-distributed resources directly. However, across these efforts, three ma-jor assumptions persist that limit their applicability to the heterogeneous and geo-distributed paradigm: 

Full-GPUs participation. Existing systems assume full-cluster participation, where all available GPUs are always used for training. This reduces the problem to optimizing configurations over a fixed set of devices, avoiding the com-binatorial complexity of GPU subset selection. However, this assumption becomes inefficient in heterogeneous, geo-distributed settings: slow GPUs and weak interconnects can dominate iteration time and negate the benefit of additional parallelism. As shown in Fig. 2a, selecting an optimal subset of 𝑘 = 4 GPUs from 7 heterogeneous devices reduces iter-ation time by 75% compared to using the full cluster. This demonstrates that carefully chosen subsets can outperform full-cluster execution in heterogeneous environments. 

Uniform stage partitioning. Prior systems partition model layers uniformly across pipeline stages, implicitly assuming homogeneous compute capacity across GPUs. This assump-tion breaks under heterogeneity, where uniform assignment

Energy-unaware optimization. Existing automatic par-allelism systems optimize solely for training throughput or iteration time, ignoring energy consumption. However, as the environmental and financial costs of large-scale training continue to rise [20, 26], energy efficiency has become a crit-ical objective. Importantly, equivalent training speeds can exhibit substantially different energy profiles across pipeline configurations due to variations in GPU selection, layer place-ment, and micro-batch sizing, especially in heterogeneous environments with diverse power characteristics. In this paper, we present GeoTrain, a system that auto-matically discovers efficient pipeline parallelism plans for training on heterogeneous, geo-distributed GPUs. To ad-dress limitations of prior work, it employs an optimized Memetic Algorithm (MA) with domain-specific operators to co-optimize three coupled decisions: which GPUs to use, how 

to partition model layers, and where to place each partition. GeoTrain encodes a plan as an integer chromosome, cap-turing all three decisions in one encoding. A key design is its fitness function: an analytical model that scores each candidate by both iteration time and energy under the het-erogeneous, geo-distributed setting, so the entire search is driven toward configurations that jointly minimize itera-tion time and energy. The MA evolves chromosomes using specialized genetic operators (i.e., crossover, mutation, and local search). The crossover cuts at training stage boundaries so offspring inherit whole stages from each parent, greed-ily keeps the fittest cut, and occasionally allows a random mid-stage cut for diversity. The mutations employ different strategies at varying granularity to refine solutions gradually, escape local optima, and broaden exploration. A local search then finds the training pipeline’s bottleneck and rebalances the workload to relieve it. Figure 1 illustrates the resulting scheduling effect by comparing GeoTrain with standard uniform planning using all available GPUs. Together, these components let GeoTrain explore the plan space efficiently and converge quickly toward balanced, low-cost pipelines. In designing GeoTrain, we make following contributions: 

• Heterogeneity-aware Pipeline Planning Unlike prior systems that typically assume all available GPUs must be used, GeoTrain jointly selects a subset of GPUs and determines a non-uniform layer partition across pipeline stages. It accounts for heterogeneous compute capacities and asymmetric network links to avoid slow devices and poorly connected links, while balancing per-stage cost to eliminate stragglers. This enables GeoTrain to construct balanced pipelines that achieve lower iteration time than full-cluster or uniform partitioning. 

• Time-Energy-aware optimization. We formulate par-allelism planning as an optimization that jointly mini-mizes iteration time and energy consumption. An ana-lytical energy cost model captures GPU-specific power profiles under the 1F1B pipeline schedule [14, 15], while a user-tunable weighting parameter allows operators to navigate the time-energy trade-off. By varying the pa-rameter, GeoTrain can produce energy-saving, balanced, or time-optimal training configurations, enabling opera-tors to select operating points along the Pareto frontier according to their performance and energy requirements.

It combines stage-aware crossover, multi-granularity mutation, and bottleneck-driven local search to efficiently explore candidate configurations and refine promising solutions. 

• Extensive real-world experiments. ## 2 Preliminaries 

Training foundation models involves repeatedly processing training batches through forward and backward passes to compute gradients. When models exceed single-GPU mem-ory, distributed training partitions computation across mul-tiple GPUs using parallelism strategies. 

2.1 Parallelism Methods 

Distributed training commonly uses three forms of paral-lelism: Pipeline parallelism (PP) partitions model layers into stages, each mapped to different devices [4, 14]. Micro-batches flow through the pipeline, with communication oc-curring at stage boundaries for activations and gradients. We focus on PP because its communication is confined to stage boundaries, making it well suited to geo-distributed en-vironments compared with Data parallelism (DP) [10], which required frequent gradient synchronization, and Tensor par-allelism (TP) [24], which incurs substantial fine-grained intra-layer communication overhead. The work could be extended to incorporate DP and TP to exploit high-speed interconnects between co-located GPUs, but this is outside our current fo-cus and left for future work.

## 3 Related Work 

In this section, we review relevant work, identify its gaps, and position our contributions. 

Automatic parallelism planning. Alpa [35] automates inter- and intra-operator parallelism using dynamic program-ming and integer linear programming, respectively. AMP [9] uses a heterogeneity-aware cost model for uneven layers and heterogeneous accelerators, while Galvatron [11] auto-mates hybrid parallelism via dynamic programming. These works automate parallelism planning but do not address geo-distributed topology or energy-aware optimization. 

Heterogeneous and geo-distributed training. DTFM [34] addresses heterogeneous networks with explicit bandwidth

and latency, but operates on a given parallelization plan. SWARM [22] targets poorly connected, heterogeneous de-vices through randomized, adaptively rebalanced pipelines, while CocktailSGD [32] reduces communication through gradient compression. Sailor [25] and HGTrainer [36] ad-dress heterogeneous geo-distributed resources; HetPipe [19] combines pipelined model and data parallelism across hetero-geneous GPUs. Oobleck [5] focuses on fault-tolerant pipeline re-instantiation. Metis [30] automatically partitions work-loads across heterogeneous GPUs but does not target geo-distributed topology or energy-aware planning. 

Pipeline parallelism and memory. GPipe [4] popularizes micro-batch pipelining, while PipeDream [14] introduces the 1F1B schedule adopted by many later works. GraphPipe [6] introduces graph pipeline parallelism for general DNN com-putation graphs by jointly optimizing graph partitioning and micro-batch scheduling. AdaPipe [27] jointly optimizes recomputation and stage partitioning for compute-memory balance, rather than network and hardware heterogeneity. ZeRO [21] and Megatron-LM [24] target intra-cluster paral-lelism, complementary to our WAN-pipeline focus. 

Energy-aware foundation model training. Zeus [33] studies GPU power and energy consumption, motivating our energy-aware planning model. Strubell et al. [26] and Patterson et al. [20] quantify training’s energy and carbon footprint. However, these works do not incorporate energy into automatic parallelism planning. Across these lines of work, no existing planner combines the capabilities required for heterogeneous, geo-distributed training, as Table 1 summarizes. GeoTrain addresses these gaps by supporting directed network topologies with asym-metric links, jointly selecting GPU subsets and non-uniform layer partitions, and incorporating energy into plan search.

## 5 GeoTrain Design 

GeoTrain solves the optimization problem using a Memetic Algorithm (MA) [13]. The design comprises a chromosome encoding, domain-specific genetic operators, and a capacity-rebalancing local search. 

5.1 Chromosome Encoding 

We encode each candidate plan as an integer chromosome of length 𝐿 :

𝐶 = [𝑥 1, 𝑥 2, . . . , 𝑥 𝐿 ] (21) where 𝑥 ℓ ∈ { 1, . . . , 𝑁 } is the GPU assigned to layer ℓ.The three decision variables in Eq. 15 are implicitly en-coded. The number of stages 𝑄 corresponds to groups of con-secutive identical GPU IDs, the layer partition 𝑛 𝑞 to group lengths, and the GPU assignment 𝑔 𝑞 to the GPU ID of each group. Decoding requires a single linear scan. We fix the micro-batch size 𝑢 and thus the micro-batch count 𝑀 = 𝐵 /𝑢 ,excluding 𝑢 from optimization; jointly optimizing 𝑢 is left to future work. Figure 3 shows encoding for 𝐿 = 8 and 𝑄 = 3. A chromosome is valid when (i) layers assigned to the same GPU form a contiguous group and (ii) per-stage mem-ory bounds are satisfied (Eq. 19).

6.1 Experimental Setup 

We implement GeoTrain in Python using the 1F1B iteration-time and two-level energy models (Sect. 4.4). We validate it on real hardware (Sect. 6.3), while other experiments use simulated heterogeneous GPU topologies to explore config-urations beyond resource limits. 

Cluster environments. Real-hardware experiments run on a six-node cluster, each node equipped with a single GPU: an A100 (40GB), an A30 (24GB), three A40s (48GB each), and T4 (16GB). Nodes are provisioned with 4-60 vCPUs and 16-240GB of host RAM. They are geographically located across different clouds (Nectar 1 and Google Cloud Platform) and connected via a WireGuard VPN exhibiting heterogeneous communication characteristics with bandwidths ranging from 100 Mbps to 2 Gbps and latencies from sub-millisecond to 50 ms. By utilizing virtualization and shared tenancy on the cloud (e.g., NVIDIA’s Multi-Instance GPU technology), each GPU slices its full capacity to provide a certain effective FLOPS, which we have to profile before the planner runs. Bandwidth sensitivity. We fix cross-site latency at 10 ms and sweep bandwidth from 0 .5 to 100 Gbps (Fig. 8c). At low bandwidth, communication dominates and both planners consolidate onto a single site. As bandwidth increases, multi-site execution becomes viable and GeoTrain exploits it faster than impr. Metis, opening its largest advantage (12 .0% at 5 Gbps). Once bandwidth is abundant, communication is no longer a bottleneck, and the two converge. Overall, GeoTrain tracks network conditions more closely, shifting between single- and multi-site plans at finer granu-larity to capture gains missed by the baselines.

## 7 Conclusion and Future Work 

We presented GeoTrain, an automatic parallelism system for foundation model training on heterogeneous, geo-distributed GPUs. By jointly optimizing GPU selection, model partition-ing, and placement under compute, memory, power, and network constraints, GeoTrain efficiently explores the combi-natorial search space using an optimized memetic algorithm. 

11 Bui-Khanh Hoang, TianZhang He, Mohammad Goudarzi 

Across real and simulated environments, GeoTrain outper-forms state-of-the-art baselines, reducing iteration time and energy by up to 61 .4% and 11 .7%, respectively, while match-ing the verifiable optimum on small instances and scaling beyond exact methods. These results show that topology-and energy-aware planning can provide a practical and scal-able approach to distributed foundation model training. In future work, we will extend GeoTrain to support additional parallelism techniques within pipeline stages, adapt to dy-namic network and resource conditions, incorporate addi-tional practical objectives, and investigate larger-scale pro-duction deployments.
