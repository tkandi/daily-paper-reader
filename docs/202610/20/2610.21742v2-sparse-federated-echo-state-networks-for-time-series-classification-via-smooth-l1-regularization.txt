Abstract
Echo State Networks (ESNs), as lightweight implementations of reservoir computing,
provide efficient solutions for temporal data processing in resource-constrained environ-
ments. While standard ESNs offer computational efficiency, their dense readout layers inflate
the stored model and the per-sample inference cost. This paper introduces a Smooth-L1
regularized ESN (SL1ESN) that promotes sparsity in the readout layer while preserving
differentiability for gradient-based optimization. The method employs a twice-differentiable
Smooth-L1 approximation compatible with Newton’s method, which we exploit both for
centralized training and for a second-order (Newton-step) federated aggregation scheme.
Under a single fixed shrinkage threshold shared by every dataset and both settings, exper-
iments on seven time-series classification benchmarks show that SL1ESN learns readouts
that are 77–95% sparse in the centralized setting and 75–95% sparse in the federated one,
while staying within 0.7 accuracy points of the strongest dense baseline on five of the seven
benchmarks in the centralized setting and within 1.6 points on the same five in the federated
one, and the second-order federated update converges in only a few rounds. The resulting
sparsity compresses the stored model, the per-sample inference cost, and the server-to-client
broadcast by a comparable factor. These results demonstrate the practical value of sparse
readouts for edge computing and IoT deployments.
Keywords: Echo State Networks, Reservoir Computing, Sparse Models, Smooth-L1 Regulariza-
tion, Temporal Sequence Modeling, Federated Learning

Introduction
Machine learning has become a cornerstone for extracting patterns from complex datasets.
While a large share of applied machine learning operates on tabular, independently sampled
records, an important class of problems is instead inherently sequential, in the sense that the
measurements arrive as a time-ordered stream and the ordering itself carries the information.
Sensor and IoT telemetry, physiological recordings such as ECG, industrial process traces, and
financial series all fall into this class, and they are also precisely the settings addressed in this
paper, where the data are generated continuously and often on distributed, resource-constrained
devices. Recurrent Neural Networks (RNNs) have long been a natural choice for such temporal
processing because their hidden-state dynamics allow them to capture dependencies across
time. However, traditional RNNs are often difficult to train reliably on long sequences and
can suffer from vanishing or exploding gradients, which hampers the learning of long-range
dependencies (Pascanu et al., 2013; Zucchet and Orvieto, 2024; Tiezzi et al., 2026). Gated
architectures such as Long Short-Term Memory (LSTM) (Hochreiter and Schmidhuber, 1997)

and Gated Recurrent Units (GRU) (Chung et al., 2014) alleviate some of these optimization
challenges, and more recent designs continue this line. The xLSTM architecture (Beck et al.,
2024) augments the LSTM cell with exponential gating and matrix memory to recover much of
the scaling behavior of Transformers while retaining a recurrent, constant-memory inference path,
and deep state-space models pursue a similar goal from the linear-dynamics side (Tiezzi et al.,
2026). All of these nevertheless remain fully trained sequence models. They introduce a large
number of trainable parameters and require backpropagation through time, with the associated
computational and memory demands. These costs can become prohibitive in resource-constrained
or real-time deployment scenarios, where compact and efficient temporal models remain especially
desirable (Lu et al., 2025; Cordova-Cardenas et al., 2025). Echo State Networks (ESNs) offer a
fundamentally different response to the same difficulty. They are not a reaction to the recent
architectures above but a parallel development. Introduced by Jaeger (2001) at essentially the
same time as gated recurrent models were becoming established, they sidestep the training
difficulties of RNNs rather than engineering around them, and they remain a highly efficient class
of recurrent models within the broader paradigm of Reservoir Computing (RC) (Lukoševičius
and Jaeger, 2009). The core idea of RC is to separate the system into a fixed, randomly
initialized recurrent layer, known as the reservoir, and a trainable output layer, called the
readout. The reservoir acts as a high-dimensional dynamic memory that nonlinearly transforms
the input signal into a rich state space. In standard ESNs, only the readout-layer weights are
trained, often via ridge regression. By decoupling recurrent state generation from output-layer
optimization, this design avoids direct training of recurrent connections and thereby reduces
many of the optimization difficulties commonly associated with conventional RNNs. As a result,
ESN training is typically much faster and often more stable than that of fully trained recurrent
architectures (Liu et al., 2026; Matzner and Mráz, 2025; Yadav et al., 2024). Despite these
significant advantages, standard ESN implementations employ a dense readout layer, meaning
every reservoir neuron is connected to every output unit. While this ensures full utilization of the
reservoir’s representational power, it carries several drawbacks. First, dense readouts are prone
to overfitting, since every reservoir neuron, including redundant ones, contributes to the output.
Second, dense readouts increase the model’s memory footprint and computational cost during
inference. These limitations are particularly relevant in resource-constrained environments such
as edge devices and Internet of Things (IoT) sensors, and in distributed or federated computing
scenarios where communication bandwidth is often a bottleneck (Dritsas and Trigka, 2025;
Akhtarshenas et al., 2024; Polato et al., 2026).
To address these issues, sparsity-promoting methods in neural networks have been extensively
studied (Haugstvedt et al., 2023; Liu, 2020; Villmann et al., 2010). In particular, L1 regulariza-
tion (Tibshirani, 1996) is well suited to promoting sparse solutions by driving irrelevant weights
exactly to zero. Applying L1 regularization to the readout layer of an ESN yields a sparse model
that is more memory-efficient and better suited for efficient inference. Sparsity is often also
motivated by interpretability; because the reservoir features are strongly correlated, however,
many different supports realize an equally sparse and accurate readout, so throughout this paper
we claim compression rather than feature selection (Section 6.4). However, the L1 penalty is
non-differentiable at zero, which complicates gradient-based optimization. While specialized
algorithms such as Least Angle Regression (LARS) (Efron et al., 2004) and Coordinate Descent
(CD) (Wright, 2015) solve the LASSO reliably in a centralized setting, they fit poorly into dis-
tributed optimization frameworks such as FedAvg (McMahan et al., 2017), a Federated Learning
(FL) approach whose aggregation step is an average of locally computed, smooth parameter or
gradient updates. The mismatch is structural rather than a matter of implementation. First,
both algorithms are path- or coordinate-sequential, since LARS builds the solution by adding one
variable to the active set at a time and CD updates one coordinate while holding the others fixed,
so neither exposes a single descent direction over all coordinates that a client could compute
locally and a server could average. Second, their state is a discrete active set rather than a
2


vector in a linear space; clients trained on different local data return different supports, and
the average of models with disjoint supports is neither sparse nor the solution of any of the
local problems, so the aggregation step loses the very property the penalty was introduced to
obtain. Third, and specific to our setting, neither method produces the curvature information
that a second-order server update requires. Because the L1 term is non-differentiable at zero, no
Hessian of the full objective exists that clients could compute and sum.
To overcome this limitation, we propose a novel ESN variant employing Smooth-L1 reg-
ularization1. Smooth-L1 is a twice-differentiable approximation of the L1 norm that retains
sparsity-inducing properties while remaining compatible with smooth, gradient-based opti-
mization (Schmidt, 2007). This differentiability is crucial for efficient training via Newton’s
method (Boyd and Vandenberghe, 2004) and for seamless integration into FL pipelines.
The main contributions of this paper are:
• SL1ESN Architecture: A novel ESN readout trained with a twice-differentiable Smooth-
L1 penalty via damped Newton steps under a continuation schedule, which yields a
sparse readout while keeping the training objective smooth and amenable to second-order
optimization.
• Federated Second-Order Extension: A distributed SL1ESN formulation, FedSL1ESN,
in which clients return local gradients and Gauss-Newton Hessians and the server performs
a damped Newton step with line search and shrinkage. The smoothness of the penalty
is what makes this second-order federated update possible; it converges within a few
communication rounds and yields a sparse global readout that reduces model storage,
inference cost, and the server-to-client broadcast.
• Comprehensive Evaluation: Extensive experiments on seven time-series classification
tasks from the UCR and UCI archives, complemented by ablation, sensitivity, and client-
count studies. The results demonstrate a favorable sparsity–accuracy trade-off, with
readout sparsity of 77–95% centrally and 75–95% in the federated setting at accuracy
close to the dense baselines, obtained under a single fixed shrinkage threshold rather than
per-dataset tuning.
The remainder of the paper is organized as follows. Section 2 reviews related work. Section 3
introduces the ESN background and the proposed Smooth-L1 regularization. Section 4 presents
the centralized and distributed training algorithms. Section 5 describes the experimental setup
and results. Section 6 discusses the findings, and Section 7 concludes the paper.

Related Work
2.1
Reservoir Computing and Echo State Networks
Echo State Networks are a foundational model within Reservoir Computing (RC). The ESN
architecture consists of three components: an input layer, a fixed randomly initialized recurrent
reservoir, and a trainable readout layer. The input is projected into the reservoir space via
an input weight matrix Win, and the reservoir’s internal dynamics evolve according to a fixed
weight matrix Wres, ensuring the Echo State Property for stable dynamics (Yildiz et al., 2012).
Only the readout weights Wout are trained, typically via ridge regression.
The simplicity and training efficiency of ESNs have led to their widespread adoption in
time-series prediction (Jaeger and Haas, 2004), speech recognition (Verstraeten et al., 2007), and
robotic control (Baldini, 2022). Research has focused on optimizing reservoir construction to
improve performance and reduce size (Rodan and Tiño, 2012; D’Inverno and Dong, 2025; Dale

et al., 2021). Rodan and Tiño (2011) proposed the minimum-complexity ESN (MC-ESN) using
simple cycles and delays, significantly reducing connection count while maintaining performance.
The concept of cycle reservoirs was further explored by Li et al. (2024a), and deep reservoir

to capture hierarchical temporal features.
Beyond classical ESNs, the RC paradigm encompasses Liquid State Machines (Maass et al.,
2002) and Random Neural Networks (Gelenbe, 1989), which share the core RC principle of a
fixed untrained recurrent core with a linear readout. Our work, focused on the classic ESN, can
be viewed as a step toward bringing sparse efficient learning to the broader RC family.
2.2
Sparsity in Neural Networks
Sparsity has long been recognized as a desirable property in machine learning for improving
generalization, interpretability, and efficiency (Hastie et al., 2015). L2 regularization penalizes
the squared magnitude of weights, producing small but in general non-zero values and thereby
smoothing the model. In contrast, L1 regularization penalizes the absolute value of weights,
driving many to exactly zero (Tibshirani, 1996). This makes L1 particularly attractive for feature
selection and model compression.
In neural networks, sparsity has been explored through a variety of approaches, including
L1-based regularization, pruning techniques, and structured sparsity schemes such as Group
LASSO (Tibshirani, 1996; Han et al., 2015; Yuan and Lin, 2006). A recent overview is provided
by Haugstvedt et al. (2023). While effective in centralized settings, many of these approaches
either require multiple training phases or are not naturally suited to distributed optimization
frameworks.
Within the ESN literature, sparse reservoirs have been shown to reduce computational load
without significantly compromising performance (Rodan and Tiño, 2011), and sparse input
connections have been explored to reduce input dimensionality (Gallicchio, 2020). The most
direct route to a compact model, however, is to induce sparsity in the readout layer.
Qiao and Wang (2019) proposed an Adaptive LASSO ESN that uses the non-differentiable
LARS solver to obtain sparse readout weights. While this successfully achieves sparsity, the
reliance on a non-gradient-based solver is a critical limitation when extending ESNs to Federated
Learning (FL), where the standard algorithm FedAvg (McMahan et al., 2017) requires clients to
compute gradients of a smooth loss function. The two decades since the LASSO was introduced
have produced a large body of algorithms for exactly this difficulty, and a comprehensive account
of the numerical options is given by the survey of Zhao and Huo (2023). Broadly, they fall
into three families. Proximal and splitting methods keep the penalty outside the smooth part
4


and handle it through its proximal operator, from iterative shrinkage-thresholding and its
accelerated variant (Daubechies et al., 2004; Beck and Teboulle, 2009) to the general proximal
framework of Parikh and Boyd (2014) and to ADMM (Boyd et al., 2011), which is the basis
of many distributed LASSO solvers. Subgradient- and stochastic-optimizer approaches instead
adapt the training loop itself, as in truncated-gradient online learning (Langford et al., 2009) or
composite-objective adaptive methods such as AdaGrad with an L1 term (Duchi et al., 2011).
Finally, smoothing methods replace the penalty by a differentiable surrogate, which makes the
whole objective amenable to ordinary gradient-based and second-order machinery. Schmidt
(2007) introduced such a twice-differentiable approximation of the L1 norm.
We adopt the third route. This is not a claim that it dominates the others in accuracy
or convergence rate; ADMM and accelerated proximal methods are at least as strong for the
centralized LASSO, and ADMM in particular has well-developed distributed forms. The reason
is the specific requirement of our federated update. A smooth objective is the only one of the
three families for which each client can form both a gradient and a Gauss-Newton Hessian of
the complete objective, penalty included, in a shared coordinate system, so that the server can
sum them and take a damped Newton step (Section 4). Proximal and thresholding schemes
place the penalty in an operator that has no additive curvature, and subgradient schemes give
first-order information only. The Schmidt surrogate is moreover simple to implement, numerically
robust, and has closed-form first and second derivatives, which is what makes the second-order
aggregation practical.
2.3
Sparsity in Signal Analysis and Inverse Problems
A stronger line of results, compressed sensing, establishes exact recovery of sparse coefficients
from few measurements under restricted isometry or incoherence conditions (Candès et al.,
2006; Donoho, 2006), together with a family of L0-oriented greedy and hard-thresholding solvers
(matching pursuit (Mallat and Zhang, 1993), orthogonal matching pursuit (Tropp and Gilbert,
2007), iterative hard thresholding (Blumensath and Davies, 2009)), the exact L0 problem itself
being NP-hard (Natarajan, 1995). We do not adopt these methods, for three reasons. First,
their guarantees rest on incoherence of the design matrix, which reservoir states violate by
construction, because all neurons are driven by the same input through contractive dynamics,
so the columns of X are strongly cross-correlated and the L0–L1 equivalence results do not
transfer. The same coherence also makes the selected L1 support unstable, in the sense that
small perturbations of the data can change which neurons are retained.
Second, that instability is harmless in our setting, because we are not trying to identify a true
support. Compressed sensing recovers a signal known to be sparse from scarce measurements;
here measurements are plentiful, no ground-truth sparse readout exists, and the goal is model
compression, so any sparse and predictive readout is acceptable regardless of which of the
correlated neurons carry the weights. Third, and decisively for the federated setting, L0 and
greedy methods return discrete support sets that differ across clients and expose neither gradients
nor Hessians that add across clients, ruling out the second-order aggregation used here. The
smooth penalty instead keeps the optimization in a continuous space where local gradients and
Hessians are summable, enabling a Newton-type server update, and defers the discrete decision
to a single shrinkage step after aggregation. The smoothing parameter governs a trade-off, in
that smaller values approximate the L1 penalty more closely but inflate the curvature near zero,
worsening the conditioning of the Hessian.
2.4
Federated Reservoir Computing
Federated Learning (FL) is a distributed machine learning paradigm that enables collaborative
model training across decentralized data sources while preserving data privacy (McMahan et al.,
2017). Each client performs local training using its private data and shares only model updates
5


with a coordinating server, which aggregates them into an improved global model. This iterative
federated optimization cycle keeps raw data on the client and leverages heterogeneous distributed
datasets, making FL attractive for healthcare, autonomous driving, smart manufacturing, and
collaborative robotics (Guo et al., 2021; Kasyap and Tripathy, 2021; Joshi et al., 2022; Eid
Kishawy et al., 2024; Kuo and Yang, 2024; Gutierrez et al., 2025).
FL compliance with regulatory frameworks such as GDPR (European Parliament and
Council of the European Union, 2016) comes at the cost of new challenges. Client datasets are
often heterogeneous and non-identically distributed, potentially causing client drift (Shi et al.,
2022). Communication efficiency is a critical design constraint, addressed through specialized
aggregation mechanisms, privacy-preserving techniques (e.g., differential privacy, homomorphic
encryption, secure aggregation (Rodríguez-Barroso et al., 2020; Fang and Qian, 2021)), and
numerous FL extensions (Liu et al., 2024; Wen et al., 2023; Zheng et al., 2023). For a broader
overview of federated learning, we refer the interested reader to Polato et al. (2026).
Recent work has explored distributed ESN implementations. In a foundational contribution,
Bacciu et al. (2021) introduced Federated Reservoir Computing, where clients share only sufficient
statistics with the server, achieving a global readout mathematically equivalent to the centralized
model. De Caro et al. (2022) extended this with Federated Intrinsic Plasticity (FedIP), enabling
unsupervised reservoir adaptation via minimal gain and bias parameter exchange. Nogami
et al. (2025) proposed an incremental federated variant for time-series anomaly detection using
Mahalanobis Distance of Reservoir States (Tamura et al., 2023). These approaches retain
classical regularization methods but lack explicit mechanisms for communication efficiency
through sparsity. Our work addresses this gap by applying smooth sparsification to the ESN
readout layer.

3.1
Echo State Network Fundamentals
Echo State Networks (ESNs) are a subclass of RNNs that efficiently process temporal data by
separating dynamic memory (reservoir) from the trainable output layer (Jaeger, 2001; Rodan and
Tiño, 2011). An ESN comprises a fixed randomly generated reservoir and a trainable readout.
The input weight matrix Win ∈RNR×NI projects input u(t) ∈RNI into the high-dimensional
reservoir space, and the recurrent weight matrix Wres ∈RNR×NR evolves the reservoir state.

In practice, a fixed large α can cause overly aggressive quadratic approximations near zero, as
the Smooth-L1 approximation is not appropriately modeled by a quadratic for variables near 0
when α is large. To address this, a continuation strategy (Schmidt, 2007) is employed. We begin
with a small α (α0 = 1) where the quadratic approximation is appropriate, and take Newton
steps or gradient updates between successive increases of the parameter α (multiplying α by a
factor κ > 1 after each Newton iteration; κ = 2 in all experiments), terminating at a sufficiently
large α (αmax = 5×106) that yields a near-exact L1 penalty. This schedule balances optimization
stability with sparsity induction. Both objectives above are implemented in our framework and
trained by the same solver. The experimental part of this paper, however, considers classification
only, and the regression variant is not discussed in detail; for completeness, its evaluation on
two chaotic benchmarks is reported in Appendix C.

4
Proposed Method
This section presents the proposed SL1ESN training framework for learning sparse readout
weights in Echo State Networks. Our approach replaces the standard closed-form readout
estimation with an iterative optimization procedure that incorporates a twice-differentiable
sparsity-promoting regularizer. This formulation preserves compatibility with gradient-based
optimization and therefore supports both centralized training and its extension to federated
learning. We first describe the method in the centralized setting and then show how it can be
adapted to the distributed case.
Two components act together to produce the sparse readout. The Smooth-L1 penalty,
sharpened over the continuation schedule in α, drives redundant readout coordinates toward
zero inside the optimization, so that the surviving coordinates are re-optimized at every Newton
step to absorb the contribution of those that vanish. A shrinkage step with threshold τ then
turns the near-zero coordinates into exact zeros, since the smooth surrogate is differentiable
everywhere and therefore never attains exact zeros by itself. Model selection is kept separate
from this mechanism. The reservoir hyperparameters and the regularization strength λ are
chosen to maximize validation accuracy, with no sparsity term in the selection objective and
with the shrinkage step effectively disabled during the search (negligible threshold τ = 10−5).
The fixed threshold τ = 0.1 is applied only when the selected configuration is evaluated, so the
sparsity we report in Section 5 is produced by a fixed threshold acting on an accuracy-selected
configuration rather than by a sparsity-targeted search. The threshold τ is not tuned either,
since all reported classification results of SL1ESN and FedSL1ESN, centralized and federated,
use the same fixed value τ = 0.1, so no per-dataset choice enters the reported sparsity.
4.1
Centralized Setting
In the centralized setting, all training data are available on a single server, and the objective is
to learn the readout matrix Wout by minimizing the regularized loss introduced in Section 3.
In contrast to traditional ESN training, where Wout is obtained in closed form by ridge
regression, our approach formulates readout learning as an iterative optimization problem. This
is particularly advantageous because it accommodates non-quadratic regularizers, supports
incremental or online updates, and provides a natural basis for extensions to distributed
optimization settings such as federated learning.
The centralized training procedure consists of two main stages. First, the input sequence is
forward-propagated through the fixed reservoir in order to collect the corresponding reservoir
states x(t). These states define the features used by the readout layer. Second, the readout weights
are optimized iteratively with respect to the regularized objective. In our case, this optimization
is carried out with Newton’s method, exploiting the smoothness of the proposed penalty. At
each iteration, the gradient ∇L and Hessian H = ∇2L are computed, the damped linear system
(H + µI)∆w = −∇L is solved to obtain the search direction, and an Armijo backtracking line
search (Nocedal and Wright, 2006, Ch. 3) is used to determine the step size. The updated weights
are then sparsified with the soft-threshold operator [Sτ(W)]ij = sign(wij) max(|wij| −τ, 0), and
the smoothing parameter is advanced along the continuation schedule, α ←min(ακ, αmax). The
factor κ, threshold τ, and regularization strength λ are treated as predefined hyperparameters;
further implementation details are provided in Appendix B.
Algorithmically, this procedure is related to, but distinct from, the classical Iterative
Shrinkage-Thresholding Algorithm (ISTA) and its accelerated variant FISTA (Daubechies
et al., 2004; Beck and Teboulle, 2009). It retains the shrinkage step Sτ, but replaces the
first-order gradient step by a damped Newton step, available only because the penalty is smooth,
and adds the continuation in α. Unlike ISTA, where the L1 penalty is handled solely by the
proximal operator, here it also enters the objective as the smooth surrogate of strength λ, so
the readout is already being pulled toward a sparse solution while it is being fitted, and the

4.2
Distributed Setting
The distributed training pipeline is illustrated in Figure 1. We consider a parameter-server
architecture with one central server and K clients, each holding private time-series data. The
goal is to learn a global readout matrix Wout without sharing raw data, while exploiting
the twice-differentiable structure of the Smooth-L1 penalty to perform second-order (Newton)
updates on the server and to obtain a sparse global readout for efficient storage and inference.
Because the smooth penalty exposes a well-defined gradient and Hessian, we implement the

Experiments
This section evaluates the proposed SL1ESN on time-series classification. The experiments are
designed to assess predictive performance, the degree of sparsity induced in the readout layer,
and the behavior of the method in centralized and distributed training scenarios.
5.1
Datasets
We use seven datasets from the UCI and UCR archives (Table 1), spanning univariate and
multivariate sequences in areas such as activity recognition (Anguita et al., 2013), character
trajectories (Williams, 2006), speech analysis (Kudo et al., 1999), and other fields (Dau et al.,
2019). The datasets cover binary and multi-class problems across various training sizes and
sequence lengths.
5.2
Centralized setup
All ESN configurations use a fixed reservoir of size NR = 500 for comparability across regulariza-
tion methods. Input matrix entries are drawn from a Uniform distribution U(−ain, ain); reservoir
adjacency matrix entries from U(−1, 1). The hyperbolic tangent activation confines reservoir
outputs to [−1, 1]. Hyperparameters are optimized via random search over the distributions in
Table 2. The search objective is predictive accuracy only, namely the validation accuracy, with no
sparsity term. During the search the shrinkage threshold is held at the negligible value 10−5, so
the candidate readouts are effectively dense; the fixed threshold τ = 0.1 is applied only when the
selected SL1ESN configuration is evaluated. The readout sparsity we report below is therefore
produced by this fixed threshold acting on an accuracy-selected configuration, not a quantity
the search was asked to maximize. Full per-dataset selected values are reported in Appendix B.
We compare three readout penalties, all trained with the same damped Gauss–Newton solver so
that only the penalty differs and no solver confound enters the comparison: an unregularized
cross-entropy readout (none), an L2-regularized readout (L2-ESN), and the proposed SL1ESN.
The two dense baselines are run with the same negligible threshold 10−5 in both search and
evaluation, which leaves them dense. Reported accuracies are means ± standard deviation
over ten independent random seeds. The softmax parameterization is shift invariant, so the
unregularized Hessian is singular along the all-ones direction; rather than fixing a reference class
we retain the full NO × NR readout and rely on the adaptive damping µI of the Newton solve
11


(Appendix B) to render the system non-singular, which keeps the parameterization identical
across the three penalties. Readout sparsity is accordingly always reported as the fraction of
zeros among all NONR entries, none of which is constrained to zero a priori.

5.3
Distributed setup
All federated experiments use the following fixed configuration: K = 5 clients and 50 communi-
cation rounds, with results averaged over ten seeds (a sensitivity study over the client count K
is reported in Appendix D). Each round follows the federated Newton scheme of Algorithm 2:
clients return the local gradient and per-class Gauss-Newton Hessian of their Smooth-L1 loss,
and the server applies a single damped Newton step with Armijo backtracking line search
(c1 = 10−4, shrinkage 0.5) followed by a soft-threshold Sτ. The smoothing parameter follows the
continuation schedule with α0 = 1 and factor κ = 2. Data are partitioned uniformly (IID) across
clients; heterogeneous (non-IID) partitions are not implemented in this study, and Section 6.4
discusses this limitation together with the existing line of Newton-type federated methods for
heterogeneous data that a future version can build on. The global reservoir parameters (Win,
Wres) are initialized once on the server and broadcast to all clients at the start; they remain fixed
throughout all rounds. As in the centralized setting, hyperparameters are selected for accuracy
alone. The proposed FedSL1ESN is compared against L2 and unregularized ESN baselines,
all trained with the same server-side Newton update so that the readout penalty is the only
variable that differs, which makes the unregularized column a federated second-order baseline
in its own right. No first-order FedAvg comparison is included, for a structural reason: clients
perform no local weight update but return (gk, Hk) evaluated at the current global readout,
and under full participation the data-fidelity part of their sums is exactly the gradient and
Gauss-Newton Hessian of the pooled cross-entropy loss, independently of how the samples are
partitioned. The federated iteration is therefore a centralized Newton iteration on an objective
that differs from the centralized one only in the penalty, which every client carries a copy of, so
that the aggregate is Kλ rather than λ. Two further differences keep Table 4 from reproducing
Table 3 numerically: the federated runs use a fixed budget of 50 rounds where the centralized
solver iterates to the support-stagnation criterion of Section 4, and λ is re-selected for the
federated setting (Appendix B). Against that background FedAvg, which optimizes the same
convex objective by first-order steps, could only approach the same optimum more slowly and
without producing a sparse readout, which is why it is not included as a separate column. The
server broadcasts the global readout in compressed sparse row (CSR) form; the resulting sparsity
benefits for model storage and inference are discussed in Section 6.2. Although the readout
typically reaches near-peak accuracy within a few rounds (Figure 3), we report at 50 rounds so
that the continuation schedule has fully sharpened the penalty and the readout sparsity has
stabilized.

5.4.3
Ablation and Sensitivity Analysis
To isolate the role of each hyperparameter we perform a one-at-a-time ablation on the UCI HAR
dataset, varying a single parameter over a grid while holding the others at their selected values
and averaging validation accuracy and readout sparsity over random seeds. Figure 5 reports the
four parameters that matter for the accuracy–sparsity trade-off. Note that the accuracies here
are measured on the validation split used for model selection and therefore sit well above the
held-out test accuracies of Table 3; only the relative trends are of interest.
Sweeping the shrinkage threshold τ drives sparsity smoothly from 0 to 100% while validation
accuracy stays essentially flat (≈97%) up to ∼80% sparsity, collapsing only once the readout is
driven to near-total sparsity. The regularization strength λ also induces sparsity in classification,
but on markedly worse terms. At matched ∼65% sparsity, λ = 1 costs about 1.5 accuracy
points where the threshold costs none, and beyond λ ≈10 accuracy collapses while sparsity
plateaus near 83%. The two mechanisms are therefore not interchangeable, and this is why the
search is free to optimize λ for accuracy alone. At the selected values it stays small, and the
sparsity in Table 3 is produced by the penalty and shrinkage acting together during training
rather than by a large λ.
The remaining two parameters do not affect sparsity. Accuracy rises with the reservoir
size NR and saturates beyond NR ≈500 (from ≈88% at NR = 50 to ≈98% at NR = 800),
while readout sparsity stays near zero throughout, confirming that NR governs representational
capacity and motivating the fixed choice NR = 500 as a cost-accuracy saturation point. Accuracy
is likewise essentially invariant to the continuation factor κ, with negligible residual sparsity
for κ ≥2 (a small effect appears only for very slow schedules such as κ = 1.1); the initial
smoothing α0 is equally inconsequential (not shown). The continuation schedule therefore needs
no per-dataset tuning.

Discussion
6.1
Sparsity–Accuracy Trade-off
The central finding is that on time-series classification the ESN readout is largely redundant,
and that SL1ESN is able to find and discard that redundancy without giving up accuracy.
Under a single threshold shared by every dataset and both settings, its readouts are 77–95%
sparse centrally and 75–95% sparse in the federated setting, and on the same five of the seven
classification benchmarks they stay within 0.7 points of the strongest dense baseline centrally
and within 1.6 points in federation, margins that in most cases are smaller than the seed-to-seed
standard deviation, so that the sparse readout is on par with the best dense one there; on UCI
HAR the centralized sparse readout is the most accurate model outright, and in federation that
holds on Distal. The accuracy–sparsity curves of Figures 2 and 4 show why this is possible,
they are flat over a wide range before they break, so there is a broad band of sparsity levels at
which the readout simply loses nothing. This is the payoff of the smooth penalty. Because the
surrogate is differentiable, the redundant coordinates are driven toward zero while the readout
is still being fitted, and the coordinates that survive are re-optimized at every Newton step to

6.2
Communication Efficiency
The sparsity of the FedSL1ESN global readout yields efficiency gains along three axes, which we
state precisely to avoid over-claiming. First, model storage and inference. The converged global
readout has only 5–25% of its entries non-zero, so the stored model and the per-sample inference
cost (a sparse matrix–vector product on each edge device) shrink by the corresponding factor.
This is the benefit most relevant to the resource-constrained IoT setting that motivates this
work. Second, the server-to-client broadcast, which carries the global readout and is transmitted
in CSR form and therefore inherits the same compression. Third, the number of communication
rounds. The second-order server update reaches near-peak accuracy within one to two rounds
17


on several datasets (Figure 3), reducing the total round budget relative to first-order schemes
that need many more rounds to converge.
It is important to be clear about which part of the communication cost sparsity actually
reduces, and which part it leaves untouched. The savings apply to the server-to-client broadcast
and to the stored model, both of which carry the readout. They do not apply to the dominant
per-round cost, which is the client-to-server upload. In the federated Newton method this message
carries the local gradient and the per-class Gauss-Newton Hessian rather than the readout itself,
and its size is therefore independent of readout sparsity. Concretely, a client uploads NONR
gradient entries and NO symmetric NR × NR blocks per round, i.e. NONR(NR + 3)/2 floats
against NONR for a FedAvg weight upload, a ratio of (NR + 3)/2 ≈250 at NR = 500 that
is independent of the dataset (0.96 MB per client and round on the binary benchmarks and
9.6 MB on Character, in single precision, against 3.9 and 39.1 KB). A first-order scheme would
thus have to require some 250 times as many rounds before the cumulative uplink favoured
the second-order update, which the convex readout problem studied here does not demand; we
therefore claim efficiency for the stored model, the inference path and the broadcast, where the
sparsity of Table 4 gives a 2–10× reduction, and explicitly not for the upload. The reduction
factor is quoted for the CSR payload rather than for the count of surviving weights: at 75–95%
sparsity only 5–25% of the entries remain, which would be a 4–20× saving on the values alone,
but CSR stores a column index alongside every value, so at equal word width the realized factor
is roughly halved. The design therefore trades a larger per-round upload for substantially fewer
rounds and a sparse final model. Reducing the curvature payload, for example via low-rank or
sketched Hessian approximations or by re-using the fixed-reservoir structure of Hk, is a concrete
and promising direction, and connects our approach to communication-efficient second-order
federated methods such as FedNS (Li et al., 2024b).
6.3
Second-Order Optimization Compatibility
A key advantage of the twice-differentiable Smooth-L1 penalty is that the second-order opti-
mization it enables is exploited not only in the centralized setting but also within the federated
protocol, directly addressing the concern that the differentiability advantage might go unused
in distributed training. Concretely, each client evaluates the local gradient and the per-class
Gauss-Newton Hessian of its Smooth-L1 loss at the current global readout; the server sums these
quantities across clients, solves a damped Newton equation per output class to obtain the descent
direction, and selects the step size by Armijo backtracking line search before broadcasting the
updated readout. This federated Newton scheme is made possible precisely by the smoothness
of the penalty (a non-smooth L1 objective exposes no usable Hessian), and it inherits the
fast, low-variance convergence observed centrally, reaching near-peak accuracy within one to
two communication rounds on several datasets. The principal cost of transmitting curvature
information, and its interaction with the sparse-readout communication savings, is discussed
in Section 6.2 and is closely related to second-order federated methods such as FedNS (Li
et al., 2024b). Sharing curvature also changes what a client exposes. The transmitted block

6.4
Limitations
Four limitations warrant future investigation. (1) Support identifiability: we report the level of
sparsity the readout reaches, not the identity of the surviving reservoir coordinates. Because
the reservoir features are strongly correlated by construction (Section 2), many supports realize
an equally sparse and accurate readout, so we claim compression rather than feature selection;
whether a stable, interpretable support can be extracted is left to future work. (2) Non-IID
data: heterogeneous client distributions are not implemented in this work, since all federated
experiments use a uniform (IID) partition, and we do not claim that the reported behavior
transfers to the non-IID case. Two risks are specific to our scheme: under client drift (Shi et al.,
2022) the local Gauss-Newton Hessians describe curvature around different local optima, so
their plain sum can bias the global Newton direction, and a single global shrinkage threshold
could prune directions that only minority clients rely on. Newton-type federated optimization
under statistical heterogeneity is an active line of work (Li et al., 2019; Safaryan et al., 2022;
Elgabli et al., 2022), and because our clients already expose exactly the local gradient and
Gauss-Newton Hessian these methods operate on, they are natural drop-in replacements for the
aggregation step of Algorithm 2; how the α-continuation and the shared threshold interact with
client-heterogeneous sparsity patterns is the question specific to FedSL1ESN. (3) Experimental
scale: the main-text federated setup uses five clients and 50 rounds; a client-count sweep up to
K = 50 (Appendix D) shows that accuracy and readout sparsity are stable under IID partitioning,
but larger client populations and system-level effects (stragglers, partial participation) remain
untested. (4) Privacy: the curvature upload exposes more than a first-order message, and
although secure aggregation applies to it unchanged, neither that nor a differentially private
variant is implemented here.
7
Conclusion
This paper introduced SL1ESN, an Echo State Network architecture that applies Smooth-L1
regularization to the readout layer. By leveraging a twice-differentiable approximation of the
L1 norm, the method keeps the readout objective amenable to second-order optimization while
still driving redundant readout weights to zero. On classification, and under a single shrinkage
threshold shared by every dataset and both settings, it learned readouts that are 77–95%
sparse centrally and 75–95% sparse in the federated setting, at an accuracy close to that of
the dense unregularized and L2 baselines. In the federated setting, a second-order (Newton)
server update made possible by the smooth penalty converges in only a few communication
rounds, and the resulting sparse readout reduces model storage, inference cost, and the server-
to-client broadcast, addressing key bottlenecks for on-device deployment. The claims of this
paper are confined to classification; a supplementary evaluation on two chaotic regression
benchmarks (Appendix C) indicates that the same readout tolerates far less pruning there before
the autonomous rollout degrades, which delimits where the method is expected to pay off. These
findings establish a foundation for sparse and efficient reservoir computing in resource-constrained
environments. Future work will address adaptive regularization strategies, robustness to non-
IID data distributions, for which Newton-type federated methods designed for heterogeneous
data offer a concrete starting point (Section 6.4), and extensions to other reservoir computing
architectures.

Search protocol.
Model selection uses a stratified 80/20 split of each training set into a fitting
and a validation part, with every class present in both; the test set of Table 1 is untouched until
the final evaluation. Each candidate configuration is an independent uniform draw from the
distributions of Table 2, scored by validation accuracy alone, and the same budget is given to all
three penalties on a given dataset: 5,000 draws on UCI HAR, Character and JapaneseVowels and
25,000 on the four binary UCR datasets. In the federated setting the reservoir hyperparameters (ρ,
ain, γ) are inherited from the centralized selection and only λ is re-drawn, for 100 configurations
per dataset and penalty. Each candidate is trained for up to 100 rounds with early stopping
(patience 10) and scored by the accuracy of the aggregated global readout on a validation set
pooled from 20% of each client’s local training shard, with the threshold at the negligible value
10−5. The selected federated values are λ = 1.1 × 10−3 (UCI HAR), 1.1 × 10−3 (Character),
9.7 × 10−3 (JapaneseVowels), 3.4 × 10−4 (ECG5000), 3.3 × 10−3 (Distal.), 1.2 × 10−4 (Yoga) and
7.4× 10−4 (Strawberry). The selected configurations are then evaluated at the fixed budget of 50
rounds over seeds 92–101 (Table 4). In the first version of this paper, the federated Strawberry
value of λ had been carried over from an earlier search that scored candidates on test accuracy;
it has been replaced by the validation-selected value above, and the federated Strawberry results
have been recomputed accordingly.
Solver settings.
The damping µ of the Newton system (H + µI)∆w = −g is not a fixed
constant but is set adaptively per solve, in the manner of Levenberg–Marquardt: starting from
µ = 1 it is multiplied by 10 until the system is solvable and the resulting step is bounded
(∥∆w∥∞< 102), which is also what keeps the singular directions of the shift-invariant softmax
parameterization (Section 5) from producing an unusable step. The Armijo line search uses a
sufficient-decrease constant c1 = 10−4 and a backtracking factor of 0.5, capped at 20 backtracks.
The centralized iteration budget of Algorithm 1 is 200 Newton iterations, which the support-
stagnation criterion (P = 8, εs = 0.2 percentage points) reaches well before exhaustion; the
federated budget is the 50 communication rounds of Section 5.3.

Regression on Chaotic Benchmarks
The Smooth-L1 readout of Section 3 is not specific to classification, and the regression objective
is trained by the same damped Newton solver with continuation and shrinkage. For completeness,
this appendix evaluates the centralized SL1ESN on the Mackey-Glass (Mackey and Glass, 1977)
and Lorenz63 (Lorenz, 1963) chaotic systems, which are standard benchmarks for evaluating
ESN performance on chaotic dynamics.

The setup follows the centralized protocol of Section 5. Hyperparameters are optimized
via random search over the distributions in Table 2, with the valid prediction time (VPT), the
number of steps for which the autonomous rollout stays within a normalized-error threshold
of the true trajectory, as the accuracy objective; the selected values are listed in Table 6. In
addition to the unregularized and L2 (ridge regression) readouts, we include an L1 (LASSO,
coordinate descent (Wright, 2015)) baseline, since a direct L1 solver is available for the quadratic

system are too dispersed across seeds for a mean to be informative. The shrinkage threshold

Table 7 reports the results, and SL1ESN is competitive on both benchmarks. On Mackey-
Glass it attains RMSE 0.118 with the second-longest valid-prediction time (VPT 377 steps, behind
only the unregularized readout), and on Lorenz it reaches the best RMSE of the regularized
readouts (10.14), better than the L1 readout on both metrics, though its valid-prediction time
(VPT 148) trails the L2 and unregularized readouts. Figures 6 and 7 show the corresponding
rollouts. On Mackey-Glass the Smooth-L1 rollout tracks the true trajectory longest among the
regularized readouts, losing phase alignment only well after the L2 and L1 rollouts. On Lorenz,
the unregularized, L2, and Smooth-L1 rollouts all reconstruct the attractor and track its two
lobes of the butterfly, whereas the L1 readout leaves it earliest, collapsing away from the far
lobe.
The readout sparsity, however, is where the two regression tasks part company with the
classification results of the main text. On Mackey-Glass the readout is 46% sparse at essentially
no accuracy cost, but on Lorenz the accuracy-selected readout stays effectively dense (0.2%).
Sparsifying it further is not free. One-step RMSE degrades gracefully, but the VPT, the horizon
over which the rollout still tracks the true chaotic trajectory, is far more sensitive. Mackey-Glass
can be pushed to 65% sparsity at VPT 107 and Lorenz to 36% at VPT 69, but beyond roughly
70% sparsity long-horizon predictability collapses altogether, since the Mackey-Glass VPT falls
below 10 steps and the Lorenz rollout diverges almost immediately (VPT ≤5). The likely
reason is that a chaotic attractor distributes predictive signal across many weakly-weighted,
cross-dimensional reservoir couplings, so pruning by magnitude discards coordinates that the
autonomous rollout still depends on, even when they contribute little to the one-step error. By
contrast, the L1 (LASSO) readout does reach 91–93% sparsity, but at a VPT well below SL1ESN
on both systems, so it buys that sparsity with exactly the long-horizon fidelity at issue. We also
note that, unlike in classification, where the penalty strength λ saturates early as a sparsity
control (Section 5.4.3), on these regression readouts λ is itself an effective sparsity knob, the
accuracy-selected Mackey-Glass readout already being 46% sparse.

Figure 8: Federated test accuracy (left axis, solid) and readout sparsity (right axis, dashed)
as the number of clients K is swept from 2 to 50 (50 rounds, IID partition, shared threshold
τ = 0.1, mean ±1 std over ten seeds). The dotted vertical line marks the K = 5 setting used in
the main-text experiments. Accuracy is essentially flat in K on every dataset, and sparsity stays
in the band observed at K = 5.
The main-text federated experiments fix the number of clients at K = 5 (Section 5.3). To
assess how the federated Newton scheme behaves as the data become more fragmented, we repeat
the federated protocol while sweeping K ∈{2, 3, 5, 10, 20, 50} and holding every other setting
fixed: 50 communication rounds, IID partitioning, the shared threshold τ = 0.1 of Table 4, and
ten random seeds per configuration. Figure 8 reports test accuracy and readout sparsity as
functions of K.
Two observations stand out. First, accuracy is essentially invariant to the client count. Over
the entire sweep, from K = 2 to K = 50, the accuracy of every dataset moves within a band
of at most 2.6 points, and on four of the seven within about one point (UCI HAR 87.8–88.2%,
Character 97.9–98.4%, ECG5000 91.4–91.8%, Distal. 73.2–74.1%). Notably, at K = 50 each
JapaneseVowels client holds only about five training sequences, yet the global readout still reaches
94.8%, indistinguishable from its K = 5 value. This robustness is expected from the structure of
the update. The server operates on the sums of local gradients and Gauss-Newton Hessians, and
under an IID partition the data-fidelity part of these sums is independent of how the samples
are divided among clients, so K enters the optimization only through the regularization term
and random variation.
Second, readout sparsity also transfers across client counts, staying in the band observed at
K = 5 (e.g. 73.9–80.2% on UCI HAR, 94.3–98.0% on JapaneseVowels) with no systematic trend
in K. This is worth stating because the aggregated objective implicitly scales the penalty with
K (each client’s local loss carries its own copy of the Smooth-L1 term), yet no corresponding
sparsity drift is visible, and fragmenting the data does not make the round-50 readout any
less sparse. We note that this sweep retains the IID partitioning of the main text; how these
conclusions change under heterogeneous client distributions remains part of the future work
discussed in Section 6.4.
