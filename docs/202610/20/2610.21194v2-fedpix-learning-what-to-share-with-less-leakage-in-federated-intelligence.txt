FEDPIX: LEARNING WHAT TO SHARE WITH LESS
LEAKAGE IN FEDERATED INTELLIGENCE

ABSTRACT
Existing federated intelligence paradigms primarily focus on how models are
trained or aggregated, while overlooking a more fundamental question: what in-
formation should leave the client? Existing methods often share model updates
that users cannot easily inspect, or data representations that may reveal sensitive
information. Moreover, generating these representations can be computationally
expensive for resource-constrained clients. We observe that image classification
does not require all the visual details needed to reconstruct the original image.
This suggests that clients could share less information while preserving task perfor-
mance. However, learning what to preserve is challenging when the server cannot
access raw client data. To address this challenge, we propose FedPix, which learns
to share compact, visually inspectable images instead of model updates or interme-
diate features. The key idea is to use the shared representations themselves to learn
what information should be preserved and what can be discarded. By balancing
task utility and reconstruction resistance, FedPix enables the server to improve
the representations without accessing raw client data, while keeping client-side
computation lightweight. Extensive experiments on BloodMNIST, CIFAR-10,
FashionMNIST, and MNIST demonstrate that FedPix achieves strong reconstruc-
tion resistance, with reconstruction PSNR as low as 6.87 dB and synthetic PSNR
as low as 6.74 dB. Meanwhile, FedPix reduces client runtime by up to 96.0% (from
1223 s to 49 s on CIFAR-10), while maintaining competitive classification accuracy
of 99.18% on MNIST, 89.97% on FashionMNIST, 82.24% on BloodMNIST, and
65.63% on CIFAR-10.

INTRODUCTION
Existing federated intelligence paradigms mainly focus on how models are trained or aggregated
across clients, while overlooking a more fundamental question: what information should leave the
client in the first place? Personalized services—recommendation systems, intelligent assistants,
medical diagnosis—increasingly depend on users’ local data such as photos, medical images, and
interaction histories to capture user preferences and support downstream tasks (Ojokoh et al., 2025).
Directly uploading raw data, however, may expose users’ private information. The core challenge is
therefore not only how to train models without centralizing raw data, but how to design a principled
data interface that controls what representation the client outputs to the server.
Existing federated paradigms provide only partial answers to this question, and they can be
understood as operating at three distinct layers of the system stack.
Model coordination layer. Federated learning (FL) retains raw data locally, yet requires clients
to perform iterative optimization and exchange large, abstract, and user-unreadable model up-
dates (McMahan et al., 2017a). Under heterogeneous client data, local optimization may also
introduce client drift before aggregation (Karimireddy et al., 2020). This line of work addresses how
models should be coordinated across clients, but does not specify what information should leave each
client—it simply transmits whatever the local optimizer produces.
Representation transmission layer. Federated data condensation (FDC) communicates human-
readable synthetic samples, but the generation process can be expensive on edge devices, and the
resulting images may still retain excessive similarity to the original images (Xiong et al., 2023;

Holland et al., 2024; Zhang et al., 2024b). Split federated learning (SFL) reduces some client-side
computation, but transmits intermediate features that users find difficult to inspect or compare against
their original data (Thapa et al., 2022; Yu et al., 2025). These methods transmit representations rather
than model parameters, yet they optimize for either distribution fidelity (FDC) or training efficiency
(SFL), not for a controlled privacy–utility trade-off at the data boundary.
Data interface layer. Existing federated paradigms have not explicitly optimized a privacy-
controllable data interface that governs what information is released from the client. Such an interface
differs from conventional compression or feature transmission in its optimization objective: instead of
minimizing storage cost, communication cost, or reconstruction distortion alone, it explicitly learns
which information should be exposed through the client-to-server boundary according to downstream
utility and privacy requirements. Data-level differential privacy provides formal protection through
random perturbation, yet often incurs a substantial loss of downstream utility (Fan, 2018; Shibata
et al., 2023; Cao et al., 2026). Conventional compression and learned compression are primarily
designed to preserve visual fidelity rather than suppress reconstructable information (Wallace, 1992;
Sullivan et al., 2012; Ball’e et al., 2018; 2017). Task-oriented data compression can, in principle,
filter out both privacy- and utility-irrelevant information; however, these methods assume direct
access to original data during optimization and mainly target compression efficiency for downstream
analytics, rather than designing a privacy-aware interface for federated data sharing (Alvar & Baji´c,
2021; Azizian & Baji´c, 2024).
Different from existing task-oriented compression methods (Alvar & Baji´c, 2021; Azizian & Baji´c,
2024), which optimize encoders with direct access to source data for compression efficiency, FedPix
studies representation interface learning under federated constraints where raw client samples are
unavailable. The central objective is therefore not bitrate optimization but controlling the information
exposed through client-to-server transmission while preserving downstream utility.
These limitations indicate that a practical federated data method should satisfy three requirements:
R1: User auditability. The representation transmitted by the client should remain in
the same readable form as the original data (e.g., image-to-image), enabling direct user
inspection.
R2: Edge-device friendliness. The primary computational burden should be borne by the
server whenever possible, accommodating personal phones and laptops.
R3: Reconstruction privacy with task utility. The representation should minimize infor-
mation in the uploaded representation that is useful for reconstructing the original input
while preserving the information required by the downstream task, thereby making the
uploaded representation auditable by and passable through user audit while still meeting the
system’s utility objectives.
We propose FedPix, a federated data interface that learns a task-sufficient yet reconstruction-
resistant representation interface before data leaves the client. In this framework, each client applies a
lightweight representation encoder once to the original image and uploads a compact, human-readable
small-sized image together with its label. The server upsamples the transmitted small-sized images,
trains a classifier on the resulting images, then improves the encoder, and redistributes the updated
encoder to participating clients. Consequently, clients perform no backpropagation or iterative
local optimization. The encoder serves as the implementation module of this interface, realizing
the privacy–utility trade-off through a learnable capacity bottleneck. FedPix provides an auditable
transmission object (R1), substantially reduces client-side computation (R2), and explicitly optimizes
the trade-off between reconstruction similarity and classification utility (R3).
FedPix must improve the encoder without observing raw client inputs. To this end, we develop an
optimization strategy that operates without access to client raw data: it first upsamples the uploaded
small-sized images to train a classifier, then obtains the classifier’s correct classification distribution
over the small-sized images. Treating the encoder and classifier as an integrated whole, for each
small-sized image that has been correctly classified, we upsample it back to the original resolution
and re-input it into the encoder for a second encoding pass. The encoder is then optimized with a
utility objective—that the twice-encoded images remain correctly classified by the classifier—and a
privacy objective—that the dissimilarity between the before-and-after encoded images is maximized.
This design encourages the encoder to correctly extract classification-beneficial information while
making the encoded images as dissimilar as possible to their pre-encoding counterparts, thereby
reducing the feasibility of reconstruction attacks.

CONTRIBUTIONS
The contributions of this work are:
• An auditable federated data interface. We introduce a representation-sharing paradigm
that treats client-to-server transmission as an information selection interface. Unlike conven-
tional representation transmission, the proposed interface learns a task-sufficient representa-
tion under federated constraints, determining what information can be exposed to the server
while maintaining downstream utility. Clients upload compact, human-readable images
through a single encoder forward pass—not model updates, not intermediate features, and
not synthetic data—while all iterative optimization is performed on the server.
• Raw-data-free representation evolution mechanism.
We develop an upsample–
recompress closed loop with correctness-filtered utility preservation, enabling the server to
train a privacy-aware encoder without observing raw client inputs.
• A mechanism-level privacy–utility analysis (Appendix).
We characterize the task-
preserving representation capacity region, identifying a free-representation regime where
reconstruction distortion can increase without increasing the Bayes cross-entropy.
• Comprehensive empirical evaluation. We evaluate utility, reconstruction resistance,
auditability, client cost, communication, non-IID behavior, and membership inference
across four datasets, including a direct ACC–Synthetic-SSIM frontier analysis over multiple
compression levels.

PRELIMINARIES
2.1
THREAT MODEL
We consider an honest-but-curious server that follows the protocol but may infer private information
from uploaded data (Shi et al., 2024; Hu et al., 2023; Shi et al., 2025). Clients share an intermediate
representation S = Φ(X) and its label rather than raw input X; S may be synthetic data, a feature
map, or a latent code (Xiong et al., 2023; Holland et al., 2024; Erdo˘gan et al., 2022; Zhang et al.,
2024b). The server observes S. For FedPix, the server trains Φ and therefore knows its architecture
and parameters. The optimization-based inversion attack below uses this encoder for gradient
computation; Appendix I additionally evaluates a white-box learned restoration attack.

3.3
INFERENCE PHASE
After T training rounds, the framework produces a representation encoder VT −1 and a classifier CT .
For a new sample Dnew, inference encodes it to Inew = VT −1(Dnew), upsamples, and passes it to
CT for prediction ˆynew. Since VT −1 suppresses visually recoverable information, even if an attacker
obtains Inew and attempts a model inversion attack, reconstructing the original input is difficult.
Meanwhile, CT is trained to classify accurately from such capacity-constrained representations,
unifying reconstruction resistance and classification utility within a single framework.

4
EXPERIMENTS
4.1
EXPERIMENTAL SETUP
4.1.1
DATASETS AND BASELINE METHODS
We evaluate FedPix on four standard datasets: BloodMNIST, CIFAR-10, FashionMNIST, and MNIST.
Experiments use 10 clients with full participation (join ratio = 1.0). Non-IID data distributions
are simulated via a Dirichlet distribution (α = 0.5). Unless stated otherwise, early stopping is
applied: training halts when accuracy improvement is below 1% compared to the previous round.
This reduces computational load on edge devices and avoids excessive communication rounds that
degrade user experience. Experiment 1 (baseline comparison) is repeated 10 times and averaged;
others are repeated 5 times and averaged.
Evaluation metrics include classification accuracy (ACC, measuring model utility), model inver-
sion attack metrics (PSNR↓, SSIM↓, LPIPS↑, measuring reconstruction resistance), client runtime
(measuring computational efficiency), and total communication volume (measuring communication
overhead).
PSNR, SSIM, and LPIPS are computed using the IQA-PyTorch toolbox (Chen & Mo, 2022).
Before computing PSNR and SSIM, all images are normalized to the range [0,1].
4.1.2
COMPREHENSIVE BASELINE COMPARISON
This experiment answers a core question: Can FedPix simultaneously satisfy user auditability
(R1), edge-device friendliness (R2), and the reconstruction-utility trade-off (R3)? Results under
the Non-IID setting (α = 0.5) are shown in Table 1.
For R2 (utility-efficiency), FedPix achieves the best or second-best ACC across all datasets while
requiring the lowest client runtime by a wide margin. On BloodMNIST and FashionMNIST, FedPix
attains the highest ACC (82.24% and 89.97%) with runtimes of only 13s and 63s—reductions of
94.6% and 95.6% versus FedAvg. On MNIST, FedPix achieves the highest ACC (99.18%) while
requiring merely 48s, a 95.7% runtime reduction versus FedAvg. On CIFAR-10, FedPix’s ACC
(65.63%) is on par with FedAvg (65.98%) yet its runtime is merely 49s against FedAvg’s 1223s
(a 96.0% reduction). Across all datasets, FedPix consistently ranks first in client efficiency while
maintaining competitive or best accuracy.
For R3 (utility-reconstruction), FedPix attains best or second-best reconstructed data quality and
ranks first across all synthetic data metrics—confirming strong resistance to model inversion attacks
(MoIA). Notably, SFL achieves high-fidelity reconstruction (PSNR 32.13–38.81 dB), revealing the
reconstruction risk of uncompressed feature transmission. FedAvg, which uploads model parameters
rather than data representations, has no meaningful reconstruction or synthetic data metrics (all “—”),
as it operates at the model-coordination layer rather than the data-interface layer.
For R1, FedPix transmits compressed, human-readable image-like data supporting intuitive
inspection—a capability absent in SFL and FedAvg which transmit opaque feature vectors or model
updates. In contrast, existing baselines each sacrifice at least one requirement: CollabDM and FedDM
incur heavy client-side computation, with runtimes up to over 140× that of FedPix; FedSD2C
compromises utility for reconstruction resistance; SFL lacks auditability and exposes raw information
to reconstruction. In summary, FedPix satisfies R1–R3 simultaneously not by improving model
aggregation, but by redesigning the data interface through which clients share information.

ABLATION STUDY ANALYSIS
We conduct an ablation comparing five variants across four datasets (Table 2): (1) Direct downsam-
pling: bicubic downsampling replaces the learnable encoder; (2) W/o anti-recon. loss: both SSIM
and PSNR terms are removed (Lssim=0, Lpsnr=0), retaining only Lce; (3) PSNR only: only the
PSNR loss is active (Lssim=0); (4) SSIM only: only the SSIM loss is active (Lpsnr=0); (5) Complete
FedPix: both SSIM and PSNR losses are active.
Direct downsampling yields high synthetic PSNR/SSIM (e.g., 22–32 dB, 0.83–0.97), confirming
that naive spatial reduction provides negligible privacy protection. Removing both anti-reconstruction
losses achieves the lowest synthetic PSNR on BloodMNIST and CIFAR-10, and the lowest synthetic
SSIM on CIFAR-10, but it does not consistently dominate the other variants. Its mean accuracy is
lower than that of the complete model on these two datasets.
The independent activation of SSIM and PSNR reveals complementary roles. On MNIST, PSNR-
only dominates nearly all privacy metrics (Syn. SSIM 0.13, Recon. PSNR 6.70, Recon. SSIM
0.09) while maintaining competitive accuracy (99.03%). On FashionMNIST, the complete model
achieves the best synthetic privacy (Syn. PSNR 5.59, Syn. SSIM 0.11), while SSIM-only excels
at reconstruction resistance (Recon. PSNR 6.92). On BloodMNIST, SSIM-only achieves the best
reconstruction SSIM (0.27), and PSNR-only achieves the highest accuracy (87.88%). On CIFAR-10,
the “w/o” variant leads in all synthetic and reconstruction SSIM, but SSIM-only achieves the best
reconstruction PSNR (9.55).
The results reveal that the interaction between anti-reconstruction losses and the utility objective
is not consistently orthogonal. On MNIST, PSNR-only dominates nearly all privacy metrics (Syn.
SSIM 0.1288, Recon. SSIM 0.0894) while the complete model has higher Syn. SSIM (0.2267) and
slightly higher Recon. SSIM (0.0903), suggesting interference between the loss terms. On CIFAR-10
and BloodMNIST, the variant without anti-reconstruction loss achieves the best privacy in several
metrics, yet the complete model incurs a visible privacy cost (higher SSIM/PSNR) without a commen-
surate accuracy gain. On FashionMNIST, the complete model achieves the best synthetic privacy, but
SSIM-only excels at reconstruction resistance. The “best” variant differs across datasets and metrics
without a clear pattern. This instability—where adding a privacy-targeted loss sometimes worsens
privacy—indicates that SSIM/PSNR minimization is not always aligned with utility preservation
in gradient space. Understanding and resolving this misalignment, perhaps through adaptive loss
weighting or gradient projection, is a direction for future work.

CONCLUSION
We propose FedPix, a Federated Data Interface where clients upload capacity-constrained representa-
tions and the server optimizes both classifier and encoder via an upsample–recompress closed loop.
FedPix achieves user auditability, edge-device friendliness (client runtime reduced up to 96%), and
strong reconstruction resistance with competitive accuracy, while remaining inherently robust to
non-IID data.
Limitations. First, the capacity bottleneck (an 8 × 8 spatial compression) is fixed globally
rather than adapted per task or dataset; our theoretical capacity region points toward adaptive
mechanisms but does not yet realize them. Second, the evaluation focuses on image classification
with a single encoder architecture, and the interface has not been validated on other modalities or
tasks. Third, the on-device deployment benchmarks FP32 CPU inference only; INT8 quantization
and NPU acceleration, which could further shrink the client footprint, remain unexplored. Finally,
reconstruction resistance is evaluated against strong but specific attack families (model inversion and
white-box restoration); future stronger attacks, e.g., diffusion-based reconstruction, could narrow the
observed privacy margins.
Future work. Beyond addressing the above, we plan to (i) develop adaptive capacity mechanisms
that negotiate the compression ratio per task and per user, (ii) extend the interface to non-image
modalities and downstream tasks, (iii) deploy quantized, NPU-accelerated client encoders, and (iv)
integrate the data interface with downstream federated aggregation and user-facing audit tooling,
allowing participants to inspect exactly what leaves their devices.
ETHICS STATEMENT
This work studies representation sharing in federated intelligence and uses only publicly available
benchmark datasets (BloodMNIST, CIFAR-10, FashionMNIST, and MNIST); it does not involve
human subjects, personal data collection, or model release. The threat model considered throughout
the paper is an honest-but-curious server, and the reconstruction and membership inference attacks
reported here are used solely to quantify the leakage of the proposed data interface, not to attack
deployed systems. We note that the representation interface we propose reduces, but does not

A
RECONSTRUCTION RESISTANCE METRICS
We quantify representation leakage from two complementary perspectives. First, Synthetic–to–
Original measures the direct similarity between transmitted synthetic or compressed representations
and the original data. Second, Reconstructed–to–Original measures the similarity between model-
inversion-reconstructed samples and the original data. Following prior reconstruction and perceptual
similarity studies (Sun et al., 2023a; Zhang et al., 2018), we use PSNR, SSIM, and LPIPS as evaluation
metrics. Higher PSNR/SSIM and lower LPIPS indicate greater similarity to the original data and thus
greater reconstruction leakage.
These metrics require access to original data and are therefore used only from the researcher’s
evaluation perspective. The HbC server in the actual protocol does not directly access raw data; it
only observes uploaded intermediate representations.
We next provide a detailed rationale for each design choice, explaining how it concretely addresses
the three requirements R1–R3.
B
DETAILED DESIGN RATIONALE OF FEDPIX
This section provides a detailed discussion of each design choice in FedPix and how it addresses the
three requirements identified in Introduction: R1 (User Auditability), R2 (Edge-device Friendli-
ness), and R3 (Balancing Dissimilarity, Non-Reconstructability, and Utility).
B.1
WHY TRAIN A UNIFIED ENCODER ON THE SERVER? (ADDRESSING R2 AND R3)
A naive approach that trains encoders locally on each client faces two fundamental limitations.
Incompatible non-IID encoders learned by separate clients cannot be reasonably aggregated, and
running training procedures on edge hardware introduces substantial computational overhead. FedPix
adopts a single unified encoder trained centrally on the server and distributed every communica-
tion round, which fully removes client-side optimization workload. Clients only execute a single
forward pass, so local objective divergence, client drift, and extra corrective stabilization mechanisms
become unnecessary. Regardless of data heterogeneity, the server’s classifier receives inputs within a
homogeneous encoding domain. This circumvents the pervasive non-IID challenge in conventional
parameter-aggregation federated learning. This design supports R2 (Edge-device Friendliness) by
limiting client computation, and addresses R3 (Balancing Dissimilarity, Non-Reconstructability,
and Utility). Consistent encoding stabilizes decision boundaries and prevents utility loss caused by
mismatched client-specific encoders. We stress that this centralized design is intentional: FedPix
targets the data-interface layer (what information leaves the client), instead of the model-coordination
layer for local model aggregation.
B.2
WHY THE “UPSAMPLE-RECOMPRESS” CLOSED LOOP? (ADDRESSING R2)
The server cannot access raw client data, which prevents end-to-end encoder training using paired
original and compressed samples. FedPix exploits the property that upsampling introduces no new
statistical information. The server upsamples uploaded compressed representation It to I′

All training procedures run server-side, and clients only perform one forward pass for compression,
with no backpropagation or iterative local optimization required. This design directly supports
R2 (Edge-device Friendliness), shifting all optimization burden away from resource-limited edge
devices.
B.3
WHY MINIMIZE SSIM AND PSNR FOR RECONSTRUCTION SUPPRESSION? (ADDRESSING
R3)
Model inversion attacks take compressed images as initialization to reconstruct original inputs.
Reconstruction difficulty rises when compressed outputs exhibit larger structural and pixel-level
differences relative to raw data. FedPix directly minimizes SSIM and PSNR measured between
the upsampled re-encoded output UpSample(I′′
t ) and the upsampled input I′
t. Since SSIM and
PSNR both increase with similarity, minimizing these loss terms pushes the encoder to reduce the
similarity between its output and the input, enlarging overall divergence. SSIM captures structural
discrepancies in luminance, contrast and spatial layout, while PSNR reflects pixel-level intensity
deviations. The two metrics deliver complementary signals to obstruct visual reconstruction. By
actively maximizing divergence between compressed representations and upsampled data, FedPix
15


suppresses visually recoverable information and mitigates inversion risks, which directly addresses
R3 (Balancing Dissimilarity, Non-Reconstructability, and Utility).
B.4
WHY USE CLASSIFIER CORRECTNESS AS A UTILITY FILTER? (ADDRESSING R3)
Pure minimization of SSIM and PSNR will indiscriminately erase all signal, including task-relevant
information critical for classification. FedPix introduces an auxiliary classifier C trained and frozen
on the server. The classifier generates hard-label predictions on upsampled compressed data; only
samples whose predictions match ground-truth labels (Icorrect
t
) contribute to the utility consistency
loss Lce. This constraint guides the encoder to preserve information shaping decision boundaries
while suppressing visually recoverable content under bandwidth limits. The loss Lce sustains task
utility, while SSIM and PSNR minimization enforces dissimilarity and non-reconstructability. Their
combination achieves a favorable reconstruction–utility trade-off and fulfills the demands of R3.
B.5
WHY COMPRESSED REPRESENTATIONS PRESERVE THE ORIGINAL DATA FORMAT?
(ADDRESSING R1)
Split learning transmits abstract feature vectors that lack intuitive interpretability for end users.
Different from such paradigms, FedPix’s encoder Vt outputs a downsampled image It that retains the
original visual format (e.g., RGB channels, spatial layout) at reduced resolution y×y. The compressed
representation maintains an intuitive correspondence with raw data. Users can compare uploaded
content against original samples to audit privacy risks: one may identify coarse semantic content,
although visual inspection alone cannot certify that fine-grained personal details are unrecoverable.
This transparency satisfies R1 (User Auditability), enabling non-expert users to understand, audit,
and consent to shared information — a benefit unavailable for opaque feature vectors or encrypted
model parameters.
B.6
WHY USE AN ADDITIVE LOSS WITH SSIM AND PSNR? (ADDRESSING R3)
Single dissimilarity metrics struggle to jointly control structural and pixel-level divergence, motivat-
ing an additive composite loss formulation. FedPix adopts λLce + Lssim + βLpsnr. SSIM is naturally
bounded ([−1, 1]) and contributes stable gradients, so its weight is fixed to unity. PSNR operates on a
wider numerical scale (typically 5–40 dB), so β is empirically selected to balance the gradient magni-
tudes contributed by the two dissimilarity terms. The tunable λ separately regulates classification
utility. The additive form decouples structural and pixel-wise suppression. SSIM and PSNR provide
different gradient signals, but their combination does not uniformly outperform isolated terms; the
ablation in Table 2 shows dataset-dependent interactions. This loss construction delivers balanced,
interpretable adjustment over the reconstruction–utility frontier and directly addresses R3 (Balancing
Dissimilarity, Non-Reconstructability, and Utility).

B.8
MODEL INVERSION ATTACK SETTINGS
The model inversion attack uses an encoder to reverse-reconstruct original images from synthetic
data via gradient descent. The reconstruction process uses the Adam optimizer, a learning rate of
0.1, 500 iterations, and a total variation regularization weight of 0.001. After reconstruction, PSNR,
SSIM, and LPIPS are computed between the reconstructed and original images (see Section A for
metric definitions).
The reconstruction attack evaluation procedure is as follows: obtain the aggregated synthetic
data and their corresponding original data indices from the server side, uniformly select samples by
category (10 per category), and compute the metrics between the reconstructed data and the original
data.
B.9
FEDPIX IMPLEMENTATION DETAILS
The representation encoder uses a convolutional architecture with an enhanced ConvPoolBlock. By
default, it has five 3 × 3 convolutional layers, each followed by GroupNorm and ReLU, ending with
AdaptiveAvgPool2d to downsample to the target spatial size. The hidden layers have 128 channels,
and the output is a 3-channel RGB image produced by a 1×1 convolution with tanh activation (pixel
range [−1, 1]). A Squeeze-and-Excitation channel attention mechanism recalibrates feature maps
via global average pooling and a two-layer fully connected network. For CIFAR-10, the encoder
compresses a 3 × 32 × 32 input to 3 × 8 × 8 (192 values), achieving a 16× compression ratio (from
3072 values). When the compressed image size is set to 24, the output is 3 × 24 × 24, giving a 1.78×
compression ratio. The classifier follows the ConvNet architecture used in FedAvg.

D.2
SYSTEM SCALABILITY
This experiment answers one question: Can FedPix maintain stable performance as the number
of clients scales up? We evaluate on CIFAR-10 under four client counts (1, 5, 20, 100), comparing
FedPix with four FL baselines (FedAvg, FedProx, SCAFFOLD, PARS) and two representation-
learning based methods (FedSD2C, SFL).
Table 3 reveals a clear scalability pattern. FL baselines (FedAvg, FedProx) uniformly exhibit
steep accuracy declines as the client pool grows: FedAvg drops from 78.52% (1 client) to 39.02%
(100 clients), and FedProx collapses from 76.68% to 32.84%, driven by the increasing difficulty of
aggregating divergent local models under diminishing per-client data. SCAFFOLD partially mitigates
this drift via control variates, retaining 57.70% at 100 clients, while PARS remains notably resilient
(64.03% at 100 clients) owing to its adaptive pseudo-labeling strategy. SFL shows strong single-client
performance (72.01%), benefiting from the full dataset being available to one split-learning instance,
but degrades to 51.27% at 100 clients as feature-space alignment across many split paths becomes
challenging. FedSD2C remains consistently low across all scales (28.72–45.54%), reflecting the
limited utility of its latent codes under data fragmentation.
In stark contrast, FedPix maintains nearly constant accuracy across all client scales: 65.79% (1
client) to 65.14% (100 clients), a drop of only 0.65 percentage points with uniformly tight standard
deviations (±0.97–1.95%). At 20 and 100 clients, FedPix outperforms the strongest baseline by 0.6
and 1.1 percentage points, respectively, while at 100 clients it surpasses FedAvg by over 26 percentage
points. This stability arises from the same architectural property that confers Non-IID robustness
(see Non-IID experiment in the main text): the server trains a single centralized classifier on pooled
compressed representations, so adding more clients simply increases the diversity and volume of
the training pool without aggravating the challenge of aggregating divergent models. The server’s
classifier operates on a unified, fixed-dimensional representation space, effectively decoupling the
final performance from the number of participating clients. The unified compressor design thus
provides inherent scalability: performance depends on the total data available to the server, not on
how that data is partitioned across clients.

I.2
RESTORATION MODEL
The restoration network is a ResUNet—a U-Net augmented with residual bottleneck blocks. The
encoder path contains four down-sampling stages (DoubleConv →MaxPool, base channels 64),
expanding to 512 channels at 8 × 8 spatial resolution. The bottleneck consists of two residual blocks
(Conv→BN→ReLU→Conv→BN with skip connection). The decoder path mirrors the encoder with
four up-sampling stages and skip connections, followed by a 1 × 1 convolution with tanh activation.
The model has ∼500K trainable parameters. Training uses Adam (lr = 10−3) with cosine annealing
over 20 epochs, minimizing ℓ1 loss.
I.3

We evaluate on the same four datasets and use the same PSNR / SSIM / LPIPS metrics defined
in Section A. Table 9 reports the reconstruction quality of the ResUNet attacker across all four
datasets, alongside FedPix’s Synthetic and Reconstructed metrics from the main baseline comparison
in Section 4. All three rows per dataset share the same compressed representations; they differ only
in whether an active restoration step is applied and how strong that restoration model is.
Active restoration recovers additional information. The Adaptive (ResUNet) attacker achieves
PSNR of 10.80–13.81 dB, improving on the direct Synthetic comparison by 2.87–5.54 dB. Relative to
MoIA, its PSNR gains range from 0.40 dB on CIFAR-10 to 5.62 dB on FashionMNIST, accompanied

understate recoverable information. These metrics do not define a universal threshold for usable
reconstruction.
Global structure partially survives compression. LPIPS values for the Adaptive attacker are
moderate (0.08–0.19), indicating that the bottleneck retains coarse spatial layout and color statistics—
precisely the information needed for downstream classification. The tension between low PSNR and

moderate LPIPS reflects the design intent of FedPix: preserving what is in the image while discarding
how it looks in detail.
Scope of the attack evaluation. These results characterize the specified white-box restoration
model and training budget. They are not an upper bound on attacker success; other architectures,
training budgets, or auxiliary data could yield stronger reconstructions.
I.4
ANALYSIS
Dataset-dependent reconstruction quality. CIFAR-10 has the highest Adaptive LPIPS (0.19),
whereas MNIST has the lowest Adaptive PSNR (10.80 dB, versus 11.34 dB on CIFAR-10). Different
metrics therefore yield different dataset rankings; these results do not establish that natural images
are uniformly the most resistant to reconstruction.
Input complexity has limited impact. Across four datasets spanning simple digits (MNIST)
to natural scenes (CIFAR-10), the Adaptive PSNR range is narrow (10.80–13.81 dB). The 8 × 8
bottleneck dominates over input complexity: even MNIST digits on blank backgrounds remain
substantially degraded after reconstruction. This suggests that reconstruction resistance is a structural
property of the spatial reduction ratio rather than dataset-dependent.
White-box attack remains relevant. Across all four datasets, the learned attacker improves
PSNR, SSIM, and LPIPS relative to MoIA. These gains show that a spatial bottleneck reduces
representation size without necessarily eliminating exploitable structure. Reconstruction resistance
must therefore be assessed against adaptive attacks as well as direct image similarity.

RELATED WORK
J.1
TRADITIONAL FEDERATED LEARNING
Federated learning enables collaborative training without sharing raw data and has become a founda-
tional paradigm for privacy-sensitive services (McMahan et al., 2017a). FedAvg (McMahan et al.,
2017a), as the earliest and most widely adopted aggregation algorithm, constructs a global model
by weighted averaging of client updates but suffers from severe client drift under non-IID data
distributions. FedProx (Li et al., 2020b) introduces a proximal term to constrain local updates, SCAF-
FOLD (Karimireddy et al., 2020) corrects update biases via control variates, FedAdam (Reddi et al.,
2020) brings adaptive optimization to federated learning, and PARS (Akpinar et al., 2026) designs
pseudo-labeling with adaptive sampling strategies for semi-supervised scenarios. FedLADA (Sun
et al., 2023b) incorporates a locally amended technique into adaptive optimizers to mitigate client
drift, while ∆-FL (Pillutla et al., 2024) employs superquantile aggregation to improve performance
across heterogeneous clients.
A common thread across these methods is the model parameter exchange paradigm: each client
locally optimizes on its own data distribution Dk, yielding a local model wk. When Dk̸ = Dj, local
objectives diverge, causing client drift and aggregation bias. Existing approaches are corrective,
primarily mitigating the optimization consequences of non-IID data without changing the underlying
communication interface.
Limitations and our distinction. FedPix does not compete with these FL aggregation algorithms.
Rather, it operates at a different layer of the federated system stack. Traditional FL addresses the
question “how should models be coordinated across clients?”; FedPix addresses the prior question
“what information should leave the client?” The two layers are orthogonal and complementary:
one could deploy a FedPix compressor at the data interface of any FL system, upload compressed
representations instead of raw data, and still apply FedAvg, SCAFFOLD, or any other aggregation
protocol downstream. FedPix’s centralized training on a shared compressor’s outputs naturally avoids
the non-IID aggregation problem, but this is a consequence of its data-interface design, not a claim to
replace FL aggregation.
J.2
FEDERATED DATA CONDENSATION
Federated Data Condensation (FDC) reduces communication overhead by compressing raw data into
small synthetic datasets. FedDM is the first to introduce data condensation into federated learning,
achieving communication-efficient model updates through iterative distribution matching (Xiong et al.,
2023). To address the multi-round communication bottleneck, FedSD2C adopts a one-round synthetic
distiller-distillate communication framework (Zhang et al., 2024b), while CollabDM captures global
data distribution through collaborative data distillation (Holland et al., 2024). Zhang et al. (2024a)
further integrated differential privacy into condensation to provide quantifiable privacy guarantees.
FedDC proposes discrepancy distribution matching and collaborative knowledge distillation for
27


IoT scenarios (Ma et al., 2025), and FedPAD adopts an aggregation-free prototype-based adaptive
distillation paradigm (Zhong et al., 2026).
Limitations and our distinction. While data condensation reduces communication by compress-
ing the number of samples, it places substantial optimization burden on the client side and targets
high-fidelity reconstruction of the original data distribution—both of which are misaligned with
edge-device deployment and privacy preservation. FedPix takes a fundamentally different path: it
compresses the per-sample information content (|zi| ≪|xi|) rather than the sample count, offloads
all compressor training to the server, and explicitly optimizes for task utility with a reconstruction-
suppression objective. Moreover, FedPix addresses the data-interface layer (what clients upload)
rather than the communication-efficiency layer (how much is uploaded). The two approaches are
complementary: one could apply FedPix’s per-sample compression and then condense the resulting
compressed dataset, or vice versa.
J.3
SPLIT FEDERATED LEARNING
Split Federated Learning (SFL) reduces client computation by transmitting only intermediate features
instead of raw data (Thapa et al., 2022). Turina et al. (2020) highlighted its efficiency-privacy balance.
FLEX-SFL further improved training efficiency in heterogeneous edge environments through adaptive
splitting and client selection mechanisms (Yu et al., 2025). However, SFL remains vulnerable to
various attacks: security analysis revealed multiple attack surfaces (Khan et al., 2022); Unsplit
demonstrated feature inversion, model stealing, and label inference (Erdo˘gan et al., 2022); subsequent
attacks include GRAMSSAT (label inference) (Zhang et al., 2025), PCAT (pseudo-client attack) (Gao
& Zhang, 2023), and PPoison (poisoning attack) (Liu et al., 2026). These studies collectively expose
the fragility of intermediate representation management in existing SFL methods and underscore the
need for stronger privacy protections.
Limitations and our distinction. A critical vulnerability of SFL is that the transmitted inter-
mediate features are not interpretable by end users, making direct human inspection of uploaded
information difficult. Moreover, as prior attacks (Unsplit (Erdo˘gan et al., 2022), GRAMSSAT (Zhang
et al., 2025), PCAT (Gao & Zhang, 2023), PPoison (Liu et al., 2026)) have shown, these features
carry enough structural information to enable feature inversion, model stealing, and label inference.
FedPix addresses this gap by transmitting compressed images that can be upsampled for inspection
that preserve a visual correspondence to the original input, allowing users to inspect what is being
uploaded. At the same time, our task-directed compression actively suppresses reconstructability,
providing stronger reconstruction resistance than the passive feature exposure in SFL.
J.4
DIFFERENTIAL PRIVACY MECHANISMS
Differential Privacy (DP) provides quantifiable privacy guarantees by injecting calibrated noise
into data or model parameters, and has been widely adopted in federated learning frameworks to
defend against privacy attacks. DP-Pix first combined differential privacy with image pixelization,
achieving provable privacy guarantees by injecting noise into the pixelization process (Fan, 2018);
DP-GLOW, based on invertible generative models, adds noise in the latent space to achieve privacy
protection for generated images (Shibata et al., 2023). LDP-Slicing proposed a local differential
privacy perturbation mechanism based on randomized bit-plane slicing, which reduces noise variance
by processing data plane by plane, thereby improving data utility while preserving privacy (Cao
et al., 2026). Furthermore, DP-FedAvg combines differential privacy with the federated averaging
algorithm, enabling privacy budget composition across multiple training rounds through privacy
accounting (McMahan et al., 2017b); PRISM-FL proposes an obfuscated image synthesis framework
under client-level differential privacy, balancing privacy and utility by generating datasets that
resemble the private data distribution yet exhibit substantial visual differences from the original
data (Soufleri, 2025).
Limitations and our distinction. The fundamental drawback of DP-based data perturbation is its
indiscriminate nature: noise is added to all information dimensions regardless of their relevance to the
downstream task, leading to a severe utility-privacy trade-off that worsens with data dimensionality.
FedPix overcomes this by replacing blind noise with a learnable, task-directed utility-privacy filter.
The compressor is trained on the server to selectively preserve task-relevant cues while suppressing
those that enable reconstruction, all without accessing raw client data. Our experiments compare
empirical utility and attack metrics for selected configurations; they do not establish superiority at a
matched formal privacy budget.

J.5
LOSSY COMPRESSION AND LEARNED COMPRESSION
Lossy compression has long been studied as a means to reduce storage and transmission costs.
Traditional codecs such as JPEG (Wallace, 1992), JPEG2000, and HEVC (Sullivan et al., 2012) rely
on hand-crafted transforms (e.g., DCT, wavelet) and quantization to achieve compression. While
these methods are computationally efficient on edge devices and produce human-readable images,
they are non-learnable and optimize for reconstruction fidelity rather than task utility or privacy.
Consequently, they cannot adapt to downstream tasks and provide no formal protection against
reconstruction attacks.
More recently, learned image compression (LIC) has emerged as a powerful alternative, employing
neural autoencoders to optimize the rate-distortion trade-off end-to-end (Ball’e et al., 2018; 2017;
Minnen et al., 2018). These methods typically encode images into compact latent representations
that are entropy-coded into bitstreams. While they achieve superior compression ratios, they suffer
from two key limitations in the context of privacy-aware federated learning: (1) the transmitted
latent representations or bitstreams are not human-readable, violating user auditability (R1); and
(2) the encoder often requires significant computation or even task-specific fine-tuning on the client
side, conflicting with edge-device friendliness (R2). Furthermore, like traditional codecs, LIC
methods optimize for reconstruction quality, which inherently conflicts with the privacy goal of non-
reconstructability (R3) (Blau & Michaeli, 2018; 2019). The seminal work of Blau and Michaeli (Blau
& Michaeli, 2019) formalized the rate-distortion-perception trade-off, showing that perfect perceptual
quality (i.e., indistinguishability from the original) is fundamentally at odds with low distortion—a
tension that directly translates to the privacy domain, where high reconstruction fidelity implies high
privacy leakage.
A few recent works have explored privacy-oriented compression with fixed (non-learned) trans-
formations. DeepFixCX (Gaudio et al., 2023) proposes an explainable and privacy-oriented medical
image compression framework based on wavelet packet transforms and spatial pooling. By removing
local spatial information and suppressing edge direction via ℓ1 pooling, DeepFixCX generates human-
readable compressed images that mitigate patient re-identification while maintaining diagnostic utility.
However, DeepFixCX is non-learnable: its wavelet filters and pooling operations have fixed weights,
meaning the compression strategy cannot adapt to new tasks or data distributions beyond the medical
imaging scenarios for which it was designed. Moreover, while the compression is fast (650–1700
images per second on a laptop CPU/GPU), the client still bears the full computational burden of the
transform and pooling, and the method does not explicitly optimize against reconstruction attacks
such as model inversion. These limitations highlight the need for a learnable privacy-aware com-
pression framework that shifts training overhead to the server while keeping client-side inference
minimal.
Other works have explored task-oriented or semantic compression (Liu et al., 2019; Wang et al.,
2025), which prioritizes task utility over pixel-level fidelity. However, these approaches still typically
require client-side training or transmit non-auditable features. For instance, Liu et al. (2019) analyzed
the classification-distortion-perception trade-off, showing that optimizing for classification accuracy
does not automatically guarantee privacy against reconstruction. Similarly, Wang et al. (2025)
proposed task-oriented lossy compression frameworks under data, perception, and classification
constraints, but the training burden remains on the client side.
Limitations and our distinction. To the best of our knowledge, FedPix is the first framework that
provides reconstruction resistance through a capacity-constrained representation interface while simul-
taneously satisfying all three requirements: it transmits human-readable compressed images, shifts all
training overhead to the server, and explicitly optimizes for dissimilarity and non-reconstructability.
J.6
TASK-ORIENTED IMAGE COMPRESSION
Task-oriented image compression has emerged as a paradigm shift from traditional rate-distortion
optimization toward preserving information relevant to specific downstream tasks such as classifica-
tion, detection, or segmentation (Liu et al., 2019; Wang et al., 2025). Unlike conventional codecs
that minimize pixel-level distortion for human perception, these methods integrate task-specific
objectives—e.g., classification cross-entropy or segmentation mIoU—into the compression pipeline,
enabling extreme bitrate reduction with minimal utility degradation (Codevilla et al., 2021; Kawawa-
Beaudan et al., 2022). A prominent line of work organizes the latent representation according to
task-specific mutual information, selectively retaining features that are informative for analytics
while discarding those that are not (Alvar & Baji´c, 2021). Azizian and Baji´c (Azizian & Baji´c, 2024)
propose a privacy-preserving autoencoder (PPA) that uses adversarial training to force the encoder to
29


discard pre-specified private attributes (e.g., faces, license plates) from compressed representations
while preserving task-relevant features for collaborative object detection. The encoder is trained on a
public dataset at the server and then deployed to edge devices for single-pass inference. More recent
efforts formulate the problem as rate-distortion-utility joint optimization, where the utility loss directly
guides the compressor to produce task-favorable representations for downstream analytics such as
classification, segmentation, and detection (Kubiak & Hadfield, 2021; Ge et al., 2024). InfoShape
further leverages mutual information neural estimators to train a lossy encoder that explicitly trades
off privacy and utility via information-theoretic criteria (Esfahanizadeh et al., 2023).
However, these methods share a fundamental assumption: the compressor can directly access
original data during optimization. They mainly optimize compression efficiency for downstream
analytics, rather than designing a privacy-controlled interface for federated data sharing. In a federated
setting where raw data never leaves the client, this assumption breaks down, and the optimization
objective must be reformulated to operate without direct data access.
Limitations and our distinction. These methods either (i) operate on latent features that are
neither human-readable nor auditable (Alvar & Baji´c, 2021; Azizian & Baji´c, 2024), (ii) require clients
to run computationally heavy encoders or even perform local training (Esfahanizadeh et al., 2023),
or (iii) treat privacy protection as a secondary concern rather than a first-class objective (Codevilla
et al., 2021). FedPix addresses these gaps by introducing a Federated Data Interface in which the
encoder is optimized on the server via an “upsample-recompress” closed loop without ever accessing
raw client data, clients perform only a single lightweight forward pass, and the output remains a
human-readable compressed image that users can audit. To the best of our knowledge, FedPix is the
first framework that realizes a federated data interface with explicit privacy protection, task-aware
representation learning, and server-side optimization without raw data access in a single cohesive
design.
J.7
POSITIONING OF OUR WORK
FedPix occupies a distinct position in the federated ecosystem. It is not a model-aggregation
FL algorithm, a data-condensation method, a split-learning variant, or a DP mechanism—though
it shares motivations with each. FedPix is best understood as a Federated Data Interface: a
principled mechanism for clients to share task-useful yet privacy-limited representations with a
server. It simultaneously ensures user auditability (human-readable compressed images), edge-device
friendliness (single forward pass on client), and a balanced trade-off between dissimilarity, non-
reconstructability, and task utility. By decoupling encoder training from client-side inference and
leveraging upsampled compressed data as optimization proxies, FedPix satisfies all three requirements
without client-side optimization or parameter aggregation. The shared encoder, trained centrally
and distributed periodically, naturally circumvents the client drift issue common under non-IID data
distributions. We emphasize that FedPix is orthogonal and complementary to existing FL methods: it
addresses the data interface layer (what leaves the client), while traditional FL addresses the model
coordination layer (how models are aggregated).
