# Bridging Compressed Communication and Local Steps via Spectral Method in Federated Learning

Innopolis University Federated Learning (FL) enables collaborative training of complex models across large-scale networks of edge devices while keeping data localized. However, data heterogeneity and high communication overhead remain fundamental bottlenecks. Recently, the spectral optimizer Muon has demonstrated remarkable advantages over Adam-like methods in centralized training. While recent efforts have attempted to transition Muon to FL, naive integrations into traditional architectures fail short. Crucially, existing approaches do not sup-port the the communication compression and local steps — the two foundational techniques of efficient FL. To address this limitation, we introduce a novel spectral FL method that utilizes both local updates and compression. We establish rigorous convergence guarantees under mild assumptions, notably eliminating the restrictive bounded heterogeneity condition. Empirical evaluation on standard image classification bench-marks validates the efficiency and robustness of our approach. 

1 Introduction 

Federated Learning (FL) [1] is a paradigm of training a shared model across a set of edge devices (often called workers or clients). The clients collect data locally and use it to perform updates, which are aggregated by the server. Formally, the problem statement is as follows:

The communication bottleneck remains a central challenge in federated learning [3]. The source of this issue is two-fold. Firstly, due to the number of devices in the network, coordinating them becomes a substantial asynchronous workload [4]. Thus, reducing communication frequency is important in practice. Algorithmically, the problem can be addressed by local steps. Here, each client performs several local update steps between aggregations [5]. Secondly, an individual model may contain up to billions of parameters [6], and its full transmission severely slows down the aggregation process. Communication compression aims to reduce the size of each message by removing redundant or low-importance information. In practice, model size can be reduced by orders of magnitude with essentially no performance loss [7, 8]. Modern machine learning problems are growing in complexity and demand advanced optimization techniques. Currently, the most widely used solutions rely on adaptive algorithms like AdaGrad [9], Adam [10], AdamW [11], and Shampoo [12]. These methods adapt the update to the local geometry of the problem using current optimization statistics. The paradigm has become the gold standard since its development [13], and it was successfully applied to the FL setting [14, 15, 16]. 1arXiv:2610.23434v1 [cs.LG] 16 Oct 2026 A different strategy emerged recently: an adaptation to the spectrum of matrix updates instead of the geometry of the parameter space [17, 18, 19]. Subsequent work has empirically demonstrated its advantages over classic optimizers [20, 21], and Muon [17] has attracted significant attention as the most efficient. Notably, Muon can be viewed as a Linear Minimization Oracle (LMO) under the spectral matrix norm, and this perspective yields theoretical improvements over classic approaches [22, 19]. Hence, transferring this paradigm to FL can yield methods that surpass adaptive approaches. 

2 Related Works 2.1 Methods for Communication Compression 

Compression aims to reduce the size of the transmitted message while retaining nearly all essential information. Two common approaches are quantization – reducing the bit precision of each parameter [23, 24], and sparsification – removing a subset of the vector’s values [25, 26]. Theoretically, compressors fall into two commonly studied classes, unbiased and contractive [27]. For unbiased compressors, the expectation of the output is equal to the original vector, while contractive compressors do not have this property. Unbiased compression is conceptually similar to the stochasticity of gradients in the finite-sum setting. In both cases, the distortion is bounded but does not vanish as the iterate approaches the optimum. Hence, techniques developed for the stochastic setting carry over. Namely, the majority of works in this line are driven by Variance Reduction (VR) [28, 29] mechanisms that were initially designed for stochastic gradients [30, 31, 32, 33]. However, unbiased compressors tend to perform poorly in practice [27, Figure 5]. Thus, we focus on contractive compression. Notably, simple SGD with an arbitrary contractive compressor does not converge. To overcome the obstacle, special techniques are required, the most widely used of which being Error Feedback ( EF ). First appeared as a heuristic [34], EF later received a theoretical foundation with arbitrary compression [35, 36, 27]. The core concept of EF is combining the current message with the error from the previous compression, which compensates for inaccuracies at previous steps. Crucially, EF allows one to establish convergence. EF21 is a noteworthy advancement that improves convergence rates in the non-convex setup by utilizing a distinct error accounting procedure [37]. The idea is to compress the residuals instead of the full message. Both EF and EF21 have found numerous applications in FL [35, 8, 38]. 

2.2 Local Steps 

Another approach to tackling the communication bottleneck is local steps. Instead of calculating a single gradient, each client performs several steps over its local dataset and communicates the accumulated model update. This way, the costly server-side aggregation is performed less often. The technique is extensively studied [39, 40, 41, 5]. Local steps have a crucial downside: clients lose the alignment of their training trajectories between aggregations. As a result, under strong data heterogeneity, the local models diverge, and the training process stalls. Early work introduced similarity assumptions across clients, such as identical data or bounded gradients [41, 5]. In practice, most of these assumptions are infeasible [42, Table 3]. The issue was tackled by the SCAFFOLD algorithm [42]. The authors introduced control variates that allow clients to maintain the correct update direction during local steps. This method was later extended with compression in SCAFCOM [43]. 

2.3 Spectral Methods in FL 

Jordan et al. [17] introduced Muon , a matrix-structure-aware method. In a nutshell, the algorithm orthogonalizes the updates via SVD, normalizing the gradients and thereby stabilizing training. Notably, this orthogonalization can be viewed as a Linear Minimization Oracle operator over the gradients in the spectral matrix norm [22, 19]. Moreover, Kim and Oh [44] established the convergence of Muon under inexact SVD computed by the Newton-Schulz iteration, further motivating its practical adoption. These theoretical insights and its practical performance 2have drawn considerable attention, motivating a growing body of work that studies and extends the method [20, 45, 46, 47, 48, 49]. Some recent papers employed spectral methods in a distributed manner [46, 50], but only in the homogeneous setting. As discussed above, this assumption is unrealistic. The first work to fully address this was [51]. The authors use a general scheme of SCAFFOLD [42]. Their FedMuon replaces the plain gradients in the local steps of 

SCAFFOLD with LMO updates. However, the scheme assumes the transmission of full model updates and control variates, which incurs communication overhead. Subsequently, compression was applied to Muon updates via EF21 

[52] and the VR technique of VR-MARINA [53]. Crucially, both works forgo local steps, accessing the LMO once per round at the server – yet local steps are a pillar of communication-efficient FL. The communication bottleneck for 

Muon -like methods, therefore, remains unresolved. 

3 Contribution 

We summarize the contribution of our work in this section. • We propose FedCALM-D (Fed erated Compressed And Local Muon — Deterministic), a novel scheme that integrates both compression and local steps into Muon -like LMO updates in the deterministic FL setting. This requires more than a direct application of existing tools: we adapt the compression mechanism to the structure of the LMO update. • We extend the scheme to the stochastic setting and obtain our main algorithm, FedCALM .• We establish rigorous convergence guarantees for both methods in the non-convex setting under mild assump-tions. Crucially, our analysis is heterogeneity-free. • We validate our method on an image-classification benchmark ( ResNet-18 on CIFAR-10 ) and further on a language task ( LoRA fine-tuning of RoBERTa on the GLUE benchmark), confirming its practical advantages across modalities. In terms of communicated bits, FedCALM outperforms all baselines in both convergence speed and final accuracy.

Communication. Finally, we introduce two notions of communication efficiency. 

Definition 2 

The number of communication rounds (round complexity) is the number of times the server communicates with the clients. This notion disregards which clients take part in a round and the sizes of the transmitted messages. 

Definition 3 

Communication time (time complexity) is the total time of communication with all clients, assuming their channels are of equal quality. Unless stated otherwise, we adopt Definition 3 as our measure of communication complexity, since it captures the practical advantages of compression: com-pression reduces the transmission time of each round, which in turn accelerates convergence. Since we assume all channels are equal, communication time is proportional to the total number of transmitted bits; we therefore report communicated bits in our experiments as a direct proxy for time complexity. 

4.2 Motivation 

This section explains the design choices of our algorithm. First, we consider the deterministic setting. 

Local drift. As discussed in Section 2, current federated methods with LMO updates lack one of two FL techniques: compression [51] or local steps [52, 53]. We aim to implement both in the same scheme. Since we assume no heterogeneity bound, local steps create client drift and spoil the convergence [42, Figure 1]. To overcome this, we consider control variates of SCAFFOLD .

A full, uncompressed gradient communication is required at this stage, but not at any other point; this one-time transmission does not affect the convergence rate. 5 Experiments 

Setup. The task at hand is image classification on the CIFAR-10 dataset [57]. The chosen architecture is ResNet-18 [58] – the standard benchmark for optimization problems [59]. We examine the following federated setting. The training data is split among M = 10 clients with two different distributions. For more details, refer to the Appendix A. 

Baselines. We compare our approach to the competitors discussed in Section 2: SCAFCOM [43], FedMuon [51], and 

EF21-Muon [52]. We also implement LocalSGD [60] as a simple FL benchmark without compression. 

Compression. We choose TopK% sparsification as a compression operator. This means the output vector retains a fixed fraction of entries with the largest absolute values. Top1% and Top10% compression rates are explored in this study. Top100% compression means communicating the full message, and is implmented for methods that do not account for compression ( FedMuon , LocalSGD ).

5.2 Network load 

Moreover, we perform a careful study of network load and observe the theoretical advantages of Algorithm 2 in terms of communication uplink. The results are described in Appendix A.2. 10 6 Limitations 

First, we rely on smoothness of the objective. In practice, it is fulfilled only locally, however, it is classic in the field. Second, the scope of our empirical validation is limited, and the future work can extend the scale of the experiments. Lastly, we consider all clients to be available at every communication round, while this may not be the case in real-world scenarios. 

7 Conclusion 

In this paper, we address the communication bottleneck of FL by proposing a novel spectral method, FedCALM-D 

, which utilizes two key techniques of FL: local steps and communication compression. Crucially, we manage to perform two separate communications with proper error compensation mechanisms for both. The scheme provably converges with arbitrary heterogeneity in the deterministic case. Furthermore, we expand our approach with FedCALM – the stochastic version of Algorithm 1. We obtain heterogeneity-free convergence bounds for FedCALM , asymptotically matching the classic rates of SCAFCOM in the spectral setting. We perform experimental validation on computer vision and language processing tasks and demonstrate the em-pirical advantages of FedCALM over standard baselines.

A.4 Experimental Details 

In this section we describe the specifics of our experimental setup. 

GLUE setup. The backbone is RoBERTa-base ( 125 M parameters) with LoRA adapters [62] of rank r = 4 and scaling α = 2 r = 8 , attached to the query, key and value projections and to every dense projection of each of the 12 transformer blocks. Per round, each client performs at most 20 local optimizer steps on minibatches of 32 

sentences, i.e. it sees 640 samples between two communications. The local optimizer is Muon with momentum 0.95 

and 5 Newton–Schulz iterations, applied to the matrix-shaped LoRA factors. Every run lasts 30 communication rounds.
