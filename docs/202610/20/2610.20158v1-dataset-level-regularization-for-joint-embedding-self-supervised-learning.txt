Abstract
Self-supervised joint embedding learning aligns representations of augmented views while
regularizing their distribution to prevent collapse. Regularization aims to shape the overall
representation distribution, but most joint embedding methods restrict its computation to
the active minibatch. We propose out-of-batch regularization, which maintains a dataset-
level state in CPU memory and uses globally constructed per-instance targets during mini-
batch training. Evaluated on natural images and biomedical datasets, these anchors approx-
imate the full-dataset regularization gradient more faithfully than conventional minibatch
estimation at small batch sizes. Applications to contrastive (SimCLR) and non-contrastive
(SIGReg) approaches show consistent improvement over the corresponding in-batch ob-
jectives for image classification, minority-class performance under controlled imbalance,
instance retrieval, and continued self-supervised pretraining, with the largest gains at small
batch sizes. These results suggest that dataset-level regularization can be decoupled from
minibatch optimization, making batch size an optimization choice that does not affect the
geometry of the regularizer.

Introduction
Joint-embedding architectures (JEAs) (Chen et al., 2020a; Grill et al., 2020; Caron et al., 2021; Bardes et al.,
2021) have been the dominant paradigm for visual self-supervised representation learning. Applications
range from 3D medical image analysis (Tang et al., 2022) to 3D scene reconstruction (Wang et al., 2025) and
vision–language alignment (Radford et al., 2021). They use augmentations to create a pretraining objective
based on view invariance. Anti-collapse regularization complements this objective to prevent trivial solutions
(Chen & He, 2021). While different regularization mechanisms have been proposed over the years, most of
them act on the embeddings of the current minibatch (or a few recent batches with EMA) that might be too
small to represent the dataset under memory or optimization constraints.
The bottleneck is particularly relevant for high-dimensional inputs, including high-resolution images and 3D
volumes, where each sample requires more activation memory. Such applications are thus often limited to
small-batch training. The problem worsens for imbalanced data, where rare classes may contribute only a
few examples to each batch (Jing et al., 2021). The simple solution of using a larger batch is constrained by
hardware (Gao et al., 2021), and even when a large batch is possible, it may not be ideal for optimization.
On small datasets, using a large fraction of the training samples per batch leaves few parameter updates
per epoch. Masters & Luschi (2018) also report that larger batches can require more careful choice of the
learning rate and training duration. The batch size needed to estimate a population regularizer can therefore
differ from the best batch size for optimization.
We propose a framework that moves the statistical context of SSL regularizers from the active batch to
the dataset-level state. We maintain a detached, dataset-indexed state in CPU memory. A periodic refresh
encodes the training set and constructs globally optimized targets, while online steps retrieve only the current

Our main contributions are:
• We introduce OOB regularization, where offline, dataset-level targets act as per-instance an-
chors during online training, and derive contrastive (CONTRASTIVE-OOB) and non-contrastive
(SIGREG-OOB) instances of this construction.
• We formulate anti-collapse as estimation of a finite population gradient and measure minibatch error
and cache staleness on 19 real datasets. The analysis also separates gradient-estimation error from
population reweighting under class imbalance.
• Across natural images and 2D/3D biomedical data, the OOB variants yield positive mean gains
over their matched in-batch objectives in image classification evaluated by kNN and linear probes.
The gains are largest at small batch sizes and extend to minority-class recall, instance retrieval, and
continued pretraining.

Related Work
Collapse prevention in joint embedding training. Joint-embedding methods must preserve informative
variation while aligning views. Contrastive objectives achieve this through negative-sample repulsion (Oord
et al., 2018; Chen et al., 2020a), linking alignment to hyperspherical uniformity for normalized representa-
tions (Wang & Isola, 2020). To avoid explicit negatives, Barlow Twins and VICReg constrain correlations
and moments (Zbontar et al., 2021; Bardes et al., 2021), while SIGReg matches projected distributions to a
Gaussian (Balestriero & LeCun, 2025). Other approaches prevent collapse through balanced assignments in
SwAV (Caron et al., 2020) or asymmetry, teachers, and stop-gradient in BYOL, DINO, and SimSiam (Grill
et al., 2020; Caron et al., 2021; Chen & He, 2021). These approaches establish how to prevent collapse, but
they have to assume that the active batch is representative of the dataset.
2


Cross-batch state and global objectives. Reusing representations across updates expands batch context.
MoCo’s momentum-encoder queue (He et al., 2020; Chen et al., 2020b), instance discrimination’s memory
bank (Wu et al., 2018), and cross-batch memory (Wang et al., 2020) expand the pool of negative samples.
NNCLR uses stored embeddings to find positive neighbors (Dwibedi et al., 2021). Chen et al. (2022) argues
that minibatch contrastive gradients can be biased, motivating global-objective optimization through per-
image moving averages in SogCLR (Yuan et al., 2022) and MCMC negative sampling in EMC2 (Yau et al.,
2024). Decoupled Contrastive Learning reduces batch sensitivity by removing positives from the denominator
(Yeh et al., 2022). OOB instead performs population regularization periodically and retrieves per-instance
targets for online attraction, avoiding cross-instance comparisons in the online regularizer.
Assigned targets and dataset-indexed representations. Constructing targets separately from encoder up-
dates also provides broader context. Noise-vector assignment (Bojanowski & Joulin, 2017), dataset-feature
clustering (Caron et al., 2018), and balanced self-labeling through optimal transport (Asano et al., 2019) orga-
nize representation learning around assigned targets. Our anchors instead retain per-instance correspondence
and are reconstructed from current representations under a specified continuous geometry. DIET also uses
sample identity, predicting datum indices as classes (Balestriero, 2023). IConE, the conceptually closest re-
cent approach (Almpanakis & Kreshuk, 2026), separates collapse prevention from encoder alignment through
a jointly optimized N × d table of auxiliary instance embeddings. Its regularization maintains a persistent
global parameter space during training and evaluates an N × N similarity matrix each step. OOB instead
periodically constructs detached per-instance targets from a chosen population-level regularizer, stores these
targets in CPU memory, and retrieves only those required by the active minibatch. This decouples online
training from the global computation while supporting different regularizers.
3
Method
To give small-batch training access to dataset-wide regularization, we construct anti-collapse anchors from
the full dataset and use them to update the encoder. We first formulate this separation, instantiate it with spher-
ical (contrastive) and Gaussian (non-contrastive) anchors, and then define a full-dataset gradient reference for
comparing minibatch estimation with cached regularization.
3.1
Alignment and population regularization

joint-embedding methods directly optimize it. For representations Z1 and Z2 of related views, the mutual

4.1
Datasets
We experiment on natural and scientific/biomedical images: CIFAR-10/100 (Krizhevsky et al., 2009), DTD
(Cimpoi et al., 2014), STL-10 (Coates et al., 2011), ImageNet-100, Imagenette, and Imagewoof (Deng et al.,

4.2
Oracle gradient analysis
Using the fixed objective from section 3.5, we compare minibatch sampling error with cache staleness at train-
ing checkpoints. Figure 2A–B shows that limited batch context can change the direction of regularization and
can produce gradients that oppose the full-dataset objective. As batch size gets bigger, its regularization gra-
dient agrees more with the oracle. A fresh cache recovers the oracle exactly, but its agreement deteriorates
as the encoder changes (Figure 2C–D). This deterioration is slower at later checkpoints, where cached gradi-
ents remain informative across more online updates. The results confirm that minibatch and cached estimators
face different limitations: incomplete population context versus temporal staleness. When training with small
batches, periodic dataset-level regularization is clearly beneficial, while the update schedule should depend
on the training stage.

4.3
Image classification across batch sizes
Evaluation protocol. We compare each OOB method with its corresponding baseline: SIGREG-OOB with
SIGREG, and CONTRASTIVE-OOB with SimCLR and MoCoV2. Within a comparison, we keep the data
split, augmentations, number of views, backbone, projector, optimizer, learning rate schedule, and evaluation
fixed. The 2D experiments use a ViT backbone (Dosovitskiy et al., 2020); 3D experiments use a 3D ResNet
(He et al., 2016) and training lasts 100 epochs. Frozen representations are evaluated by k-NN and linear
probe (Wu et al., 2018; Chen et al., 2020a). We use three independent seeds. In-batch baselines are omitted at
B = 1, because anti-collapse regularization based on a single-sample batch is either undefined or statistically
uninformative. OOB methods encode dataset-level state and remain well defined. Training details are in
section A.2.
Results. The low-batch regime shows the clearest separation (Figure 3). Averaged over small batch sizes,
SIGREG-OOB improves 5-NN/linear probe performance over SIGREG by 14.17/12.89pp on natural im-
ages, while CONTRASTIVE-OOB improves over SimCLR by 10.83/6.58pp on biomedical data. On 3D
volumes, both OOB methods retain positive mean gains, showing that the effect is not tied to a 2D encoder.

Population weighting and minority classes under imbalance
Oracle diagnostic. Class-balanced reference eq. (14) is used to distinguish estimator error from the choice
of population weighting. The exact empirical gradient for minority probes diverges from the class-balanced
gradient as minority classes are downsampled (fig. 4). A cached empirical estimator tracks the empirical
objective closely and a cache built with class-balanced targets tracks the class-balanced oracle. OOB state
solves the estimation problem, but not class imbalance. When class information is available, population
weighting can be used as a hyperparameter.

Conclusion and Future Work
We introduced out-of-batch (OOB) regularization, which decouples population-level representation regu-
larization from minibatch optimization through periodically constructed dataset-level targets. Across con-
trastive and non-contrastive objectives, OOB more faithfully recovers the full-dataset regularization signal
9


and improves representation quality, particularly in cases where small minibatches poorly approximate the
full population. Our results show that the active minibatch does not need to define the population over which
representation geometry is regularized.
OOB replaces minibatch sampling error with a different trade-off: dataset-level state must be stored and peri-
odically refreshed as the encoder evolves. Understanding this trade-off at larger scales remains an important
direction. More broadly, our results motivate separating globally coupled representation objectives from the
minibatch updates used to train the encoder, with regularizer-specific constructions providing a possible route
to population-aware learning beyond the objectives studied here.

A.4
Ablations of the SIGReg-OOB configuration
We ablate the components of SIGReg-OOB on seven 2D natural/scientific domains and batches 1–64. The
study comprises 1,792 evaluations from 39 configurations over 48 dataset–batch matched experiments, cover-
ing projection resampling, refresh period, the alignment and anti-collapse weights, and the number of projec-
tions. Since the sweep is not a full Cartesian product, we form controlled slices in which one factor changes
while the remaining settings are held fixed. Within each slice, we retain only dataset–batch experiments
shared by every variant.
The resampling, refresh-period, and alignment-weight ablations use the common configuration p = 0.5,
λalign = 5, λac = 1, and M = 256, omitting the factor being varied. The anti-collapse-weight slice fixes
(p, λalign, M) = (2, 25, 512), and the projection-count slice fixes (p, λalign, λac) = (2, 25, 5); projection
resampling is enabled in both.
Figure 8 shows mean downstream linear-probe balanced accuracy. Projection resampling produces the largest
improvement. A refresh period of 0.5 epoch performs best, while one epoch remains close and overly frequent
refreshes are not beneficial. The intermediate alignment weight is preferable to either extreme, and increasing
the anti-collapse weight or the number of projections does not improve the matched slices. These results
indicate that maintaining diverse, periodically updated targets and balancing the two loss terms matter more
than increasing the raw number of projections.

The held-out evaluation confirms that the transductive improvements are not solely memorization of the

views for 100 epochs. The backbone and projector learning rates are 10−5 and 10−4, respectively; weight
decay is 0.05 and the gradient norm is clipped at 1. OOB refreshes occur every 0.5 epoch. Refresh batch sizes
for ViT-S/B/L/G are 128, 64, 16, and 4 to accommodate their different activation footprints. Experiments use
an NVIDIA A100 GPU.

G
Computational measurements
We benchmark each method on one NVIDIA L40S GPU for 100 epochs (Figure 16). The five-method
benchmark spans BloodMNIST at 500, 1k, 2k, and 5k training examples and STL-10. We evaluate batches
1–64 where each method is defined. Peak allocated GPU memory is measured during training; wall time
includes refresh passes and anchor construction.
At a matched batch size, SIGReg-OOB and CONTRASTIVE-OOB have essentially the same peak allocated
GPU memory as their direct SIGReg and SimCLR counterparts. They add approximately 10% and 4% wall
time, respectively, due to periodic cache construction.
