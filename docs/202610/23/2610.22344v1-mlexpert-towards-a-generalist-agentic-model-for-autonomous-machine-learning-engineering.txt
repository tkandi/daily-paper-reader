Large language models (LLMs) are expanding from assisting scientific research and engineering
to autonomously developing and improving AI systems, advancing AI for AI (AI4AI) as a pathway
toward recursive self-improvement (RSI). Workflow-driven machine learning engineering (MLE)
systems provide a practical path toward this pursuit through complex orchestration that manages
long-horizon experimentation, while recent MLE systems adopt model-directed interaction, allowing
models to autonomously organize experiments and adapt optimization strategies based on execution
feedback. However, feedback from each experiment reflects several preceding decisions, and a long
MLE run contains many such costly experiments. Training must therefore select useful supervision
from long interaction trajectories and learn from intermediate outcomes as they become available.
To address these challenges, we propose a post-training method for autonomous long-horizon MLE
optimization and apply it to train MLExpert, a 35B Mixture-of-Experts agentic model. We design
the Kaggle-MLE Factory to construct executable tasks and an interaction harness for model-directed
experimentation. Hierarchical agentic SFT transfers general tool-use and coding capabilities to
MLE through outcome-guided supervision. Windowed Interaction RL (WIRL) treats the multi-turn
decisions between successive experiment results as an execution window and assigns it a shared
outcome-based reward. Asynchronous collection and policy correction support policy updates
before the trajectory ends. On MLE-Bench Lite, MLExpert improves the average medal rate of the
base model from 37.9% to 72.7%, achieving performance comparable to larger frontier models
such as Kimi-K3 and GLM-5.3. Further experiments show that MLExpert improves performance on
NatureBench Lite, agent scaffold optimization, and algorithm optimization, suggesting that the
learned optimization capability generalizes to other tasks.

1. Introduction
The rapid advancement of reasoning, code generation, and tool interaction in large language models
(LLMs) is enabling artificial intelligence (AI) to move beyond assisting scientific research and engi-
neering toward autonomously developing and improving AI systems. These efforts include model
training, optimization of solver programs, and algorithm design, making AI for AI (AI4AI) a research
direction toward recursive self-improvement (RSI). In this context, machine learning engineering
(MLE) provides a concrete setting for this goal, requiring long-horizon optimization through repeated
execution, feedback analysis, and revision rather than one-shot generation.
Most existing MLE systems [1, 2, 3] extend code generation into iterative optimization through search,
memory, or multi-agent collaboration. As shown in Figure 1(a), predefined workflows or search rules
typically control experiment sequencing and stage transitions, while LLMs primarily generate or
debug code at designated steps. These systems rely heavily on manually designed control logic to

Recently, Arbor [4] and Argus [5] maintain persistent research states and allow models to design,
continue, and revise experiments, placing greater demands on the model’s ability to plan experiments
and sustain optimization over long interaction horizons. ML-Agent [6] combines multi-turn demon-
strations with single-step RL, while Frontis-MA1 [7] trains program evolution operators through
SFT and RL and integrates them into an external evolutionary search framework. These approaches
primarily train models for single-step decisions or local operations within externally organized pro-
cesses. In contrast, we study how to train models to organize experiments and improve solutions
over sustained interaction, using experience and execution feedback from real-world MLE tasks.
However, training this long-horizon optimization capability poses three challenges. 1) Open-ended
MLE requires an interaction environment that preserves experimental states while leaving action
selection to the model. 2) High-quality long-horizon supervision is difficult to obtain because MLE
tasks are heterogeneous and interaction trajectories may mix useful exploration and failure recovery
with degraded decisions. 3) Each costly experiment returns feedback on several preceding decisions
before the full run ends; online RL must use this intermediate signal while handling policy mismatch
during asynchronous collection.
To address these challenges, we propose a post-training method for autonomous MLE that combines a
model-directed long-horizon interaction harness, hierarchical agentic SFT, and Windowed Interaction
Reinforcement Learning (WIRL). Based on this, we train MLExpert, a 35B Mixture-of-Experts (MoE)
agentic model, to learn long-horizon optimization strategies. As shown in Figure 1(b), MLExpert
uses sustained tool interaction within the interaction harness to autonomously organize experiments,
revise solutions based on execution feedback, and maintain submission candidates. We build the
Kaggle-MLE Factory to convert historical Kaggle competitions into executable MLE tasks. Hierarchical
agentic SFT transfers general tool-use and coding capabilities to MLE optimization, with execution
outcomes guiding the selection of trajectories and segments for supervision. To use this intermediate
feedback, WIRL treats each completed experiment as the boundary of an execution window containing
decisions since the previous result. It trains those decisions with a shared outcome-based reward, while
asynchronous collection and policy correction allow updates as trajectories continue. On MLE-Bench
Lite, MLExpert improves the average medal rate of the base model from 37.9% to 72.7%, achieving
performance comparable to larger models such as Kimi-K3 and GLM-5.3. MLExpert also improves
performance on NatureBench Lite and transfers its optimization capabilities to agent scaffold and
algorithm optimization tasks. Our key contributions are:
• We introduce MLExpert, a 35B MoE model for autonomous MLE, trained with hierarchical agentic

• We build the Kaggle-MLE Factory and an Interaction Harness to provide executable tasks and

3.2. Interaction Harness for Long-Horizon MLE Optimization
To support model-directed long-horizon MLE optimization, we design an interaction harness that
follows ReAct-style loop [9], leaving branch selection and optimization-path decisions to the model
rather than an external search policy. The harness stores experimental states in solution nodes,
each containing candidate code, execution logs, and metadata. Parent-linked revisions form an
experiment tree that the model can revisit and compare to decide whether to continue, backtrack,
or change optimization direction. The harness provides tools for solution analysis, code revision,
experiment execution, history retrieval, and submission management. The model launches candidate
execution through execute_code and updates its submission candidate through update_answer.
The complete tool list and descriptions are provided in Appendix B.
As interaction history approaches the context limit, earlier messages are summarized while recent
interactions are retained. For complex analysis, analyze delegates task analysis, data exploration,
and result diagnosis to an analysis sub-agent with independent context, returning only a summary to
the main model. Since overfitting or data leakage can inflate validation scores, the model can call
4


invalidate_node based on experimental evidence and analysis to exclude unreliable candidates.
With new evidence, the model can update its submission candidate while exploring alternatives.
4. Agentic MLE Post-Training
4.1. Hierarchical Agentic SFT
Long-horizon MLE optimization requires general tool-use, coding skills, and domain-specific decision
making. We therefore use two-stage hierarchical agentic SFT. In the first stage, coding and tool-use
trajectories from diverse agentic tasks are used to learn basic tool-use and code revision capabilities.
The second stage adopts teacher trajectories from MLE domain and literature-derived scientific tasks
to improve algorithm design, experimental analysis, and iterative optimization.
Complementary MLE demonstrations. MLE adaptation data cover both task initialization and
sustained optimization. For trajectories whose first complete solution executes successfully and
reaches a quality threshold, we extract the interaction prefix from task start through the corresponding
execution feedback as an initial-solution trajectory, typically covering task understanding, algorithm
design, and code generation. We also use long-horizon optimization trajectories from complete
interactions, covering solution construction, feedback analysis, iterative revision, and final submission
selection. In addition, analysis sub-agent trajectories are adopted to train the model for independent
data analysis and result diagnosis.
Outcome-guided supervision selection. Long-horizon MLE trajectories can contain effective ex-
ploration and failure recovery, but may also include misguided decisions or continued degradation
after reaching a strong solution. Therefore, we use execution results of submitted solutions to select
high-quality trajectories and the segments used for supervision. For trajectories that pass a quality
threshold, we identify the first best-performing submission and measure the subsequent performance
degradation. When the post-peak degradation exceeds a threshold, we use the first best-performing
submission as the anchor for retaining a trajectory prefix; otherwise, we use the last submission. The
prefix ends at a complete tool-interaction boundary near the anchor. This removes degraded tails
while preserving useful exploration and failure recovery before the peak.
Supervision samples and training objective. To preserve multi-turn context dependencies, each
supervision sample combines existing interaction context with a continuous sequence of subsequent
model actions and environment feedback from the selected MLE interactions. When context com-
paction occurs, the retained history and summary form the conditioning context for subsequent
samples. Training loss is applied only to newly generated reasoning, text responses, and tool-call
tokens, while tool feedback and inherited interaction history are used only as conditioning context
and masked out from the loss. Details of outcome-guided filtering, score normalization, and the SFT
objective appear in Appendix C.1.
4.2. Windowed Interaction Reinforcement Learning
Building on SFT, we further optimize the long-horizon decision policy using execution feedback from
the model’s own exploration. In MLE, solution quality is available only after training and evaluation,
and each result depends on multiple rounds of analysis, code revision, and tool interaction. We
introduce Windowed Interaction RL (WIRL), which groups consecutive model decisions made between
successive experiment results into an execution window 𝑢𝑚. Each window is conditioned on the
accumulated interaction history and associated with the outcome of the corresponding experiment
launched through the execute_code tool (Sec. 3.2). The first window starts from the initial task
state. This allows policy updates to use feedback from completed experiments without waiting for
the entire task trajectory to finish.

6. Related work
6.1. Agents for Automated ML Research
LLM agents increasingly automate data analysis, model development, and experimental iteration, with
recent work extending to autonomous AI system design and improvement [27]. AIDE [1] and MLE-
STAR [2] improve candidate solutions through iterative search and refinement, while MLEvolve [28]
and MARS [29] use structured tree- or graph-based search, memory, and planning to organize long-
horizon experimentation. R&D-Agent [3] studies multi-role collaboration, while InternAgent [30]
and FM-Agent [31] extend autonomous AI research across machine learning, algorithm optimization,
and engineering tasks. Recent systems including Arbor [4] and Argus [5] maintain persistent research
states and increase model control.
10


Recent work develops MLE capabilities by training models on interaction experience. ML-Agent [6]
combines multi-turn demonstrations with single-step RL, while RL for MLE Agents [32], MLE-RL [33],
and AceGRPO [34] study online MLE training with execution feedback, asynchronous sampling,
or intermediate trajectory states. Learning to Ideate [35] trains models to generate algorithm
improvement suggestions, whereas Frontis-MA1 [7] applies SFT and RL to program evolution operators
and combines them through an external evolutionary framework. These approaches primarily train
individual interaction steps or local components of externally organized optimization workflows.
SandMLE [36] applies trajectory-wise on-policy RL in micro-scale synthetic MLE environments. We
train model-directed long-horizon interaction policies on real-world MLE tasks, with WIRL using
execution windows to learn multi-turn decisions from delayed execution feedback under asynchronous
updates.
6.2. Post-Training Long-Horizon Agentic Models
Recent research has increasingly focused on post-training agentic models for sustained interaction,
complex tool use, and long-horizon decision making. SWE-Gym [37] trains coding agents using
trajectories collected from executable environments, while Search-R1 [38] and WebRL [39] learn
multi-turn tool use from environment feedback and outcome rewards. AgentFlow [40] directly
optimizes planning policies in general multi-turn agentic interaction and uses trajectory-level outcomes
to train intermediate decisions. Recent work has also explored generalist agentic models for long-
horizon interaction. Agents-A1 [41] uses long-horizon knowledge-action trajectories and multi-stage
training, together with multi-teacher on-policy distillation (OPD), to combine capabilities from
multiple domain teachers into a unified model. Ornith-1.5 [14] extends self-scaffolding by jointly
optimizing task generation, scaffold construction, and solution rollouts through RL.
7. Conclusion
In this work, we introduce MLExpert, a 35B MoE agentic model trained to autonomously conduct and
improve machine learning experiments over long interaction horizons. We build an automated MLE
task factory and a model-directed interaction harness, and train MLExpert with hierarchical agentic
SFT and WIRL to learn from teacher demonstrations and execution feedback collected from its own
exploration. Extensive experiments show that MLExpert improves over its base model on MLE-Bench
Lite and achieves performance comparable to larger frontier models. Further improvements on
NatureBench Lite and in agent scaffold and algorithm optimization demonstrate cross-task gener-
alization. These results support treating long-horizon optimization decision making as a trainable
model capability, providing a practical path toward autonomous machine learning engineering and
scientific experimentation.

A. Details of the Kaggle-MLE Factory
We begin with a Meta-Kaggle1 competition metadata snapshot containing 11,649 entries. Guided by
the task-selection principles of MLE-bench [8], the discovery agent considers task relevance, data
availability, and the feasibility of offline evaluation. Automated initial screening uses competition
completion, at least 50 participating teams, and a dataset size limit of 200 GB as default criteria,
checks submission-interface compatibility, and consults an exclusion list of existing tasks. The agent
further selects candidates across domains and data modalities, including vision, text, audio, tabular
data, and time series. Candidates are collected and processed in multiple batches.
The downloader and scraper retrieve competition data, official descriptions, and leaderboard infor-
mation. The analyzer agent extracts data structures, field information, and modality characteristics
to form a task specification, which the generator agent uses to produce data-preparation and scoring
code. When test labels are unavailable, the pipeline constructs a held-out split from the original
labeled data and keeps its labels on the evaluation side. The executor runs generated task packages
on real data, while the validator agent checks structural completeness, submission-format consistency,
non-degenerate labels, train–test identifier overlap, and data availability. The validator also executes
the scoring function to check that it produces a valid score. Execution and validation errors are
returned to the construction agents for repair. We conduct a final human review of the 160 registered
tasks, excluding six with unresolved label or scoring-implementation issues and retaining 154 tasks
for subsequent trajectory collection.

D.3. AIME Agent Scaffold Optimization
The outer model optimizes a Python agent scaffold that calls a fixed gpt-4.1-nano-2025-04-14
solver. All optimizers start from the same single-query scaffold and share the task environment, tools,
and evaluator. Each search has a four-hour time limit and a maximum of 1,000 interaction steps,
with a context limit of 200,000 tokens and an output limit of 65,536 tokens per step. The scaffold
can modify prompting, calling, verification, voting, and answer-extraction procedures, with at most
ten inner-model calls per problem. Initial uses one solver call, while Majority@5 takes a majority
vote over five independently sampled answers.
Development uses all 30 problems from AIME 2025, with three evaluations per problem for each
candidate scaffold. The outer model receives aggregate accuracy, call counts, and sanitized error
summaries. Exact-request response caching is used during development to reuse inner-solver responses
to identical requests. After search, the selected scaffold is frozen and evaluated using fresh solver
calls on AIME 2023, 2024, and 2026. Each held-out year contains 30 problems and is evaluated three
times; these years provide no feedback for scaffold development or selection. Thus, each optimizer
contributes one frozen scaffold, with three evaluation repetitions per year.
Accuracy is the fraction of answers that exactly match the reference integer answer; missing or
incorrectly formatted answers count as incorrect. Table 11 reports development accuracy, accuracy
for each held-out year, the mean across the three held-out years, and mean inner-model calls per
problem on the held-out evaluations. Each annual accuracy averages the three evaluations of that
year’s 30 problems.
Solver-call efficiency after SFT. The scaffold optimized by MLExpert-SFT achieves 38.1% held-
out accuracy with 3.51 solver calls per problem, approaching the 40.0% achieved by Kimi-K3 with
approximately 59% fewer calls. It also outperforms Majority@5, which reaches 24.8% with five calls
per problem. MLExpert-SFT therefore improves held-out accuracy over fixed majority voting while
using fewer solver calls, suggesting that its optimized scaffold uses the solver more effectively. These
results indicate that SFT already supports effective scaffold optimization across held-out years.
Further gains from WIRL. WIRL raises held-out accuracy from 38.1% to 43.7%, a gain of 5.6
percentage points, with improvements on all three held-out years. The resulting scaffold uses 8.40
calls per problem, compared with 3.51 for MLExpert-SFT and 8.65 for Kimi-K3. With a similar number
of calls to Kimi-K3, MLExpert exceeds its 40.0% accuracy, while GLM-5.3 achieves 48.1% with 9.39
calls. The SFT and RL results therefore offer different accuracy–call trade-offs: SFT yields an effective
scaffold with fewer calls, while further RL finds a more accurate scaffold that uses more calls.
