Asynchronous On-Policy Self-Distillation
Xidong Wu 1, Qi Qi 2, Yuqiong Ji3, Shangqian Gao4
1University of Pittsburgh, 2University of Iowa, 3University of Pennsylvania, 4Florida State University
Abstract
Unlike on-policy distillation (OPD), which de-
pends on a separate teacher model, on-policy
self-distillation (OPSD) leverages a single lan-
guage model acting simultaneously as the stu-
dent and the teacher, with the teacher condi-
tioned on privileged information. Despite this
architectural simplicity, synchronous rollout
generation dominates overall training time, cre-
ating a severe systems bottleneck when scaling
OPSD. To address this, we introduce Asyn-
chronous On-Policy Self-Distillation (Asyn-
cOPSD), a framework that decouples roll-
out generation from learner updates to im-
prove training throughput. Since the teacher
and student share parameters, AsyncOPSD
eliminates the multi-model coordination over-
head inherent to OPD architectures in asyn-
chronous training.
As the student policy
evolves, buffered trajectories generated by
older policies become stale, potentially degrad-
ing performance. To mitigate this, we introduce
a distillation objective based on staleness-aware
policy control and token-level entropy weight-
ing. Across three model sizes and mathematical
reasoning benchmarks, AsyncOPSD improves
the training throughput by 1.96–2.25× over
synchronous OPSD while increasing average
benchmark accuracy.

Introduction
On-policy distillation (OPD) has been used as an
effective approach in the large language model
(LLM) post-training (Yang et al., 2025; Zeng et al.,
2026; Gu et al., 2024). A student is trained on its
own generated on-policy responses using token-
level feedback from a teacher. Training on student-
generated prefixes mitigates the train-test distribu-
tion shift inherent in distilling from fixed trajecto-
ries (Agarwal et al., 2024). OPD typically uses a
separate large teacher model while On-policy self-
distillation (OPSD) instead instantiates both roles
from a single language model. The privileged in-
formation, including a verified solution reasoning
trace or feedback, augments the teacher’s context,
while the student receives only the question. Match-
ing their token-level distributions transfers the ben-
efit of this additional context to the student without
requiring an external teacher (Zhao et al., 2026).
Despite this simplification, large-scale OPSD
and OPD inherit the on-policy rollout bottleneck.
Long reasoning traces dominate training time,
and strict synchronization forces the learner to
wait for new rollouts, leaving compute resources
idle. While asynchronous execution improves uti-
lization by generating rollouts and updating the
learner concurrently, buffered trajectories quickly
become stale.
Training on this outdated data
introduces a distribution mismatch and creates
a performance–efficiency trade-off (Kang et al.,
2026).
Recent work explores this trade-off in
asynchronous OPD (Wang et al., 2026; Rang
et al., 2026; Kang et al., 2026).
These stud-
ies highlight the importance of designing asyn-
chronous pipelines. We study asynchronous ex-
ecution for OPSD by overlapping rollout genera-
tion and learner-side privileged-context distillation
under a fixed GPU budget.
We introduce Asynchronous On-Policy Self-
Distillation (AsyncOPSD), a framework that decou-
ples rollout generation from learner updates. Learn-
ers update model weights asynchronously from a
shared replay buffer and periodically export the cur-
rent policy (model weights), and distributed rollout
actors generate trajectories after synchronizing the
refreshed policy. At each update, learners evalu-
ate buffered trajectories under both the student and
a privileged teacher context. Computing teacher
supervision on the learner side eliminates teacher-
scoring stages and the communication overhead of
transmitting cached teacher distributions, substan-
tially simplifying system coordination compared to
AsyncOPD (Kang et al., 2026).
However, buffered sequences inevitably reflect
the behavioral distribution of older policies. Our

Related Work
2.1
On-Policy and Self-Distillation
On-policy distillation (OPD) reduces the mismatch
between training and inference by supervising
trajectories sampled from the student (Agarwal
et al., 2024; Gu et al., 2023; Lu and Lab, 2025;
Ko et al., 2024; Yang et al., 2026).
Per-token
KL-divergence feedback from a teacher model
in the OPD also offers a dense learning signal
at every step to reinforce sub-optimal reasoning.
Recent studies optimize transfer efficiency via
thinking-pattern compatibility (Li et al., 2026c),
teacher-entropy regularization (Jin et al., 2026),
and uncertainty-disagreement token filtering (Xu
et al., 2026). Other OPD efficiency methods adapt
rollout horizons (Tan et al., 2026) or combine
rollout-depth control with turn-normalized supervi-
sion (Zhou et al., 2026). Self-distillation extends
self-taught reasoning paradigms (Zelikman et al.,
2022) by removing external teachers entirely, ex-
tracting supervision from self-generated reasoning
2


traces (Zhao et al., 2026), environment execution
feedback (Hübotter et al., 2026), hybrid reward-
distillation objectives on failed rollouts (Li et al.,
2026a), or majority-vote pseudo-solutions (Li et al.,
2026d). POPO studies positive-only policy opti-
mization with implicit negative gradients (Xu and
Fang, 2026). Recent work examines reference-
induced shortcuts (Shen et al., 2026), reasoning
degradation (Kaur et al., 2026), reduced output di-
versity (Nicolicioiu et al., 2026), and sensitivity
to reasoning modes and teacher prefixes (Li et al.,
2026b) in OPSD. Broader OP(S)D diagnostics iden-
tify supervision mismatch and objective-dependent
instability (Zhu et al., 2026).
2.2
Asynchronous Reinforcement Learning
Decoupling rollout generation from policy opti-
mization provides the foundation for scalable dis-
tributed RL (Mnih et al., 2016; Espeholt et al.,
2018). In LLM post-training, asynchronous archi-
tectures maximize accelerator utilization through
pipeline overlapping (Noukhovitch et al., 2025),
worker load balancing (Fu et al., 2026), disaggre-
gated streaming generation (Zhong et al., 2025),
and dynamic trajectory repacking (Sheng et al.,
2026). To mitigate distribution mismatch caused by
policy lag, algorithmic solutions employ proximal
interpolation (Li et al., 2025) and second-moment
importance-weight bounding (Zheng et al., 2026).
However, while RL relies on scalar feedback, distil-
lation depends on dense prefix-conditioned teacher
distributions, creating distinct staleness dynamics.
2.3
Asynchronous On-Policy Distillation
Asynchronous OPD introduces coupled systems
and distribution bottlenecks when integrating
teacher evaluation into concurrent pipelines. Sys-
tems frameworks address these bottlenecks via
pipelined scheduling (Sheng et al., 2025) and dis-
tributed hidden-state streaming to reduce vocabu-
lary communication (Zhang et al., 2026). Algorith-
mically, AsyncOPD (Kang et al., 2026) analyzes
staleness under cached teacher scores, demonstrat-
ing divergence sensitivities and the inherent lim-
its of action reweighting. Extending this, f-OPD
(Chen et al., 2026) separates policy drift from super-
vision drift using freshness weighting and adaptive
refreshes and DivOPD (Wang et al., 2026) priori-
tizes high-disagreement turns across stale queues.
However,
asynchronous
on-policy
self-
distillation is still underexplored. To address this
gap, we introduce AsyncOPSD, which overlaps
rollout generation and learner updates under
a fixed GPU budget and simplifies distributed
coordination by recomputing privileged teacher
supervision directly within the learner.

AsyncOPSD: Framework, Analysis,
and Method
4.1
Asynchronous Training Framework
Extending On-Policy Self-Distillation (OPSD), we
implement a baseline asynchronous variant (Asyn-
3


cOPSD) by decoupling trajectory rollout genera-
tion from policy optimization across disjoint GPU
pools. The GPU cluster is partitioned into the
learner pool and the rollout actor pool. The learn-
ers execute forward passes, compute gradients, and
optimize the model with the batch in the queue
from rollout actors and periodically export the cur-
rent policy (model weights). Concurrently, in the
rollout actor pool, distributed rollout actors host in-
dependent, single-GPU vLLM instances to sample
after loading the refreshed policy, pushing gener-
ated trajectories into a shared rollout queue.
Consistent with standard OPSD, no auxiliary
teacher model is required; the student is optimized
using a clipped forward-KL distillation objective.
Model export frequency is governed by sync_step,
enabling generation and gradient computation to
overlap seamlessly at the cost of bounded policy
staleness. To prevent memory overload and control
queue delays, the system limits queue size based
on learner microbatches.
4.2

degradation to student rollout staleness and the
privileged teacher entropy. We first analyze the
evolving shared-teacher formulation, in which priv-

rent parameters θk. For the fair comparison in
Section 5.1, we freeze the teacher policy to be the
initial policy following the OPSD protocol (Zhao
et al., 2026). Appendix A.4 establishes the same
form of staleness–entropy bound.

Discussion.
The bound motivates two comple-
mentary controls on prefix staleness and teacher
entropy. The required signals are already avail-
able during training. We use the policy-version gap
k−r as an inexpensive proxy for prefix-distribution
staleness, while teacher entropy is available from
the existing teacher forward pass. Their joint use
therefore requires no additional forward pass.
4.3
Staleness Control and Entropy Weighting
Staleness-Aware Policy Control.
Let k be the
current learner optimizer step and ri the step of
the published policy that generated sample i. Its
staleness is si = max{0, k−ri}. We reject rollouts
with a staleness si greater than K using the filter:
gK(τi) = 1{si ≤K}
where 1{·} is the indicator function. The learner
rejects staleness above K when claiming a queued
rollout batch, fetching another batch until an ad-
missible one is available. Generation and learning
continue asynchronously. We set K = 6 based on
the mean staleness in Table 1 and an ablation study
in Section 5.6. Policy version gap is an inexpensive
proxy for distribution drift.

5.2
Policy Export Frequency
The sync_step specifies the learner policy export
frequency and indirectly changes the mean stale-
ness on the learner side. We compare sync_step ∈
{1, 2, 4, 8} at a fixed 4/4 learner/actor allocation
and effective batch 32, with policy control and en-
tropy weighting disabled.
Table 1 shows the trade-off between through-
put and staleness. Mean accepted-rollout stale-
ness increases monotonically with sync_step for
each model, whereas throughput does not. Chang-
ing sync_step from two to four increases mean
throughput by only 2.0% and 1.6% for 1.7B and 4B
and reduces it by 1.1% for 8B; mean staleness in-
creases by 13.6%, 17.0%, and 17.5%, respectively.
To get a high throughput without excessively sac-
rificing staleness, we select sync_step = 2 as a
common throughput–freshness compromise in all
experiments later.
5.3
Vanilla Asynchronous OPSD
Vanilla AsyncOPSD decouples generation from op-
timization. Actors generate with refreshed student
policies and place rollouts in a shared queue; learn-
ers consume them, perform model training, and
periodically export the policy. Only student LoRA
adapters are published, avoiding full-model reloads
on actors.
Table 2 reports the complete accuracy compar-
ison, including the controlled variants discussed
next. Vanilla asynchronous execution reduces the
cross-benchmark average from 43.43 to 42.97 on
1.7B, from 63.60 to 62.30 on 4B, and from 64.80
to 64.23 on 8B. The decline is largest on 4B; it is
not uniform across individual benchmarks. These
results motivate controlling the optimization mis-
match introduced by stale rollouts. The analysis in
4.2 provides a complementary motivation by iden-
tifying how prefix-distribution drift and teacher
entropy interact in the objective discrepancy.

Training Throughput
Synchronous baseline.
To get a stronger base-
line, we decouple synchronous generation batch
size from training microbatch size to avoid unnec-
essarily restricting inference throughput. All three
synchronous models generate 4 prompts per GPU
per vLLM call. For 1.7B and 4B, the training mi-
crobatch is also 4, with one gradient-accumulation
step. For 8B, we retain a training microbatch of 2
and 2 gradient-accumulation steps, while generat-
ing both microbatches together. This reduces the
number of generation calls per GPU per optimizer
update for 8B model from two to one without in-
creasing the forward/backward microbatch. The
generated responses are consumed within the same
optimizer update, preserving synchronous execu-
tion and an effective global batch of 32.

Sources of throughput improvement.
The asyn-
chronous implementation combines several sys-
tems changes. 1) Larger generation batches: Each
rollout actor generates 32 completions per vLLM
call, compared with four completions per GPU
in the synchronous baseline.
Larger batches
can improve inference efficiency through higher
GPU utilization. 2) Lower policy-refresh over-
head. The synchronous implementation refreshes
vLLM by merging the LoRA adapters every step.
The asynchronous implementation instead exports
only the updated LoRA adapters at a configurable
sync_step. 3) Nonlinear learner scaling. Dou-
bling the number of learner GPUs does not nec-
essarily halve optimization time. Communication,
synchronization, and small per-device workloads
can limit scaling efficiency. Consequently, split-
ting GPUs into a learner pool and rollout actors
pool can improve overall utilization despite using
fewer GPUs for optimization. 4) Overlapping gen-
eration and optimization. Rollout actors generate
upcoming batches while learners optimize previ-
ously generated samples. This overlap reduces idle
periods associated with alternating generation and
optimization in synchronous execution.

Conclusion
We introduced AsyncOPSD, which improves train-
ing efficiency by decoupling rollout generation
from learner updates. Our analysis bounds the
resulting objective discrepancy in terms of pre-
fix staleness and teacher entropy, motivating stal-
eness control and token-level entropy weighting.
Across three model sizes and mathematical rea-
soning benchmarks, AsyncOPSD achieves 1.96–
2.25× the sample throughput of an optimized syn-
chronous baseline while increasing average bench-
mark accuracy over OPSD by 0.37–1.10 percent-
age points.
8


Limitations
Our experiments use eight NVIDIA A100 GPUs
and models ranging from 1.7B to 8B parameters.
Within this budget, comparisons across three model
sizes and targeted ablations provide evidence for
the throughput benefits of asynchronous execution
and the effectiveness of staleness control and en-
tropy weighting in the evaluated settings. A broader
exploration of model scales and training configura-
tions is left to future work.

ous tokens of a prefix. It is not interchangeable
with policy update lag or a local one-token rollout
KL without an additional argument accounting for
earlier positions. Teacher entropy controls the cur-
rent loss envelope under the contrast assumption;
it does not represent a separate cached-target mis-
match. Because both learner-side predictions use
θk and the privileged reference is fixed, the com-
parison changes only the prefix law. The theorem
therefore concerns the generating population laws,
rather than the empirical distribution of a finite
realized buffer.

Implications and scope.
The same interaction
between prefix staleness and teacher entropy there-
fore holds for a frozen teacher under the stated
contrast bound. In particular, µr = µk implies
∆fr
k = 0, even if the current student differs substan-
tially from the teacher. There is no teacher-version
mismatch on a fixed prefix: cached and recom-
puted frozen-teacher predictions coincide when the
privileged context and evaluation settings are un-
changed. Low teacher entropy alone cannot en-
sure a small distillation loss without a uniform
bound on the teacher–student logit contrast: for
qϵ = (1 −ϵ, ϵ) and p = (1/2, 1/2), H(qϵ) →0

then diverges.
