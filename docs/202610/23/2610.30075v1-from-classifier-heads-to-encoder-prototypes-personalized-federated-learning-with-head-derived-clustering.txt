Abstract
Personalized federated learning must decide which clients should share representations. We
introduce HDC-FL (Head-Derived Clustering for Personalized Federated Learning), which reads
that decision off a signal shared-encoder training already produces: the client-specific classifier
heads. At periodic refresh rounds the server projects the cached heads into a normalized low-
rank descriptor space, groups the descriptors, builds one encoder prototype per group from the
cached client encoders, and returns to each client a soft mixture of those prototypes paired with
its own unchanged head. Client-specific heads are never averaged or distributed to other clients;
the shared-encoder trajectory is left intact; and no second optimization trajectory, additional lo-
cal objective, or post-training fine-tuning stage is required. Across 11 CIFAR-10 and CIFAR-100
configurations covering label-subset, Dirichlet and rotation heterogeneity with ResNet-18 and
MobileNetV2 encoders, HDC-FL is competitive with the strongest personalized and clustered
baselines in macro-averaged mean accuracy and attains the numerically highest macro-averaged
worst-client and bottom-20% accuracy among the evaluated methods. The tail results distin-
guish methods that look similar when only their means are compared. A controlled donor-swap
diagnostic further finds positive donor-ranking associations between head similarity and encoder
compatibility in all three settings tested, most clearly under covariate shift.

Introduction
Federated learning (FL) trains a model from decentralized data without transferring clients’ raw
examples to a central server (McMahan et al., 2017; Konečný et al., 2016). In practice, however,
client data are rarely independent and identically distributed. Clients may observe different label
frequencies, subsets of classes, acquisition devices, or environmental conditions (Kairouz et al., 2021;
Zhao et al., 2018; Hsu et al., 2019; Li et al., 2020). A model that performs well for the population
average can therefore serve some clients poorly. This failure is easily obscured when models are
evaluated solely by mean accuracy, which can mask severe degradation for the worst-performing
clients. Personalization has also been studied as a route to more uniform performance across clients
(Li et al., 2021a). Personalized FL must consequently be judged not only by average performance,
but also by how well it serves the clients that benefit least from uniform collaboration.

Personalized FL addresses this problem by allowing selected model components to vary across
clients. A common decomposition shares an encoder while retaining a private classifier head for each
client (Arivazhagan et al., 2019; Collins et al., 2021; Oh et al., 2022). Although the private head
adapts the decision boundary to local data, every client still relies on the same representation. This
can be limiting under non-IID data: clients with different label support or acquisition conditions
may require different features even when their classifiers are trained independently. Personalizing
the encoder can reduce this form of negative transfer, but only if the server can determine which
clients should share representation information.
Existing clustered and similarity-based methods attempt to infer such relationships, but their
affinity signals may vary with individual local updates, require additional client computation or
communication, or impose a hard assignment to one group. The central challenge is to obtain a
stable client-relatedness signal from information already produced during training and to use it for
graded representation sharing without maintaining separate optimization trajectories for different
groups.
We use the geometry of client-specific classifier heads for this purpose. All client-specific heads
operate on features produced along the same shared-encoder trajectory and use the same class
ordering. The rows of a linear head can therefore be interpreted as client-specific class directions in a
common feature space. Under label-subset skew, their arrangement reflects which class distinctions
are supported by a client’s data. Under covariate shift, such as image rotation, the label frequencies
may remain similar while the class directions move because clients use the shared features differently.
Head geometry thus provides a model-side description of the local prediction problem without
requiring an additional data summary or local objective.
We introduce Head-Derived Clustering for Personalized Federated Learning (HDC-FL). At peri-
odic refresh rounds, the server projects cached classifier heads into a normalized low-rank space and
groups clients using the resulting descriptors. It then constructs prototype encoders from cached
client encoders and gives each client a soft mixture of those prototypes. The personalized encoder
is paired with the client’s unchanged private head and is used for evaluation or deployment; it is
not fed back into later federated updates. HDC-FL therefore personalizes representations while re-
taining one shared-encoder training trajectory. It neither averages client-specific heads nor requires
a second local training stage or independently optimized cluster models.
We evaluate HDC-FL on CIFAR-10 and CIFAR-100 across 11 configurations covering label-
subset, Dirichlet, and rotation heterogeneity with ResNet-18 and MobileNetV2 encoders. HDC-FL
remains competitive with the strongest evaluated personalized and clustered baselines in macro-
averaged mean accuracy, while providing consistently strong worst-client and bottom-20% accuracy.
The tail results distinguish methods that appear similar when only their means are compared.
Our contributions are threefold:
• We identify classifier-head geometry as a client-relatedness signal for representation person-
alization and explain how it can reflect both label and covariate heterogeneity.
• We develop HDC-FL, which converts low-rank head descriptors into soft mixtures of encoder
prototypes without averaging client-specific heads, changing the local objective, or maintain-
ing multiple training trajectories.
• We evaluate average and poorly served clients separately using mean, worst-client, and
bottom-20% accuracy across 11 settings. This evaluation reveals failures hidden by mean
accuracy and shows that HDC-FL combines competitive average performance with consis-
tently strong client-level tails.

Related Work
Personalized federated learning relaxes the requirement that every client use the same global model.
HDC-FL connects two branches of this literature: model decomposition and similarity-based col-
laboration.
Shared representations and client-specific components.
FedPer, FedRep, and FedBABU
decompose the model into a shared feature extractor and client-specific prediction layers (Arivazha-
gan et al., 2019; Collins et al., 2021; Oh et al., 2022). FedBN instead keeps batch normalization local
to address feature shift (Li et al., 2021b), a complementary way to handle covariate heterogene-
ity. Private predictors adapt decision boundaries to local data, but all clients still use one shared
representation. HDC-FL retains this decomposition and uses private-head geometry to determine
which clients should share encoder information.
From hard clustering to soft association.
Clustered FL constructs specialized models for
groups of related clients (Sattler et al., 2021). IFCA, PACFL, and EMD-CFL infer groups from
candidate-model fit, local-data subspaces, and embedding distributions, respectively (Ghosh et al.,
2020; Vahidian et al., 2023; Zhang et al., 2025). A hard assignment is restrictive when a client
can benefit from more than one group. FedSoft, FedCE, and FedAMP instead allow graded or
multi-source collaboration (Ruan and Joe-Wong, 2022; Cai et al., 2023; Huang et al., 2021), and
pFedGraph infers graded pairwise collaboration strengths from model similarity and client data
size (Ye et al., 2023). Some such methods maintain multiple cluster models or use time-varying
model evidence. HDC-FL instead mixes encoder prototypes drawn from one shared optimization
trajectory.
Classifier-geometry-based collaboration.
PFedCS is the closest evaluated method because it
also uses classifier-parameter geometry to identify related clients (Wu et al., 2025). The methods
differ in what they personalize. PFedCS combines classifier parameters from similar clients, and
FedPAC combines feature alignment with classifier collaboration (Xu et al., 2023); both personalize
the decision layer. HDC-FL instead keeps heads private, never aggregating or distributing them
across clients, and uses their geometry only to mix encoder prototypes, so it personalizes the
representation. In HDC-FL an encoder prototype is an averaged vector of encoder parameters;
FedProto instead communicates class-level feature prototypes (Tan et al., 2022).

HDC-FL
HDC-FL adds a server-side personalization step to federated training in which the encoder is ag-
gregated across clients while each client retains a client-specific classifier head. Participating clients
return their locally updated encoder and head, and the server stores the most recent encoder–head
pair available for each client. At a refresh round, the server converts the cached heads into client
descriptors, groups the descriptors, constructs one encoder prototype per group from the corre-
sponding cached encoders, and returns to each client a soft mixture of those prototypes to be used
with its own head (Figure 1). The personalization step does not modify the local objective or
introduce a separate optimization trajectory.

Head-Derived Descriptors
What head geometry encodes.
Within a communication round, participating clients start
from the same broadcast encoder and use the same class ordering. Because local updates in our
setting are short, the resulting client encoders remain close to the shared parameter trajectory. The
coordinates of their associated linear heads are therefore approximately aligned, allowing the rows

Under label-subset skew, the learned head geometry can reflect which class distinctions are

label-support overlap in the label-skew settings. Under covariate shift, label frequencies can remain

Why cached encoders can be averaged.
The encoders in Eq. (6) are not independently
trained networks: each local update starts from the same broadcast encoder θ(t−1) and uses only
one local epoch. Cached encoders therefore remain near a common parameter-space trajectory,
which makes weighted averaging empirically effective in the regime we study. This argument is
specific to short local updates and frequent aggregation; longer local training or heterogeneous
architectures may weaken it, as discussed in Section 5.1.
3.5
Refresh Schedule and Algorithm
Early in training the descriptors still change quickly, so HDC-FL does not personalize from the first
round. The server repeats the refresh every q rounds and enables personalization only once the
grouping has become stable, which it measures with the silhouette score of the current clustering
together with the agreement between the assignments of consecutive refreshes (adjusted Rand
index) and the average change of the descriptors themselves.
Once this criterion has held for
m consecutive refreshes, prototypes are constructed and delivered; a maximum activation round
guarantees that personalization is eventually enabled. The exact criterion and its parameter values
are given in Appendix A, and Algorithm 1 states the complete procedure.
Cost.
HDC-FL does not change the client-side objective, optimizer, or local epoch budget and
requires no additional local optimization stage. Relative to shared-encoder training without head-
based personalization, participating clients additionally make their client-specific heads available
to the server, which stores the most recent encoder–head states required for personalization. At
a refresh, the server computes a truncated SVD of the stacked heads, clusters the resulting K
descriptors, constructs G weighted encoder prototypes, and computes the client-specific prototype
mixtures. Personalized encoders must additionally be delivered to clients for evaluation or deploy-
ment. Thus, HDC-FL adds server-side computation, storage, and communication, but does not
require independently optimized group models or additional client-side training.

Experiments
4.1
Setup
Data and heterogeneity.
We evaluate on CIFAR-10 and CIFAR-100 (Krizhevsky and Hinton,
2009) with K = 20 clients and private client-level train and test distributions. We consider three
forms of heterogeneity: label-subset skew (LS2/LS3), where clients observe only 20% or 30% of the
label space; Dirichlet label skew with α ∈{0.07, 0.3}, which produces continuously varying label
proportions (Hsu et al., 2019); and rotation shift, where clients receive one of four fixed image
rotations while label proportions follow Dir(1).
These settings respectively test discrete label-
support differences, continuous label skew, and client-level feature shift. Full partition construction
is given in Appendix A.
Models and baselines.
We evaluate ResNet-18 and MobileNetV2 encoders (He et al., 2016;
Sandler et al., 2018) with linear client-specific heads, initialized from the same pretrained encoder
weights within each setting. We compare against FedAvgSharedEncoder (FedAvg-SE), which uses
the same shared-encoder/private-head training protocol without personalization; the personalized
methods FedBABU (Oh et al., 2022) and FedALA (Zhang et al., 2023); the clustered methods
IFCA (Ghosh et al., 2020), PACFL (Vahidian et al., 2023) and EMD-CFL (Zhang et al., 2025); the
soft or multi-cluster methods FedSoft (Ruan and Joe-Wong, 2022) and FedCE (Cai et al., 2023);
and PFedCS (Wu et al., 2025), the closest baseline because it also derives client relationships
from classifier geometry.
Baseline implementation and hyperparameter details are provided in
Appendix A.

4.2
Mean Client Accuracy
Table 1 reports mean client accuracy for the eight ResNet-18 configurations; MobileNetV2 results
are provided in Appendix B. HDC-FL and FedBABU obtain the best average rank on ResNet-
18 (2.12). Across all 11 configurations, HDC-FL, PFedCS and FedBABU achieve macro-averaged
mean accuracies of 88.08%, 87.86% and 87.58% respectively. Because these differences are small
relative to the per-setting seed variation, we regard the three methods as comparable in mean
accuracy rather than interpreting the aggregate values as a clear ordering.
The most informative differences occur within individual heterogeneity families. On CIFAR-
100 label-subset skew, HDC-FL and PFedCS obtain numerically higher mean accuracies than the
remaining evaluated baselines, reaching 88.43% and 88.32% on LS2 and 85.26% and 85.45% on
LS3, respectively. HDC-FL also reaches 91.55% under CIFAR-10 rotation, while FedBABU has
higher observed mean accuracy in the more difficult CIFAR-100 Dirichlet and rotation settings.

4.4
Ablations
We isolate two central design choices: whether head-derived grouping improves over population-
level prototype averaging, and whether soft prototype membership improves over hard assignment.
Additional descriptor and hyperparameter diagnostics are reported in Appendix E.
Grouping signal.
Table 3 isolates the two steps that build the personalized encoder, on CIFAR-
10 rotation without pretrained initialization. Replacing the client’s own cached encoder by a single
population-level prototype already raises mean accuracy from 77.57% to 80.30%, so part of the
benefit is simply that a prototype averages several cached encoders. Splitting that prototype into
four head-derived groups adds a further 0.74 points in mean accuracy and also improves both tail
metrics. Thus, after accounting for the benefit of prototype averaging itself, introducing multiple
head-derived prototypes provides an additional improvement on all three metrics. Additional diag-
nostics in Appendix E show that the low-rank projection substantially improves cluster separation
and temporal stability relative to raw heads without materially changing predictive accuracy.
Membership.
Replacing the soft mixture by a hard assignment to the most similar prototype
costs 0.85 points of mean accuracy and 1.08 and 0.95 points on the two tail metrics on CIFAR-100
with α = 0.3, the setting in which client distributions form a continuum rather than discrete groups

(Appendix E).
4.5
Does Head Similarity Predict Encoder Compatibility?
We also test the central assumption of the method directly: that clients with similar classifier heads
can use related encoders. At the final round of the MobileNetV2 runs we take every ordered pair
of clients (t, d), keep target t’s own classifier head and test data fixed, replace only its encoder with
donor d’s cached encoder, and re-evaluate. The swap is zero-shot, with no retraining or fine-tuning.
Each pair yields a loss benefit and an accuracy gain relative to the target’s own encoder, and we ask
how well head similarity ranks the 19 available donors by these quantities. Three settings and three
seeds give 3,420 directed pairs; metrics are computed per target and then averaged (full protocol
in Appendix F).
Table 4 reports the outcome. Head similarity has positive mean Spearman correlation with loss
benefit and AUROC above 0.5 in all three settings, with the largest observed association under
rotation shift (ρ = 0.243, AUROC = 0.783) and the smallest under CIFAR-100 LS3 (ρ = 0.117,
AUROC = 0.565). The practical effect is measured by donor selection: choosing the nearest-head
donor rather than a random one is worth 2.50 accuracy points under rotation, 1.57 points on
CIFAR-10 label skew and 0.41 points on CIFAR-100 label skew, and the closest distance quintile
outperforms the farthest in every setting. Average precision must be read against prevalence: on
CIFAR-100 label skew 71.1% of donors already help, so an AP of 0.782 is a lift of only 0.071.
The rotation setting provides the cleanest check, because the client groups are known indepen-
dently of the learned heads. The nearest-head donor shares the target’s rotation in 75.0% of cases
against a 21.1% base rate, head similarity separates same-rotation from different-rotation pairs with
AUROC 0.887, and same-rotation swaps average +0.83 accuracy points against −3.46 points for
different-rotation swaps. Two qualifications follow from the same experiment. First, a useful rank-
ing is not a guarantee of useful transfer: on CIFAR-10 label skew only 15.6% of individual swaps
reduce the target’s loss, so most single-donor replacements are harmful even where the ranking is in-
formative. This is consistent with HDC-FL combining several cached encoders by sample-weighted
averaging and soft mixing rather than adopting one donor’s encoder. Second, in the label-skew
settings head similarity is closely associated with the overlap of client label supports (Spearman
0.89 and 0.78), and the partial correlation with transfer benefit after controlling for that overlap is
near zero (Appendix F). In these settings the signal is therefore better described as an operational
proxy for shared label support than as an independent measure of encoder compatibility.
5
Discussion
HDC-FL is not uniformly more accurate than the strongest evaluated baselines. The results instead
support a narrower claim: classifier-head geometry is a useful model-side signal for deciding how to
share representations. Cross-method comparisons cannot isolate this signal from other algorithmic
differences, so the donor-swap diagnostic in Section 4.5 tests the underlying association directly.
Head similarity positively ranks encoder-transfer benefit in all three tested settings and recovers
the known rotation structure under covariate shift. Under label skew, Appendix F shows that much
of this association is explained by shared label support.

6
Conclusion
We introduced HDC-FL, which uses the geometry of the private classifier heads that shared-encoder
training already produces to decide how representations are shared, returning to each client a
soft mixture of head-derived encoder prototypes paired with its own unchanged head. Across 11
configurations and two backbones, HDC-FL is competitive with the strongest baselines in mean
accuracy and attains the highest macro-averaged worst-client and bottom-20% accuracy, with small
margins over PFedCS and no additional client-side optimization. The contrast with FedBABU—
a comparable mean but a 38-point fairness gap—illustrates why personalized federated learning
should also be evaluated by client-level tails.

EXPERIMENTAL DETAILS
Partitions.
Every configuration uses K = 20 clients. Under label-subset skew each client draws
a random subset of the classes containing 20% of them (LS2) or 30% of them (LS3)—two or three
classes on CIFAR-10 and twenty or thirty on CIFAR-100—and the examples of each class are
divided only among the clients that own that class. Under Dirichlet skew the class proportions of
each client are drawn from Dir(α) with α ∈{0.07, 0.3}. Under rotation shift each client is assigned
one of 0◦, 90◦, 180◦, 270◦, giving five clients per rotation, and its label proportions are drawn from
Dir(1).
Train and test splits.
We use the official CIFAR splits and do not resample them. The 50,000
training images are partitioned across the 20 clients by the procedure above, and the 10,000 test
images are then allocated class by class in proportion to the clients’ training counts for that class:
a client holding a given fraction of the training examples of class c receives the same fraction of the
test examples of class c, and a class no client trains on is given to no client. Each client therefore
has a private test split that follows its own training distribution, and every reported accuracy is
computed on that split.
Training and preprocessing.
All methods run for 100 communication rounds, except CIFAR-
100 with α = 0.07, which runs for 80. Unless otherwise required by a method-specific optimization
stage, client updates use AdamW with one local epoch per communication round, batch size 20,
encoder and head learning rates of 10−3, weight decay 10−4, and no learning-rate scheduler.
Training images are processed using ImageNet-style augmentation: a 224 × 224 random resized
crop with scale [0.2, 1.0], random horizontal flipping, and ImageNet normalization. Test images are
resized and center-cropped to 224 × 224 and use the same normalization. The same preprocessing
is used across methods. Method-specific optimization stages, including FedBABU personalization
and PFedCS classifier fine-tuning, are specified below.
Initialization.
Encoders start from the torchvision ImageNet-1k checkpoints (Russakovsky et al.,
2015): ResNet18_Weights.DEFAULT for ResNet-18 and MobileNet_V2_Weights.IMAGENET1K_V1
for MobileNetV2. The classifier heads are single linear layers and are always randomly initialized.
Within each setting and seed, all methods use the same pretrained encoder initialization and the
same randomly initialized client heads. The diagnostics in Table 3 and Appendix E instead start
from randomly initialized encoders and are labeled as such.
Asset provenance and terms.
CIFAR-10 and CIFAR-100 were obtained from their official
public distribution page, which requests citation of the accompanying technical report but does
not state a separate dataset license (https://www.cs.toronto.edu/~kriz/cifar.html). Model
definitions and the ImageNet-1k checkpoints named above were obtained through torchvision. The
torchvision source code is distributed under the BSD 3-Clause license; its documentation notes
that pretrained weights may also be subject to terms inherited from their training data. We use
the ImageNet-pretrained weights only for non-commercial research, consistent with the ImageNet
terms of access (https://www.image-net.org/accessagreement). All baseline algorithms were
independently reimplemented in our common experimental framework from their published descrip-
tions; no third-party baseline repository code was incorporated.

Training dynamics.
Figure 2 shows mean client accuracy over communication rounds for three
representative settings. Before activation, HDC-FL follows the shared trajectory; afterwards, the
curve reports the personalized prototype mixture. The activation step is most visible on CIFAR-10
rotation and CIFAR-100 LS3 and smaller on CIFAR-100 α = 0.3. After activation, performance
remains stable because prototypes are rebuilt from the current cache at each refresh. These curves
describe round-wise predictive accuracy rather than optimization speed, since activation changes
the evaluated model but not the underlying training procedure.
Figure 3 extends Figure 2 to the remaining configurations. In every case HDC-FL follows the
shared trajectory until activation and then improves or remains level, and the size of the step at
activation is larger in the label-subset and rotation settings than in the Dirichlet ones.
Figure 4 reports the internal diagnostics of the grouping over training on CIFAR-10 rotation
without pretrained initialization. The silhouette score increases as the descriptor space becomes
more structured, the adjusted Rand index between consecutive refreshes stays high once the group-
ing has settled, and the membership entropy falls, indicating that clients become more confidently
associated with one prototype. The personalization gain is largest immediately after activation and
decreases as the shared encoder catches up, which is consistent with the flattening of the curves in
Figure 2.

E.1
Low-Rank Descriptor
Table 10 compares the rank-8 descriptor with the raw classifier head on CIFAR-100 LS3, where the
class dimension is large enough for the projection to act. Accuracy is unchanged (85.24% against
85.27%), so the projection is not what produces the predictive performance. What it changes is the
geometry of the descriptor space: the silhouette score rises from 0.027 to 0.108 and the agreement
between the assignments of consecutive refreshes, measured by the adjusted Rand index, rises from
0.152 to 0.401. Groups found on raw heads are therefore poorly separated and unstable over time,
while the projected descriptors give a grouping that persists between refreshes; this is an argument
for stability, not for accuracy.

DONOR-COMPATIBILITY DIAGNOSTIC
This appendix gives the full protocol and the additional measurements behind Section 4.5.
Protocol.
The diagnostic is run at the final round (t = 100) of MobileNetV2 training with 20
clients, full participation and seeds 42, 123 and 2026, under three settings: CIFAR-10 label-subset
skew, CIFAR-100 label-subset skew (both with 30% label support per client) and CIFAR-100
rotation with Dir(1) label proportions, where clients 0–4, 5–9, 10–14 and 15–19 receive 0◦, 90◦,
180◦and 270◦respectively. For each ordered pair (t, d) with d̸ = t, the target is first evaluated
with its own encoder and head to obtain self_loss(t) and self_acc(t); the target encoder is then
replaced by donor d’s cached encoder, the target head is left unchanged, and the hybrid model is
evaluated on the same target test split to obtain donor_loss(t, d) and donor_acc(t, d). The loss
benefit is self_loss −donor_loss and the accuracy gain is donor_acc −self_acc, both positive
when the transfer helps. Nothing is retrained or fine-tuned after the swap. Pairs are directed,
since target data and target head differ between the two orderings, giving 20 × 19 = 380 pairs per

Distance quintiles.
For each target, donors are ordered by low-rank head distance and split into
five groups, Q1 being the closest. Table 14 reports the accuracy gain per quintile. The ordering
is monotone or nearly monotone in all three settings, which supports the ranking interpretation
without assuming a linear relationship. The absolute values also make the scale of the intervention
clear: on CIFAR-10 label skew and CIFAR-100 rotation a single encoder swap costs accuracy on
average in every quintile, and head proximity reduces that cost rather than turning it into a gain.
Rotation structure.
In the rotation setting the true client groups are known, so head similarity
can be evaluated directly as a group detector. The nearest-head donor shares the target’s rotation
in 75.0% of cases against a base rate of 21.1%; head similarity detects same-rotation pairs with
AUROC 0.887 and average precision 0.606; and mean head similarity is 0.789 within a rotation
group against 0.752 across groups. Transfer follows the same split: same-rotation swaps give a
mean loss benefit of +0.072 and +0.83 accuracy points, different-rotation swaps −0.162 and −3.46
points.
Label-support overlap.
In the two label-skew settings we compare head similarity with the
Jaccard overlap of the clients’ observed label supports. The two are strongly associated (Spearman
0.888 ± 0.058 on CIFAR-10 and 0.779 ± 0.097 on CIFAR-100).
After residualizing the overlap
ranks, the partial Spearman correlation between head similarity and loss benefit is −0.092 ± 0.265
on CIFAR-10 and 0.090 ± 0.270 on CIFAR-100, i.e. indistinguishable from zero at this sample size.
We therefore read head similarity in the label-skew regime as largely reflecting shared label support.
This partial correlation is exploratory and is not a causal estimate.

Scope.
The diagnostic measures zero-shot encoder interchangeability conditional on a fixed target
head at one point in training, on one backbone. It establishes an association and a donor-ranking
result, not a causal or general claim about encoder equivalence, and it does not evaluate HDC-FL’s
prototypes themselves, which average several cached encoders rather than substituting one.
