Mining the Self-Rewriting System: A Vision for
Data Mining on AI-Native Infrastructure
Mayank Darbari∗, Chiman Salavati†, Liang Wu∗, Dawei Wang∗,
Jiong Zhang∗, Kelly Wan∗, Krishna Chakka∗, Liangjie Hong∗
∗Nokia, Sunnyvale, California, USA

Abstract—Classical data mining treats data as generated out-
side the learner. Emerging AI-native infrastructure invalidates
this assumption: as agents observe system state, act, update
retrieval stores and models, and promote new artifacts into
the serving path, their own decisions reshape the future logs,
traces, corpora, and evaluation records used for later training
and release. We call this new research object a self-rewriting
system and the resulting system-induced distribution shift en-
dogenous drift. Our central, falsifiable claim is that cross-surface
causes are not identifiable in general from marginal per-surface
monitoring: different coupled failures can produce the same
individual signals while differing in their joint behavior, requiring
joint analysis for reliable attribution. This agenda comprises
four foundational research problems: fusing heterogeneous drift
signals into calibrated gating and attribution (C1); mining
agent traces whose activity alphabet evolves (C2); maintaining
knowledge fabrics through online reindexing, faithfulness, and
compression decisions (C3); and safely promoting concurrent
learning streams through one shared evaluation gate (C4). These
remain fragmented across concept drift, performative prediction,
process mining, streaming retrieval-augmented generation, and
MLOps, without a unified data-mining agenda. We outline public
benchmarks and evaluation criteria, using AI-native telecom only
as a motivating exemplar with analogues in finance, software
CI/CD, and clinical decision support.
Index Terms—self-rewriting systems, endogenous drift, agent-
trace mining, streaming retrieval-augmented generation.

The central thesis is that self-rewriting systems render the
classical exogenous-data assumption insufficient because the
learner becomes part of the data generator. Classical data
mining treats the world as something to observe; a self-
rewriting system observes a world its own prior actions helped
create. The result is not concept drift in one stream but
endogenous drift across coupled artifacts: models, retrievers,
tools, policies, traces, corpora, and promotion logs.
Why now? The loop is becoming practical because three
ingredients have converged: agents can call external tools
through standard interfaces [3], adapted models can be served
and hot-swapped on shared infrastructure [4], [5], and re-
trieval/evaluation layers can continuously update the evidence
on which agents act [6], [7]. Early research and industry
activity on AI-native cores [8] signal this direction as an
emerging trend, and the pattern is emerging wherever agents
operate critical infrastructure. Individually these components
are evaluated as if they observe an external stream; together
they form the closed loop in Fig. 1.

A concrete failure the loop creates. A network-operations
agent applies a newly retrieved runbook; the configuration
change shifts telemetry. An anomaly-detection adapter is fine-
tuned on the traces and promoted, and its explanations alter the
citations a root-cause agent emits, which re-enter the corpus
that drives the next retrieval and training slice. When quality
later regresses, the question is not whether one stream drifted
but which coupled artifact to gate: the corpus, tool catalog,
adapter, reward model, or safety policy, since independent
detectors may alarm on several axes yet cannot attribute the
cascade the loop itself created. This is the seed of C1–C4.
Why this is a Blue Sky data-mining agenda. The ob-
ject of study is not the agent architecture but the data the
loop emits: heterogeneous drift signals, structured reason-
ing/action traces, evolving text/graph/multimodal corpora, and
registry-level promotion logs, that is, streams, sequences, non-
stationary graphs, evolving alphabets, and coupled decision
logs, all central objects for data mining. The Blue Sky step
is to treat the loop itself as a new object: the data stream is
no longer external to the mining system, but partly produced
by the same actions, retrieval updates, policy changes, and
promoted artifacts the system later trains, evaluates, and gates.
The opportunity for data mining is to define the public tasks
and metrics for this loop before they are settled ad hoc inside
proprietary stacks; telecom is only a feasibility witness. More
precisely, marginal per-surface monitoring is not sufficient in
general for cross-surface attribution: distinct coupled failures
can share the same individual signals but differ in their
joint behavior. We treat this claim as refuted if marginal
baselines match joint attribution on the drift-arena. This single
assumption break induces four benchmarkable surfaces:
• C1. Endogenous drift: fusing co-evolving drift signals
across corpora, models, tools, policies, and serving deci-
sions into calibrated gating and attribution decisions.
• C2. Agent-trace mining: treating structured reason-
ing/action traces as primary event streams over evolving
tool and role alphabets.
• C3. Self-evolving knowledge fabrics: making reindex-
ing, summary invalidation, citation faithfulness, prove-
nance tracking, and compression online decisions over
versioned multimodal corpora.
• C4. Closed-loop train-to-serve: safely promoting con-
current learning streams through a shared evaluation gate,
registry, observability surface, and safety contract.
II. WHAT SHOULD WE PONDER ABOUT IT?
C1–C4 are coupled surfaces, not independent topics: C1
drift signals can trigger C4 gating; a C4 promotion can
change the C3 knowledge available to agents; that change
alters C2 traces, whose outputs feed future C1 signals. Thus a
perturbation on one surface can change the data-generating
process observed by the others, rather than merely adding
correlated features.
How this differs from existing agendas. The distinction
from adjacent areas (concept drift, performative prediction,
process mining, streaming RAG, MLOps) is multi-artifact
endogeneity: a coupled loop in which corpora, tools, policies,
adapters, retrieval indices, reward models, serving decisions,
and evaluation gates co-evolve and generate training and
evaluation data. Performative prediction names model-induced
change an endogenous shift, as opposed to environment-
driven exogenous drift [9], [10]; together with counterfactual
/ feedback-loop learning [11] it is closest in spirit, but treats a
single deployed model rather than an infrastructure loop whose
artifacts co-evolve. We keep this endogenous/exogenous vo-
cabulary and generalize it from a single-artifact setting to a
coupled loop: formally, st+1 = F(st, at) over an artifact-state
vector st (corpora, adapters, indices, policies, gates) whose
actions at and induced data distribution D(st) both depend
on st; performative prediction is the single-artifact special case
where st represents one deployed model. Characterizing F for
coupled, heterogeneous artifacts is an open problem we leave
to the agenda. A related but distinct concern is model collapse,
the degradation that follows from training indiscriminately on
model-generated content [12]: that is a single-model diversity
loss, whereas endogenous drift is a distribution shift across
coupled, heterogeneous artifacts that jointly gate the next
release. Each existing agenda isolates one surface (Table I).
The distinctive failure arises when one artifact’s action changes
another artifact’s future data or evaluation context; without
such cross-artifact feedback, C1–C4 reduce to their existing
single-surface counterparts.
A. C1. Endogenous drift surfaces
Motivation. Multiple system surfaces may change concur-
rently, so the central problem is to identify which change
caused a failure and whether the loop should be gated.
Problem. In a self-rewriting loop, drift becomes action-
able when one subsystem’s outputs no longer match the
next subsystem’s assumptions, and it is endogenous when
the mismatch is induced by the loop’s own earlier actions
(Def. 2). The difficulty is that drift is not a single stream:
the knowledge surface shifts as specs and runbooks enter
the corpus, the model surface as adapters re-tune and reward
models update [23], the serving surface as expert routing
shifts mass [24], the agent surface as tools change, and the
safety surface as policies are rewritten. The resulting signals
are incommensurable: embedding divergence, likelihood-ratio
shift, reward-function shift, expert-mass shift, tool-catalog
Jaccard distance, and semantic policy difference. C1 asks how
to fuse them into one calibrated drift surface that decides what
to attribute, what to gate, and when to keep the loop running.
Why joint signals can be necessary. Let binary surface
signals X, Y
have two causes: A yields (0, 0) or (1, 1)
equiprobably, whereas B yields (0, 1) or (1, 0). Both causes
give identical Bernoulli(1/2) marginals for X and Y , so
marginal monitoring cannot distinguish them; successful late
fusion must preserve their cross-surface dependence.
Benchmark spec. The drift-arena exposes time-indexed cor-
pus, model, tool, policy, and serving signals and asks a system
to gate and attribute the root cause, compared with indepen-
dent, learned, and causal-graph fusion baselines (Table II).

B. C2. Agent-trace mining
Motivation. Agent sessions produce structured traces of tool
use, retrieval, reasoning, and handoffs, but these traces must
be mined while the available tools, roles, and skills evolve.
Problem. Every tool call, model call, sandbox execution,
retrieval step, and human handoff leaves a structured span,
and a session’s spans form its agent trace (Def. 3). A span
is a tuple of caller, role, tool, argument summary, retrieved
evidence, latency, error class, citation set, parent span, and
outcome, following OpenTelemetry GenAI conventions [25]
for reasoning-and-acting agent sessions [26]. These traces
carry far more than request/error/duration telemetry because
they expose the agent’s intermediate decisions. The mining
tasks include detecting capability gaps, slow tool paths, redun-
dant retrievals, citation failures, prompt-injection detours [27],
[28], role mismatch, and reusable span subsequences that can
become new skills [29].
What is missing. Prior work spans process mining, agent
telemetry, agent benchmarks, retrieval evaluation, and LLM-
as-judge evaluation [7], [18], [25], [29]–[35]. However, these
lines of work do not yet formulate a public data-mining
task for agent traces whose event alphabet evolves during
deployment. Business-process drift [19] studies control-flow
change over a largely fixed activity set; in self-rewriting
systems, by contrast, the alphabet is endogenous: tools, roles,
and skills may be added, modified, or retired by the same loop
that generates the trace data.
Benchmark spec. An agent-trace benchmark provides ses-
sions of spans and poses the mining tasks above over an
evolving tool and role alphabet, scored for robustness to
tool additions/removals and generalization to unseen tools
(Table II). The key open question is how to define frequent
or useful patterns when the span alphabet grows, shrinks, and
changes meaning during deployment.
C. C3. Self-evolving knowledge fabrics
Motivation.
In
self-rewriting
systems,
the
knowledge
sources used by an agent evolve over time, so answer va-
lidity, citation faithfulness, and summary consistency must be
maintained under continuous corpus updates.
Problem. The hard part of a self-rewriting system’s knowl-
edge fabric is not retrieval alone but the stream of online
decisions required as the corpus changes underneath the agent:
when to reindex, when to invalidate a summary, when a
citation is no longer valid with respect to the current corpus,
how to keep faithfulness over versioned corpora, and how to
compress context under a bounded budget. Batch faithfulness
scoring [7] hides these because it never asks when a corpus
update becomes visible to future answers. C3 treats citation as
an online contract, summary maintenance as a streaming graph
problem, reindexing as a drift-triggered decision, multimodal
updates as first-class corpus events (vision-language models
such as LLaVA [36] make image and text jointly indexable),
and compression as a budgeted fidelity trade-off [6], [37], [38].


Beyond streaming RAG. Graph-augmented retrieval [6], hi-
erarchical summarization [37], and faithfulness evaluation [7]
largely address retrieval quality and corpus maintenance [20].
The key difference here is endogeneity: answers, citations,
summaries, and promoted artifacts can re-enter the corpus,
so retrieval decisions alter future evidence. C3 studies this
feedback loop across versioned citation, summary invalidation,
multimodal updates, and compression. We measure time-to-
truth, the lag from an update to the first answer reflecting it,
and the compression-faithfulness frontier, the best faithfulness
at each compression ratio under a fixed retrieval budget.
Benchmark spec. A streaming-RAG benchmark provides
a versioned, time-stamped multimodal corpus with known
update events, queries, answer-validity intervals, and a re-
trieval budget, scored by time-to-truth and the compression-
faithfulness frontier among other metrics (Table II). The key
open question is whether freshness and faithfulness can be op-
timized together, or whether a fixed inference budget imposes
a fundamental trade-off.
D. C4. Closed-loop train-to-serve
Motivation. Multiple artifacts may be updated concurrently
and promoted through one shared gate, so release decisions
must account for cross-stream interactions.
Problem. The data-mining challenge is not how to optimize
one training stream; it is how to decide which of many
interacting streams may safely enter a shared serving path.
Concurrent updates to adapters, preference models, distilled
models, routing policies, or skill libraries [5], [23], [24], [29],
[38]–[40] each follow a path from production traces to a
trained artifact, evaluation log, and serving decision. These
streams share one registry, evaluation surface, and safety con-
tract behind a single promotion gate (Def. 4), so a promotion
in one stream can change the evidence, workload, or evaluation
context seen by another. C4 asks how to mine promotion logs
and evaluation traces to decide when to promote, delay, isolate,
retest, or roll back an artifact.
What is missing. MLOps [16] and streaming ML [13], [15]
take a single-artifact or single-policy view; C4 instead stud-
ies heterogeneous streams sharing one gate under properties
such as no-skip-eval, promotion provenance, and multi-stream
non-interference (Def. 5). In an internal deployment (details
withheld), tightening one stream’s gate from 50 to 200 targets
reduced judge-graded quality from 2.78 to 2.60 on 500 held-
out prompts, recovering to 3.15 with LLM-judge gating. A
public counterpart can inject concurrent promotions and replay
each with and without the other stream’s update to measure
cross-stream interference.

Benchmark spec. A closed-loop benchmark provides re-
playable promotion/evaluation logs and asks a system to
promote, delay, rollback, isolate, or retest under a shared safety
contract (Table II). No-skip-eval is the fraction of promotions
completing all required evaluations (target one); promotion
regret is excess deployment loss relative to the safe-promotion
oracle. The open question is whether safe concurrency can
bound cross-stream interference.
III. WHAT IS THE BLUE SKY IDEA?
The Blue Sky idea is to treat the self-rewriting system as
a primary unit of study and to turn C1–C4 into a shared,
open benchmark agenda: a drift-arena (C1), an agent-trace
bench (C2), a streaming-RAG bench (C3), and a closed-loop
promotion bench (C4), each turning a hidden assumption into
a measurable task. The bet is that if the community builds
these in the open, the core problems of self-rewriting systems
will be defined by public tasks rather than private production
conventions.
A public benchmark plan. Because production traces are
often proprietary, public benchmarks can use public corpora
and synthetic generators with controlled perturbations, while
internal trajectories only inform the schema and failure modes.
Generators should vary coupled updates, evolving alphabets,
non-IID/federated clients, and adversarial events such as poi-
soning or prompt injection. Recording the affected artifact,
root-cause axis, and counterfactual no-promotion baseline pro-
vides ground truth for attribution and non-interference.
IV. WHAT WILL SUCCESS LOOK LIKE?
Success progresses from public C1–C4 benchmarks and
baselines in the near term to shared metrics and theory for
endogenous drift, trace mining, knowledge maintenance, and
safe promotion. Long term, self-rewriting systems should
report reproducible cross-surface evidence across domains, not
model quality alone.
V. IMPLICATIONS FOR DATA MINING
Self-rewriting loops recur beyond telecom: finance agents
reshape future monitoring, CI/CD agents alter later tests and
training logs, and clinical recommendations affect future notes
and retrieval. Across domains, system outputs become inputs
to the next loop.
C1–C4 are new forms of objects the data-mining com-
munity already studies: streams, traces, graphs, and coupled
decision logs. The community that built concept drift, process
mining, streaming algorithms, and retrieval evaluation is best
positioned to define these public tasks and metrics before
they settle inside private stacks: self-rewriting systems should
become a first-class object of data mining, and C1–C4 provide
the initial benchmark map. When cross-surface causes are not
identifiable from marginal signals, systems deployed today
may be evaluated with tools blind to important loop-induced
failure modes.
