Abstract:  
The rapid growth of artificial intelligence (AI) depends on interconnected 
water, energy, and data systems spanning infrastructure life cycles and diverse 
geographies, 
with 
the 
potential 
to 
intensify 
water 
and 
energy 
insecurity, 
exacerbate 
social 
inequities 
and 
lead 
to 
cascading 
sustainability 
issues 
globally. Yet assessments commonly examine them separately, limiting our 
understanding of where resource pressures arise, how they are transferred 
across space, and who bears their consequences. To address these challenges, we 
established  a water–energy–data (WED) nexus framework and use large language models 
to synthesize 12, 825 water- or energy-related studies across mineral extraction, 
semiconductor manufacturing, data center operation and electronic-waste processing. The 
evidence map identifies a mismatch between the interconnected activities supporting AI 
infrastructure and boundaries used to assess them. Researchers assess operational energy 
more than water use and other indirect demands, while interactions across locations and 
life-cycle activities remain understudied. These gaps obscure how resource demands shift 


among infrastructure nodes and regions, and how data-enabled benefits compare with their 
underlying costs. The WED nexus provides a foundation for integrated assessment of 
spatially metacoupled systems, while the evidence map can inform priorities for managing 
the sustainability implications of the AI boom 
Introduction 
Artificial intelligence (AI) is increasingly deployed to support sustainable development 
globally, including the management of water and energy systems1,2. Its rapid growth, 
however, relies on physical infrastructure that generates significant environmental and 
social pressures3,4. For instance, data centers consumed approximately 415 TWh globally 
(1.5% of total demand) in 20245, with U.S. facilities alone accounting for 192 TWh (4.7% 
of national electricity) projected to surge to 649 TWh (11.8%) by 20306. Additionally, U.S. 
data centers consumed roughly 66 billion litres of water directly and an estimated 12 times 
that amount indirectly in 20237. While existing assessments provide important evidence on 
such facility-level electricity demand and cooling water use8–10, these metrics alone cannot 
fully capture AI's sustainability consequences. Reductions in direct facility cooling water 
can be offset by increased electricity demand and water consumption at power plants11. 
Beyond these systemic trade-offs, local vulnerabilities are exacerbated because data center 
capacity is highly clustered12, with two-thirds of post-2022 facilities located in water-
stressed regions13. Furthermore, AI services depend on the broader digital supply chain, 
such as semiconductor manufacturing, mineral extraction and the management of 
discarded equipment, connecting demand for computing to resource use and waste burdens 
across multiple regions14–17. Therefore, understanding how AI affects sustainability 
requires a comprehensive examination of the lifecycle of resource systems and local 
communities that support its infrastructure, weighed alongside the potential benefits of AI 
applications. 
To address this complexity, we conceptualize the Water–Energy–Data (WED) nexus as the 
dependencies, interactions and feedbacks among water systems, energy systems, and data 
systems across the activities that support AI services (Fig. 1a). Building on the water–
energy nexus, which explains the water requirements for energy production and energy 
requirements for water provision18, the WED nexus extends it by introducing data and 


computing infrastructure as a third interacting system. These components connect demand 
for AI services to resource consumption while supporting monitoring and management of 
water and energy systems19,20. Treating data infrastructure as part of the nexus therefore 
expands the analytical boundary of the issue: it connects resource consumption at 
computing facilities with upstream production, downstream waste, regional resource 
constraints and the potential feedback through which water and energy systems may 
themselves limit future infrastructure development.  
While the WED nexus captures the systemic dependencies within specific places, we 
organize these relationships across space (within and across different places) using the 
metacoupling framework21. The metacoupling framework captures how human–
environment interactions are distributed within systems (e.g., places), between adjacent 
systems, and across distant systems, making it particularly suited to analyze AI 
infrastructure whose resource demands and environmental consequences are spatially 
decoupled from end-users3. A data center provides an illustrative focal system—cooling 
and computing occur within the facility, utilities and communities connect it to surrounding 
systems, and hardware production and end-of-life activities can connect it to distant 
locations (Fig. 1b). Extended definitions and illustrative pathways are provided in 
Supplementary Note 1 and Supplementary Table 1. 
Here, we conduct a large language model (LLM)-assisted structured synthesis of 12,825 
unique water- or energy-related studies across four resource-intensive AI infrastructure 
activities: mineral extraction, semiconductor manufacturing, data center operation and e-
waste processing. We quantify gaps in the evidence base by examining how the retrieved 
quantitative literature reports direct and indirect resource use, water–energy relationships, 
pressures on nearby resource systems, connections across infrastructure activities, and 
environmental and social impacts and feedbacks. The analysis maps differences in 
coverage across resource domains, life-cycle activities, and spatial scales. We interpret 
these patterns through the WED nexus to identify priorities for integrated assessment and 
inform governance of AI’s implications for sustainability.

Results 
Rapid growth and geographical unevenness of the evidence base 
To quantify gaps in the evidence base, a structured coding framework was conducted to 
evaluate how existing research addresses the WED nexus across four resource-intensive 
AI infrastructure nodes: mineral extraction, semiconductor manufacturing, data centers, 
and e-waste processing. Publication output across the water-data (WD), energy-data (ED), 
and overlapping WED corpora rose overall, with year-to-year fluctuations and a marked 
acceleration after 2023 (Fig. 2a–c) aligning with the generative AI boom. However, the 
scale and composition of the evidence remain highly uneven. Energy-related research 
constitutes by far the largest share of the evidence base and is dominated by data center 
studies, reflecting a strong focus on electricity consumption, operational efficiency and 
cooling demand in assessments of AI infrastructure. Water-related research is less and more 
distributed across the life cycle, with mineral extraction contributing to a substantial share 
of recent growth. Studies that jointly consider water and energy remain comparatively 
limited, despite their rapid expansion after 2023. 
The distribution of research across the four nodes also reveals a fragmented understanding 
of resource demand. Data centers dominate energy-related studies, whereas upstream 
mineral extraction accounts for a much larger share of water-related and integrated water–
energy research. Semiconductor manufacturing remains weakly represented across all 
three corpora, despite the intensive electricity and ultrapure water requirements of 
semiconductor fabrication. E-waste processing contributes to a growing but still 
comparatively small body of evidence. Research attention therefore does not follow the AI 
infrastructure life cycle evenly: different resource pressures are examined in different node-


specific literatures, rather than as connected demands generated by a common 
infrastructure system. 
Geographical coverage is similarly uneven (Fig. 2d, e). Among the 12,825 unique eligible 
studies, 1,863 (14.53%) reported identifiable country information in their titles or abstracts. 
Country-level studies are concentrated in the United States, China, Europe and a limited 
number of countries in East and South Asia. The maps compare these counts with national 
Water Resource Stability and Energy Trilemma scores22,23. Some countries with lower 
indicator scores had few geographically identifiable studies, highlighting locations for 
further evidence of retrieval. These patterns show that the evidence base is expanding 
rapidly without becoming proportionately more balanced. Research remains concentrated 
on data center energy use, selected upstream water pressures, and a relatively small group 
of countries. As a result, current scholarship provides an incomplete basis for comparing 
resource dependence across the principal water- and energy-intensive nodes of AI 
infrastructure or for evaluating how their expansion interacts with heterogeneous resource 
constraints worldwide.

Limited attention to indirect water and energy consumption 
Energy-related interactions are quantified much more consistently than water-related 
interactions, particularly at the operational stages of AI infrastructure (Fig. 3). At the data 
center node, 70.60% of ED papers quantify direct energy or electricity use, compared with 
only 24.53% of WD studies that quantify water withdrawal, water consumption, or cooling-
water flow. Direct energy quantification is similarly prominent in chip-manufacturing 
studies (73.7%) and remains substantial in the mining (54.2%) and e-waste (52.8%) 
literature. This pattern is consistent with the treatment of electricity demand and energy 
efficiency as core performance variables across AI infrastructure, whereas water use 
remains more unevenly measured and is often framed as a secondary requirement of 
cooling or production processes. 
The analytical boundary varies among infrastructure nodes. In energy-focused data center 
and semiconductor studies, 11.99% and 6.58%, respectively, quantify energy use for water 
systems, while 0.75% and 3.84% quantify other life-cycle energy requirements. Other life-
cycle energy use is reported more often in mineral extraction and e-waste processing, at 
14.92% and 21.97%, respectively. The water-focused data center literature remains 
particularly concentrated on direct operations: 21 of 856 studies (2.45%) quantify water 
use for energy, and five (0.58%) quantify other life-cycle water use. Despite often being 
magnitudes larger than direct use7, indirect water use remains disproportionately 
overlooked. These results reveal not only an imbalance between energy and water 
quantification, but also a fragmented division of analytical attention across the AI-
infrastructure life cycle: operational energy use dominates research on data centers and 
semiconductor manufacturing, whereas upstream and downstream studies more often 
address life-cycle burdens within their own nodes.

Little assessment of nearby and distant resource pressures 
Assessment of resource use remained concentrated within facility boundaries, with limited 
attention to pressures on surrounding water and energy systems (Fig. 4a). Facility-level 
assessment covered 21.71-58.38% of WD studies and 52.77-73.70% of ED studies across 
the four AI infrastructure nodes. By comparison, nearby water pressures were assessed in 
only 7.01% of mineral extraction studies, 6.18% of e-waste processing studies, 4.62% of 
semiconductor-manufacturing studies, and 1.75% of data center studies. Nearby energy 
pressures received even less attention, appearing in 1.88% of mineral extraction studies 
and 1.38% of data center studies, and in none of the eligible semiconductor-manufacturing 
or e-waste processing studies. Resource demand was therefore assessed much more 
frequently within individual facilities or production processes than in relation to the 
availability, capacity, or competing demands of surrounding resource systems. 
Coverage extending to other infrastructure nodes or broader life-cycle activities was also 
uneven and did not consistently decline with increasing analytical scope. Such coverage 
reached 13.43% in WD mineral extraction studies, compared with 4.12% for e-waste 
processing, 3.47% for semiconductor manufacturing, and 0.70% for data centers. In the 
ED literature, it reached 28.13% for e-waste processing and 23.14% for mineral extraction, 
but only 8.49% for semiconductor manufacturing and 0.87% for data centers. These 
comparatively high values in the mining and e-waste literature exceeded their coverage of 
nearby resource pressures, indicating that an expanded life-cycle boundary does not 
necessarily entail greater attention to host region resource constraints. 
The directional matrix further reveals that cross-node assessment was concentrated on a 
small number of connections (Fig. 4b). The most frequently examined connection ran from 
mineral extraction to e-waste processing, appearing in 71 WD studies (2.37%) and 149 ED 
studies (7.56%). ED semiconductor studies most often connect manufacturing energy use 
to data center operation, whereas ED e-waste studies most often connect processing energy 
use to mineral production. By contrast, no WD data center study quantified a connection 


with any of the other three nodes, and ED data center connections with semiconductor 
manufacturing and mineral extraction appeared in only 0.05% and 0.04% of studies, 
respectively. Current research therefore captures selected material recovery and production 
relationships without providing a balanced account of resource flows across extraction, 
semiconductor fabrication, data center operation, and end-of-life processing. Together, the 
limited assessment of host region pressures and the selective treatment of cross-node 
connections leave substantial uncertainty about how water and energy burdens accumulate, 
interact and shift between regions across the AI infrastructure life cycle.

Gaps in environmental and social impacts and feedback 
Both environmental and social interactions were seldom assessed across the four types of 
AI infrastructure, with social interactions receiving substantially less attention than 
environmental interactions (Fig. 5). Environmental interactions were assessed in 7.81% of 
WD studies and 12.00% of ED studies, compared with only 1.05% and 1.58%, respectively, 
for social interactions. This difference was present at every kind of infrastructure. 
Environmental interactions were most frequently assessed in water use of mineral 
extraction and energy use of e-waste processing studies, but even these relatively high 
values represented only a minority of the relevant literature. Overall, environmental 
interactions received approximately 7.5 times more attention than social interactions, 
indicating that the evidence base privileges biophysical processes over effects on 
communities, livelihoods, institutions and resource access. 
Interactions between distant systems were even less frequently studied than those between 
adjacent systems. Across the four nodes, nearby interactions appeared in 7.81% of WD 
studies and 11.97% of ED studies, whereas distant interactions appeared in only 0.39% and 
2.26%, respectively. E-waste was the only node for which distant ED environmental-
impact coverage slightly exceeded nearby coverage, reflecting comparatively greater 
attention to environmental conditions and consequences across end-of-life supply chains. 
This broader spatial reach did not extend to social interactions. Only two ED mineral 


extraction studies assessed distant social impacts, and none assessed distant social feedback. 
Research extending beyond host regions therefore remains concentrated in selected 
environmental dimensions of resource intensive supply chains, while distant communities 
and ecosystems are almost entirely absent. 
Feedbacks from environmental or social systems were less frequently studied than outward 
impacts. Impacts of energy use from e-waste processing were assessed in 20.53% of studies 
but feedbacks in only 0.62%, and in water use of mineral extraction studies, where the 
corresponding values were 9.49% and 0.67%. Data center research differed from this 
general pattern. In the WD literature, feedback coverage reached 3.04%, exceeding impact 
coverage of 1.75%, while ED data center studies showed nearly equal coverage of impacts 
and feedbacks at 7.24% and 7.10%. This relative attention to feedback may reflect the 
operational sensitivity of data centers to climate, water availability, electricity systems, and 
utility conditions. Nevertheless, studies rarely integrated both directions: the highest 
bidirectional coverage was only 0.96% in ED data center research, and no study assessed 
bidirectional social interactions. These gaps limit the ability of current research to explain 
how resource pressures translate into unequal exposure, costs and benefits, and how 
affected communities and institutions subsequently influence infrastructure development 
and resource demand.

Discussion 
Fragmented evidence establishes the need for the WED nexus 
The evidence map identifies a mismatch between the interconnected activities supporting 
AI infrastructure and the boundaries used to assess them. Direct energy use is prominently 
represented in data center and semiconductor research, while broader life-cycle 
requirements receive greater attention in parts of the mining and e-waste literature. Yet 


broader life-cycle coverage does not consistently include pressures on nearby resource 
systems. This distinction matters because accounting for an upstream or downstream 
process and evaluating the capacity of the system that hosts it answers different questions. 
The findings point to a need to connect life-cycle accounting with assessment of resource 
availability, competing demands and feedbacks. 
This fragmentation matters first because the infrastructure nodes in the lifecycle of AI 
services are materially interdependent. Computing demand stimulates hardware 
production6,17; semiconductor fabrication requires electricity, chemicals, minerals and 
ultrapure water24; equipment replacement creates downstream recovery and waste-
processing requirements17; and electricity generation connects data center operation to 
water consumption beyond facility boundaries9. Furthermore, such fragmentation obscures 
the social and environmental consequences of distant interactions. Digital services are not 
immaterial flows but outputs of resource-intensive infrastructures. Data and reusable 
digital knowledge can exhibit quasi-public-good characteristics with broadly distributed 
benefits25, while their supporting infrastructure imposes resource pressures and 
environmental burdens on particular production and host communities26,27. Efficiency 
gains can lower service costs and stimulate further demand—the mechanism underlying 
the Jevons paradox—so lower resource use per service does not necessarily translate into 
lower total use28. When growing infrastructure demand places pressure on shared resources 
without accounting for costs borne elsewhere, weak coordination may create common-
pool-resource conflicts and, under sufficiently weak coordination, tragedy-of-the-
commons dynamics29. Facility-level evidence alone cannot reveal these aggregate effects 
or clarify how responsibility should be shared among the actors that support and use AI 
services30. 
The WED nexus responds to this fragmentation by treating AI services and their supporting 
infrastructure as a network of connected resource flows. It connects facility operations to 
wider water–energy dependencies, including water consumed in electricity generation and 
energy required for ultrapure-water production. It follows these demands through the 
infrastructure life cycle, from upstream production to equipment recovery14,17,31,32. This 
nexus framework makes cross-sectoral and cross-regional transfers visible. Additionally, it 


allows findings from different nodes to be interpreted in their local and temporal contexts. 
For instance, ore grade and hydrology33,34, fabrication processes and recycling rates24,31, 
workloads and cooling strategies4,9, and waste treatment pathways all change resource 
demand17. AI workloads and hardware replacement patterns further shape these demands 
across the infrastructure life cycle14,17. The framework thus brings otherwise separate 
estimates into an account of where, when, and through which processes water and energy 
pressures arise, including during drought, heat waves, and peak grid demand9.  
Beyond connecting infrastructure burdens, the WED nexus has the potential to capture the 
active role of data and computing in water and energy systems19,35. AI-enabled monitoring, 
forecasting and control can help electricity systems balance supply and demand and 
integrate renewable generation20,35, while waste heat from data centers can provide a useful 
thermal-energy source36. Considering these benefits alongside the resources required to 
produce them enables assessment of net system effects rather than treating AI either solely 
as a burden or automatically as a solution20,37.  
Priorities for the WED nexus assessment and governance 
The WED nexus offers a way to assess AI services and infrastructure as coupled systems 
rather than separate demands for water, electricity, and computing. Similar to the Water-
Energy-Food nexus, water and energy are not only essential to each other, but also both 
essential to the data system38. Future research should therefore trace how choices across 
the AI infrastructure life cycle alter several resource demands at once. Evaporative cooling, 
for example, can lower data center electricity demand while increasing direct water 
consumption and dependent on local water availability9,36. Dry or hybrid cooling can 
reduce direct water dependence, but it can increase electricity demand during hot periods 
and shift part of the burden from the water system to the power system9. Similarly, greater 
material recovery from mining or electronic waste can reduce resource losses while 
requiring additional water and energy39,40. These trade-offs are shaped by regional resource 
conditions, while their social consequences depend on how affected communities are 
exposed to the resulting pressures4,40. Assessments should examine marginal conditions 
alongside annual averages and evaluate net impacts across multiple objectives, including 


whether growing AI demand competes for electricity and infrastructure capacity needed 
for decarbonization5. 
A second priority is to trace how these trade-offs propagate across space through coupled 
infrastructure and supply-chain networks. Infrastructure expansion can reshape host-region 
water and energy systems, with consequences for the local environment and affected 
communities3,9. Those conditions can, in turn, influence infrastructure operations and 
workload management14. AI infrastructure also relies on distant systems for essential 
hardware, raw materials, and energy resources14,16. Consequently, a decision that improves 
performance at one facility may shift environmental footprints elsewhere26. This creates 
cascading effects that are not visible in facility-level assessments, requiring spatially 
explicit analysis of how system constraints move through metacoupled AI infrastructure 
networks. 
This broader assessment should inform governance. Because social impacts remain 
insufficiently examined, WED assessments should address who bears burdens, whose 
contributions and concerns are recognized, and who can participate in decisions42. A flow-
based approach can connect direct and indirect resource use, operational and embodied 
impacts, and local and transboundary burdens43. This requires coordination across the 
resource systems and supply chains supporting AI, informed by disclosure of their life-
cycle resource demands. 
Finally, WED research should test how these interconnected systems perform under 
concurrent disruptions. Concurrent shocks like climate change, geopolitical events, 
pandemics, and supply-chain shocks can disrupt the resource supplies and production 
networks on which AI infrastructure depends44–46. Heat waves and droughts jointly increase 
cooling demand while constraining water and electricity supply27; extreme weather 
damages power, transport, and communication infrastructure47,48; and pandemics or 
geopolitical disruptions delay semiconductor production, equipment supply, and system 
repair49. Because such disturbances can overlap and amplify one another48, system 
resilience and sustainability depend not only on facility-level robustness, but also on the 
stability of interconnected water, energy, and data systems and their supply chains under 
concurrent stress. 


Limitations and Outlook 
This study was designed as a systematic evidence map to characterize the distribution and 
connectivity of a large literature rather than extract every result reported in every study. 
Systematic mapping commonly prioritizes consistent coverage of broad evidence bases and 
the identification of knowledge clusters and gaps50. Automated assistance, including 
machine learning and LLM-based approaches, has been developed to support efficient title-
and-abstract screening in large evidence syntheses51. Accordingly, coding titles and 
abstracts offered a scalable and reproducible method for comparing our 12,825 records 
across multiple resource domains and infrastructure nodes. While human full-text 
validation confirmed that consensus among three LLMs achieves high accuracy, an 
inherent limitation is that assessments detailed exclusively within full texts may still be 
omitted. Regarding data sources, the Web of Science Core Collection was selected because 
it provides consistent bibliographic metadata across fields, although relevant grey 
literature52, reports, and non-English publications may be underrepresented53. Similarly, 
the four selected nodes capture major resource-intensive activities associated with AI 
infrastructure but do not constitute an exhaustive life-cycle inventory (e.g., buildings, 
networking equipment, and transport may enter broader life-cycle categories without being 
searched as independent nodes14). Finally, because mineral extraction, semiconductor 
manufacturing, and e-waste processing also support non-AI activities, this synthesis maps 
evidence relevant to AI infrastructure but does not attribute all sectoral resources used 
exclusively to AI. 
Future research can deepen this broad evidence map by conducting full-text syntheses 
utilizing more advanced LLMs and larger token allowances, and by incorporating 
additional databases that capture other infrastructures across the digital service life cycle. 
Studies distinguishing shared digital infrastructure from incremental requirements 
associated with specified AI workloads would further strengthen attribution54. 
Beyond these methodological refinements, our synthesis reveals a critical mismatch 
between the interconnected activities supporting AI and the fragmented ways researchers 
assess them. Despite rapid growth in literature following the AI boom, the focus on direct 
resource consumption of facility operations obscures how resource demands and their 


social and environmental interactions shift across host regions and distant supply chains. 
Furthermore, the evidence base exhibits a stark spatial imbalance, as research concentrates 
on major economies while resource-vulnerable regions—where many indirect impacts 
occur—remain underrepresented. The WED nexus brings these fragmented relationships 
into a unified frame. Looking forward, moving beyond isolated estimates to examine the 
complex interactions among AI infrastructure, global resource systems, and affected 
communities is essential to navigate the sustainability implications of the AI era.
