## ABSTRACT 

Mixture-of-Experts (MoE) architectures scale language models efficiently by ac-tivating only a few experts per token, but the saving is confined to computation: every expert must stay resident on the accelerator, so memory bounds where these models can be deployed. Expert pruning reduces this footprint, yet existing meth-ods are static — a single mask, calibrated offline, is applied to the model for every subsequent request. This assumption can fail when the workload is heterogeneous, most prominently in multi-agent systems, where one backbone serves many tasks and roles at once: our analysis shows that different tasks and roles recruit dif-ferent experts, while static methods assign one fixed subset to all of them. We therefore propose Dynamic Expert Pruning (DEP), which rests on a finding we establish here: an agent’s system and task prompts are by themselves sufficient to identify the experts that agent and its task require, since that text already describes what the agent will do. A lightweight predictor, trained once on workflow tran-scripts, turns those prompts into a specialized per-request mask in a single forward pass, with no per-configuration calibration. Across diverse tasks and roles, model scales, and MoE architectures, DEP achieves better overall accuracy than static pruning and merging baselines, and generalizes to workflows unseen in training without retraining. Its margin over those baselines is largest when few experts are retained, suggesting that the role specialization inherent to multi-agent systems permits sparser serving than static pruning allows. 

## 1 INTRODUCTION 

Scaling language models has increasingly meant scaling them sparsely rather than densely. AMixture-of-Experts (MoE) layer replaces a single feed-forward network with a pool of expert net-works and a router that activates only a few of them per token, so the parameter count can grow without a proportional growth in per-token computation (Shazeer et al., 2017; Fedus et al., 2022). The design now underpins the strongest open models (Jiang et al., 2024; DeepSeek-AI, 2024; Yang et al., 2025) and frontier proprietary systems alike (Gemini Team, Google, 2024). The saving it delivers, however, is in arithmetic, not memory: routing is re-decided at every token, so any expert may be needed at any step and the entire pool must stay resident on the accelerator — or be paged in at a latency cost. Memory, not compute, therefore often determines where an MoE model can be deployed. This asymmetry has made expert pruning an active line of work — removing the experts that contribute least (Lu et al., 2024; Lasby et al., 2026), or merging redundant ones (Chen et al., 2025) — with a shared, encouraging finding: a large fraction of the experts can be discarded at modest loss, provided the retained subset matches the workload the model will actually serve. Current methods simply assume that condition holds. Previous expert pruning methods are calibration-based: expert importance is averaged over a corpus taken to stand for future traffic, and the resulting static mask is frozen for every subsequent request. But a single backbone increasingly serves many prompting contexts at once — most prominently multi-agent systems, which assign it different tasks and agentic roles through prompts alone. Static calibration answers this diversity by finding one uniform, best-on-average set of experts; we argue that different tasks and roles may require different experts, so a single averaged subset ends up fitted to none of them. Our analysis bears this out. Static pruning methods that deliver on single-workload benchmarks lose much of their accuracy in settings that mix tasks and roles, multi-agent systems chief among them, 1arXiv:2610.02951v1 [cs.LG] 2 Oct 2026 and the loss has a mechanism: agents in different roles and on different tasks recruit measurably different experts, so preserving quality calls for an expert mask specialized to each agent and task. Specialization by calibration, however, scales poorly — one pass per configuration, redone whenever a prompt is edited, a role is added, or a workflow is re-searched. This motivates expert pruning that is dynamic : specialized to each request, without a calibration pass for each. This is precisely what we propose: Dynamic Expert Pruning (DEP) . Instead of calibrating a mask, DEP predicts it, with a lightweight predictor that learns which experts an agent on a given task will need from nothing but the agent’s system prompt and its task input. A central claim of this paper is that these prompts are by themselves sufficient : the system prompt specifies what the agent is and what it will do, and the task input specifies what knowledge doing it demands, so the text that configures an agent is enough on its own to identify its specialized expert set before a single token is generated — and our experiments bear this out, on agents and workflows the predictor never saw. The predictor is trained on transcripts of the backbone at work — supervised by which experts contributed to each output, and by how little the masked model’s distribution moves — and at serving time the mask is held fixed for the turn, preserving what makes static pruning deployable: a bounded resident expert set, reuse across a batch, untouched weights. Because it conditions on prompt text rather than a roster identifier, even agents and workflows never seen in training receive their masks in one shot — a single small forward pass on the system and task prompts, with no additional calibration. Empirically, DEP achieves better overall accuracy than static pruning and merging baselines across benchmarks, workflows, model scales, and MoE architectures, and it generalizes to configurations unseen in training; our analyses trace why. No single static criterion is uniformly worse — each wins somewhere — but each also collapses somewhere, and DEP is the only policy that does not. Its advantage is largest in the sparsest regime: as the budget tightens, a fixed subset must cover every context and ends up serving none well, while DEP spends the whole budget on the experts one request needs. These results suggest that specialization opens the door to sparser pruning — narrowing a mask to a single agent’s task and knowledge can let far fewer experts suffice. 

Contributions. 

• We analyze why static expert masks underperform in multi-agent systems — agents in different roles and on different tasks recruit different experts, so any single mask is fitted to none of them — motivating dynamic, per-agent expert masks. • We show that an agent’s system and task prompts are sufficient to identify the experts its task and role require, and build DEP on that finding: a mask predictor that reads only those prompts and emits a specialized expert mask on demand, in one shot , with no per-configuration calibration. • We validate DEP across six agentic workflows, unseen structures, families, benchmarks, and domains, on SWE-bench Verified at 30B and 480B scale, and on a different MoE ar-chitecture, where it achieves the best overall accuracy among pruning policies and separates from them by a large margin in the sparsest regime — evidence that per-agent specialization can enable sparser serving. 

## 2 BACKGROUND AND MOTIVATION 

Static expert pruning scores each expert on a calibration corpus — by routing frequency, recon-struction error (Lu et al., 2024), or router-weighted activation magnitude (Lasby et al., 2026) — and freezes the resulting subset, or a merged version of it (Chen et al., 2025), for every future request; the implicit assumption is that the corpus represents the traffic. Figure 1 shows what happens when it does not. With the backbone, retention, and calibration data held fixed, the same static masks that track the dense model when serving a single agent lose a large fraction of its accuracy on a multi-agent workflow over the same tasks, while a mask chosen per agent does not. The reversal matters because the workflow is there to buy accuracy in the first place: unpruned, the multi-agent system scores above the single agent, yet under the same static masks it ends up below it — static pruning does not merely cost accuracy here, it cancels the advantage the workflow was adopted to gain. ## 3 METHODS 

3.1 PROBLEM FORMULATION 

We consider serving a frozen Mixture-of-Experts (MoE) language model M with L layers, each containing E routed experts of which the router activates the top-k per token. The workload is not a single task but a collection of multi-agent workflows {W 1, . . . , WM }: each workflow W =

{a1, . . . , a n} is a program over agents, where agent a is defined by a system prompt pa and is invoked one or more times while the workflow processes an input problem q. All agents of all workflows are served by the backbone M; they differ only in their prompts and intermediate context. Crucially, the set of workflows is open: workflows are edited, re-searched, and added over the life of a deployment, so a serving-time method cannot assume it has seen every configuration it will serve. Because the predictor reads prompt text rather than an identifier drawn from a fixed roster, nothing ties it to the agents it was trained on: any new prompt yields a mask, in one shot. 

Applying the mask. The mask is installed on the frozen backbone at the router: logits of masked experts are set to −∞ before the router’s softmax-and-top-k, so routing renormalizes over the K

kept experts and never selects a pruned one. Per-token top-k routing is otherwise unchanged, model weights are untouched, and we require K ≥ k so that routing remains well defined. This routing-level view is what accuracy evaluations use; the memory mechanism that realizes the saving is the serving runtime of Section 3.4. 3.3 PREDICTOR TRAINING 

The predictor is trained on workflow transcripts : each training workflow is executed by the dense backbone on a calibration split disjoint from evaluation, and every agent call contributes a triple (system prompt pa, input q, response r) attributed to the node that produced it. The resulting corpus pools all training workflows, so one predictor learns from the union rather than one artifact per 4configuration. The backbone stays frozen; only θ is trained. Inference. A request arrives as (agent prompt, input). The serving engine first obtains the request’s mask: one predictor forward if it is new, or a cache hit — masks are content-addressed by (pa, q ),so repeated calls of an agent on the same problem, and batches grouped by agent, reuse one mask. The backbone then decodes the entire turn under that mask: per-token top-k routing runs natively, restricted to the K kept experts. Nothing about the mask changes mid-turn, so the inner decoding loop is identical to serving a statically pruned model. Resident memory is the non-expert weights plus a ρ fraction of the experts — which dominate an MoE’s footprint, so memory shrinks nearly in proportion to ρ — and adapting to a new workflow, agent, or edited prompt costs one small predictor forward rather than the recalibration pass a static mask requires. 

Delta loading. The memory saving comes from keeping only the kept experts on the accelerator. Expert weights live as per-expert tensors in host memory; each layer holds a resident buffer of K

slots plus an index map from global expert ids to slots, and the router is masked to the resident set so an absent expert can never be selected. When the mask changes — typically at an agent hand-off — the engine copies only the set difference between the required and resident experts into the slots that evicted ones vacate, and updates the map; experts shared by consecutive masks never move. That delta stays small because the masks of agents in one workflow overlap substantially (Section 4.4), and it is largely hideable because the workflow’s topology is known in advance: the next agent’s mask can be predicted and its delta prefetched while the current turn is still decoding, and batching by agent amortizes whatever remains.

DEP does give up real accuracy against dense here — this is graceful degradation, not free compression — but the regime is informative in its own right: it suggests that a multi-agent decomposition is itself a form of specialization that can be exploited for efficiency, since an agent responsible for one narrow part of a task needs correspondingly fewer experts, and can therefore be served far more sparsely than a model expected to do everything. 4.3 GENERALIZATION TO UNSEEN WORKFLOWS AND BENCHMARKS 

Section 1 claimed that DEP masks new configurations in one shot, because nothing ties a prompt-conditioned predictor to the workflows it was trained on. We test that claim directly with a single universal predictor. Unlike the other experiments, which serve one discovered workflow per task, here the predictor is trained on transcripts from a pool of 32 discovered workflows spanning three tasks (full rosters in Appendix D), then evaluated without any retraining on a ladder of increasing distance from training: unseen structures of workflow families seen in training; an entire unseen workflow family ; unseen benchmarks served by unseen workflows (SVAMP (Patel et al., 2021), near the training math distribution; MBPP, far); and an unseen domain (MMLU). Every static baseline is calibrated once on the same training transcripts and reused unchanged at every rung — exactly the situation a static mask faces when the workload changes after calibration. The predictor transfers. With no retraining and no recalibration it is the strongest policy on most rungs at both retentions (Table 2), and at ρ=50% it comes close to the dense reference on several of them: a mask produced for a workflow the predictor has never seen is still a mask worth serving. This is the claim of Section 1 tested at its weakest point — the prompts here describe agents, structures, and in two rungs whole benchmarks and domains that were absent from training — and the prompts still carry enough to pick a usable expert set. Two things temper the result. Expert merging remains competitive while the budget is loose, leading on a minority of rungs at ρ=50% and on the unseen benchmarks in particular; averaging experts rather than committing to a subset is a reasonable hedge when the workload is unfamiliar and the budget can absorb it. And with 30 problems per cell the seed spread is not negligible: on a few rungs the gap between the best and second-best policy is smaller than a seed standard deviation, so the ordering across the ladder, rather than any individual cell, is what this table supports. How does the predictor actually spend its budget? Figure 3 looks inside the predicted masks, mea-suring the overlap of the kept-expert sets between benchmarks and between the agents of one team. ## 5 CONCLUSION 

Serving many agents from one backbone breaks the assumption expert pruning rests on: agents in different roles and on different tasks recruit measurably different experts, so a mask averaged over all of them is fitted to none, and pruning has to become dynamic rather than better calibrated. DEP makes it dynamic by prediction rather than by calibration: a small predictor, trained once on workflow transcripts and frozen thereafter, emits a per-request mask in a single forward pass. The observation we would most want carried forward is that the prompts already suffice — the system and task prompts that configure an agent are by themselves enough to identify the experts it will need, before it has generated a token and even when neither the agent nor its workflow appeared in training. Our experiments bear that out across workloads, model scales and MoE architectures, and our analyses trace the behavior to its cause: masks that specialize by task and by role, and a wrong mask that costs more in degenerate generation than a right one costs in expert swaps. The price is a single predictor training run, and how aggressively a given backbone tolerates pruning must still be measured rather than assumed (Appendix H). Role specialization, then, cuts both ways: the division of labor that multi-agent systems adopt for capability also narrows what each agent must know, and a narrower need is one that far fewer experts can serve. 9AI USE STATEMENT

## A RELATED WORK 

Static MoE expert compression. Static expert pruning scores experts on a calibration corpus and freezes the result: by routing frequency, by the reconstruction error of removing an expert (Lu et al., 2024), or by router-weighted activation magnitude (Lasby et al., 2026), a state-of-the-art static criterion; HC-SMoE (Chen et al., 2025) instead merges redundant experts by agglomerative clustering, trading peak accuracy for graceful degradation. All of these produce a single artifact fitted to the calibration distribution — the assumption that Section 2 shows failing in multi-agent serving — and all serve as baselines in Section 4. 

Input-conditioned sparsity. Closer to DEP are methods that condition sparsity on the input rather than on a corpus. Deja Vu (Liu et al., 2023b) exploits per-token contextual sparsity in dense models, predicting on the fly which attention heads and MLP neurons a token needs; the prediction is per token and per layer, so the resident weight set changes continuously. Instruction-following pruning (Hou et al., 2025), whose predictor design we adopt (Section 3.2), conditions a dense-model FFN mask on the instruction, but selects among sparsity patterns for a single-call setting and fine-tunes the backbone jointly with the predictor. DEP differs in granularity and setting: it masks routed experts of a frozen MoE backbone, holds the mask fixed for a whole agent turn so that the resident set is bounded and cacheable, is trained once on pooled multi-agent transcripts with a self-distillation objective (Eq. 5), and is evaluated on its central claim — one-shot masks for agents, workflows, and tasks never seen in training (Section 4.3) — which per-call sparsity selection does not address. 12 MoE offloading and serving systems. A complementary line keeps all experts but moves them: quantized expert offloading with LRU caching and speculative prefetch (Eliseev & Mazur, 2023), activation-aware request-level tracing for prefetching and caching (Xue et al., 2024), and pre-gating that computes the next layer’s expert set one layer ahead (Hwang et al., 2024). These systems chase per-token routing decisions reactively; DEP inverts the problem — the whole turn’s expert set is known before generation begins , so expert movement becomes a deterministic per-turn delta that can be prefetched at agent hand-offs (Section 3.4) rather than a per-token cache-miss stream. Weight quantization (Frantar et al., 2023; Lin et al., 2024b) reduces the bytes per expert rather than the number of resident experts; it is orthogonal to DEP and composable with it, as our FP8 480B experiments already illustrate. 

Multi-agent systems and their efficiency. Multi-agent frameworks assign the same backbone differentiated roles through prompting (Wu et al., 2024; Hong et al., 2024), and automated search extends this by discovering workflow programs (Zhang et al., 2025); whether such compositions beat a single well-prompted model is actively debated (Cemri et al., 2025; Kim et al., 2025; Jwalapuram et al., 2026), but either way the resulting traffic is heterogeneous, which is all DEP requires. Systems work on serving LLM applications optimizes scheduling and cross-request state — e.g. exposing inter-request dependencies to the engine (Lin et al., 2024a) — while leaving the model weights untouched; DEP addresses the orthogonal memory axis, deciding which experts must be resident for each agent’s turn, and could sit inside such engines. 

## B EXPERIMENTAL DETAILS 

Workflow discovery and structure. Per task we run the AFlow MCTS search (Zhang et al., 2025) with a Claude-Sonnet optimizer and the target backbone as executor, for up to 10 rounds, and select the best-scoring discovered workflow on the search validation split. The discovered workflows are genuinely heterogeneous multi-agent programs; e.g. the MATH workflow runs five parallel solvers, self-consistency ensembling, a programmatic cross-check that writes and executes code, and a ver-ifier (8 model calls per problem). ## C ABLATIONS : THE TRAINING RECIPE IS ROBUST AND UN -TUNED 

All ablations use a three-task subset of the main setting (MATH, HumanEval+, HotpotQA; discov-ered workflows, ρ=50% , n=50 held-out problems); every cell shares identical hyperparameters, and the sweeps were run after the main configuration was fixed — so the numbers in Section 4 are not the product of a hyperparameter search. 

Both loss terms are load-bearing. Over a 4×5 grid of distillation objectives × saliency crite-ria, no cell beat the shipped combination (Table 6). Removing the criterion term entirely ( α=1 ,pure self-distillation) collapses MATH from 76.0 to 30.0, while replacing the KD term with a plain masked language-modeling loss both degrades the mean and can produce degenerate predictors — the LM +norm cell decodes a single repeated token on 47/50 HotpotQA problems. Between these extremes the mixing weight is uncritical: α ∈ [0 , 0.75] varies MATH by ±3 points with no trend. 

The saliency criterion is already near-optimal. ## H LIMITATIONS 

This section expands the summary in Section 5. 

Training cost and its scope. DEP needs transcripts of the dense backbone running the training workflows, and one predictor per retention level — our ρ=50% and ρ=25% checkpoints differ in exactly one field, K. The cost is small (roughly 3 GPU-hours per level, Appendix B) and is paid once rather than per configuration, but it is a step static calibration does not require, and serving a new retention level means training a new predictor. 

The training recipe is heuristic and the gap to dense is open. Pruned accuracy still trails the dense workflow at both retentions: we claim graceful degradation under compression, not free com-pression. We do not believe the current recipe is near the ceiling. Its objective mixes a per-example saliency target with a self-distillation term in a fixed proportion, and the mask keeps a uniform top-K

in every layer; both are heuristic choices. The sweeps in Appendix C explore only a neighbourhood of that design — mixing weight, learning rate, annealing temperature and the choice of saliency criterion — and leave structurally different alternatives untouched, such as an objective that scores a whole candidate mask rather than experts individually, or a global budget allocated unevenly across layers in place of a fixed K per layer. Fine-grained expert pools are assumed, and few architectures are tested. Masking requires 

K ≥ k, so retention below k/E cannot be expressed at all, and just above it the router has little freedom left inside the kept set. Every backbone we study has E ≥ 128 routed experts; on coarse-grained MoEs with few experts per layer this constraint binds quickly and there are correspondingly few distinct masks to choose among, so we expect the benefit of per-request specialization to shrink as expert granularity coarsens. More broadly, our architectural coverage is thin: the backbones we evaluate span two model families, so architecture independence here is supported by example rather than established, and coarse-grained designs in particular remain untested. 

The workload must actually be heterogeneous. DEP’s advantage comes from agents that need different experts. A workflow whose nodes are near-identical gives the predictor little to specialize over, and DEP should then behave much like a static mask fitted to that one configuration. 

Scope of the evidence. Our multi-agent workloads are automatically discovered workflows, and our agentic-coding benchmark is run oracle-assisted; neither is a production deployment, whose prompts, tool use, and traffic mix could differ in ways we cannot anticipate here. The largest back-bone we study is 480B, so frontier-scale behavior is extrapolation. Validating DEP on production multi-agent systems and at frontier scale is the most useful next test. 

Measurement caveats. Several evaluations use small per-cell sample sizes — stated in Section 4.1 and in each table caption — so individual cells carry wide intervals and small per-cell differences should not be read as an ordering. MATH is subset-sensitive in our harness — dense accuracy varies substantially between random draws of the same pool — so MATH numbers are interpretable only against a dense reference measured on the same problems. The mask-overlap analysis of Section 4.4 is measured on hand-crafted agent teams rather than the discovered workflows of Table 1.
