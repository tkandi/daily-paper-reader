# ABSTRACT 

Large Language Model (LLM) inference has become the dominant workload in modern AI systems, requiring serving infrastructures to simultaneously maximize throughput while satisfying stringent latency Service-Level Objectives (SLOs). Since state-of-the-art LLMs exceed the compute and memory capacity of a single GPU, inference is commonly distributed across multiple GPUs us-ing tensor parallelism (TP), pipeline parallelism (PP), or hybrid parallelism (HB), which combines both approaches. However, selecting an appropriate parallelism strategy remains a challenging task because inference performance is governed by complex interactions among computation, inter-GPU communication, pipeline utilization, sequence length, batch size, and model architecture. Existing studies primarily rely on empirical evaluations or optimize specific serving components, offering limited analytical insights into the performance trade-offs among different parallelism strategies, particularly across the distinct prefill and decoding phases of inference. In this paper, we present a unified analytical framework for modeling distributed LLM inference under TP, PP, and HB. Our framework decomposes end-to-end inference latency into computation, inter-GPU communication, and pipeline bubble overhead, and derives analytical models that explic-itly characterize TP collective communication, PP point-to-point communication, and pipeline uti-lization as functions of hardware, model and workload characteristics. The proposed model further captures the fundamentally different execution characteristics of prefill and decoding, explaining why PP-dominant configurations favor compute-intensive prefill while TP configurations achieve lower decoding latency by eliminating pipeline bubbles. Extensive experiments using modern LLMs on multi-GPU platforms validate the model and confirm the fundamental trade-off between com-pute and communication of TP and PP across diverse workloads. Our framework provides practical insights for selecting efficient parallelism strategies and establishes a principled foundation for per-formance modeling and capacity planning in future LLM serving systems. 

# 1 Introduction 

Large Language Models (LLMs) have rapidly become a fundamental component of modern artificial intelligence (AI) applications, enabling conversational assistants, retrieval-augmented generation, code generation, and long-context reasoning systems. The rapid evolution of models from billions to hundreds of billions of parameters, together with the increasing adoption of long-context workloads, has introduced unprecedented computational and memory chal-lenges during inference. Unlike model training, where large batches and long-running executions allow systems to maximize accelerator utilization, LLM serving must simultaneously achieve high throughput and satisfy strict latency requirements, including time-to-first-token (TTFT) and inter-token latency (ITL), under highly dynamic and heteroge-

To overcome the computational and memory limitations of large models, modern LLM serving systems increasingly rely on distributed execution across multiple GPUs. Tensor Parallelism (TP), introduced by Megatron-LM [3], parti-tions transformer computations across devices by sharding model weights and intermediate activations, reducing the computation and memory requirements per GPU. Pipeline Parallelism (PP), explored in systems such as GPipe [4] and PipeDream [5], partitions model layers across devices and enables models that exceed the memory capacity of a single accelerator. More recently, hybrid parallelism (HB), combining TP and PP, has been adopted in large-scale de-ployments to balance computation, memory capacity, and communication overhead [6,7]. These parallelism strategies form the foundation of modern LLM inference frameworks, including TensorRT-LLM [8], vLLM [9], and NVIDIA Dynamo [10]. Despite extensive progress in distributed LLM serving, selecting an appropriate parallelism strategy remains challeng-ing because performance depends on complex interactions among computation, communication, memory bandwidth, KV-cache capacity, and workload characteristics. Existing evaluations typically compare parallelism strategies through empirical measurements under fixed model configurations, batch sizes, and hardware settings. While such evaluations provide valuable insights, they often fail to explain the underlying mechanisms that determine performance scalabil-ity. In practice, the dominant bottleneck of LLM inference can shift significantly across workloads: prefill execution may be limited mainly by matrix computation, whereas decoding may become constrained by memory bandwidth, KV-cache accesses, and repeated synchronization overhead [1, 2]. Consequently, conclusions obtained from a single workload configuration often do not generalize to different models, sequence lengths, or deployment environments. This challenge is further amplified by the fundamentally different characteristics of the prefill and decoding phases. During prefill, the system processes the entire input sequence simultaneously and benefits from large matrix opera-tions with high arithmetic intensity. In contrast, decoding generates tokens sequentially, requiring repeated execution of the model for every generated token and introducing frequent communication and synchronization operations. For example, TP reduces computation per GPU but introduces collective communication after transformer components, while PP reduces communication volume but suffers from pipeline bubbles when the amount of useful work is insuffi-cient to keep all stages occupied. Although recent serving systems employ advanced scheduling techniques, including continuous batching, KV-cache management, and phase-aware execution [1, 2, 11, 12], most systems still rely on a fixed parallelism configuration across prefill and decode phases. Therefore, understanding how parallelism overhead evolves across different inference phases remains an open challenge. In this work, we address this challenge by developing a unified analytical framework for modeling distributed LLM in-ference performance. Unlike prior studies that focus on empirical benchmarking or isolated optimization techniques, our framework captures the fundamental latency components introduced by TP, PP, and HB across both prefill and decode execution. Specifically, we model TP communication overhead using collective communication costs, char-acterize pipeline bubble effects for autoregressive inference, and integrate computation, communication, and pipeline overhead into a unified performance model parameterized by model architecture, sequence length, batch size, GPU configuration, and interconnect topology. Our goal is to provide a fundamental understanding of the scalability lim-its of distributed LLM inference and enable systematic reasoning about the trade-offs among different parallelism strategies. 

1.1 Related Works 

To enable efficient execution of LLMs, prior research has extensively explored distributed parallelism strategies that partition computation and memory requirements across multiple accelerators. TP, introduced in Megatron-LM [3], distributes the computation of transformer layers by partitioning weight matrices across GPUs and synchronizing intermediate activations through collective communication operations. While TP effectively reduces per-device com-putation and enables high throughput within tightly coupled GPU systems, its scalability is limited by frequent com-munication overhead introduced by synchronization operations. PP, investigated in systems such as GPipe [4] and PipeDream [5], addresses memory scalability by partitioning model layers across sequential pipeline stages. However, PP introduces pipeline bubbles during pipeline fill and drain periods, reducing hardware utilization when the amount of available parallel work is insufficient. To overcome the limitations of TP and PP, HB strategies combining TP, PP, and Expert Parallelism (EP) have been proposed for large-scale model deployment [6, 7, 13]. Although these im-plementations provide effective mechanisms for scaling model execution, they primarily focus on improving system scalability and implementation efficiency rather than developing a unified understanding of the performance trade-offs among different parallelism strategies. Beyond designing parallel execution techniques, analytical performance modeling has been extensively studied in high-performance computing and distributed deep learning to understand scalability limitations. Classical commu-2nication models, including the Hockney α − β model [14] and the LogP model [15], characterize communication latency as a combination of fixed startup overhead and bandwidth-dependent transmission cost. These models have been widely adopted for analyzing collective communication operations such as AllReduce and AllGather, including implementations provided by libraries such as NVIDIA collective communications library (NCCL) [16]. Similarly, the roofline model [17] provides a framework for identifying whether applications are limited by computation throughput or memory bandwidth. Recent distributed deep learning systems have extended these ideas to analyze communica-tion and computation overlap in large-scale training workloads. However, existing analytical models are primarily designed for traditional HPC applications or distributed training scenarios and do not capture the unique characteris-tics of autoregressive LLM inference, where TP communication occurs repeatedly during decoding, pipeline bubbles depend on inference workload characteristics, and performance varies significantly between prefill and decode phases. From serving perspectives, numerous techniques have been introduced, including continuous batching, KV-cache man-agement, memory optimization, request scheduling, and workload-aware execution. Frameworks such as vLLM [2], TensorRT-LLM [8], Sarathi-Serve [1], DistServe [11], Splitwise [12], Mooncake [18], and NVIDIA Dynamo [10] have demonstrated significant improvements in practical LLM deployment by optimizing resource utilization and re-ducing serving latency. In parallel, several studies have proposed analytical approaches to model inference latency based on GPU utilization, memory bandwidth, KV-cache behavior, and workload characteristics. However, existing efforts generally focus on either improving serving efficiency empirically or modeling individual performance com-ponents in isolation. To the best of our knowledge, there is no unified analytical framework that jointly captures TP communication overhead, PP bubble effects, and their hybrid execution across both prefill and decode phases. This limitation prevents a fundamental understanding of how parallelism choices affect LLM inference scalability across different models, workloads, and hardware configurations, which motivates the analytical framework developed in this work backed by experiment results. 

# 2 Parallelism Strategies 

Existing LLM serving frameworks primarily employ three parallelism strategies: TP, PP, and HB, where HB com-bines TP and PP. Each strategy exposes a fundamentally different trade-off between computation, communication, and hardware utilization. TP partitions the computation of individual transformer layers across multiple GPUs, which enables concurrent execution at the cost of frequent collective communication and synchronization. In contrast, PP partitions the model layers across GPUs. It effectively reduces the inter-GPU communication to point-to-point acti-vation transfers while introducing pipeline bubbles due to pipeline fill and drain effects. HB seeks to balance these trade-offs by combining TP within each pipeline stage and PP across stages, thereby reducing the limitations of either approach alone. Consequently, the relative efficacy of these strategies depends on multiple factors, including batch size, sequence length, model architecture, and GPU topology. This motivates a detailed analysis of their computation, communication, and pipeline characteristics. The following sections describe each parallelism strategy and develop analytical models that characterize their latency behavior during both the prefill and decoding phases. 

2.1 Tensor Parallelism 

Tensor Parallelism is one of the most widely adopted techniques for scaling LLM inference across multiple GPUs. As shown in Fig. 1, TP partitions the weight matrices of each transformer layer across multiple GPUs, allowing the computation of large matrix multiplications to be distributed among participating devices. This partitioning reduces the amount of work performed by each GPU and enables larger models to be served efficiently. However, because each GPU computes only a portion of the layer output, intermediate results must be exchanged and synchronized through collective communication operations. Consequently, TP introduces a fundamental trade-off: increasing the TP degree reduces computation per GPU but increases communication between GPUs. Because transformer decoding performs these collective operations repeatedly for ev-ery layer and every generated token, the efficiency of inter-GPU communication directly affects the scalability of TP for inference. Consequently, accurately modeling communication latency is essential for understanding the trade-off between reduced computation and increased synchronization overhead as TP degree increases. In Megatron-style TP [3], AllReduce is a dominant communication operation, where partial activations computed on different GPUs must be synchronized after the attention and feed-forward blocks of every transformer layer. Al-though TP reduces the computational workload assigned to each GPU, it introduces these recurring synchronization events that can significantly impact inference performance. Specifically, each transformer layer performs two acti-vation synchronization steps, and these collective operations are executed for every layer and every generated token during decoding. As the TP degree increases, the amount of computation executed by each GPU decreases; while the synchronization overhead increases, which makes communication an increasingly important component of the overall latency budget. Our profiling results in Section 3.2 show that, despite the reduced per-GPU message size at higher TP degrees, inter-GPU communication can account for 30–40% of the end-to-end decoding latency. 2.2 Pipeline Parallelism 

Pipeline Parallelism adopts a fundamentally different approach from TP by partitioning the transformer layers across multiple GPUs rather than splitting individual layer computations. As shown in Fig. 2, each GPU is responsible for an LPP contiguous subset of layers, and activations are forwarded from one stage to the next as execution progresses through the model. This approach significantly reduces the collective communication overhead associated with TP synchronization, as GPUs primarily exchange activations only between neighboring pipeline stages. Furthermore, PP enables serving larger models by distributing model weights across multiple devices. However, these benefits come at the cost of a performance inefficiency referred to as a pipeline bubble. Since different pipeline stages must wait for activations to arrive before computation can begin, some stages may remain idle during pipeline startup and drain phases, resulting in underutilized hardware resources. The magnitude of this idle time depends on both the pipeline depth and the number of batches available to keep the pipeline occupied. 3.2 Tensor Parallelism Scaling Characteristics Communication Characterization: In Fig. 3, we plot T comm TP per token vs batch size for various TP degrees. For fixed TP, NCCL has three competing operating regions that govern the behavior of TP. At small batch sizes, and according to (5), the fixed communication latency term α dominates the overall communication cost. In this regime, the amount of data being exchanged is relatively small, and most of the communication time is spent initiating and coordinating collective operations rather than transferring payload data. As a result, increasing the batch size has only a limited impact on communication latency, since the fixed startup overhead is already the primary contributor. The system therefore operates in a latency-bound regime where communication cost is largely insensitive to message size. As the batch size increases, the contribution of data transfer begins to grow and communication enters a transition region. In this regime, both α and the bandwidth-dependent transfer cost contribute meaningfully to T comm TP . Con-sequently, communication latency increases with batch size, but not yet in a strictly linear manner. The system is gradually shifting from being dominated by synchronization overhead toward being constrained by data movement. This region is particularly important because it often corresponds to the operating range of production serving systems, where both latency and throughput considerations must be balanced. At sufficiently large batch sizes, α (i.e., startup latency) becomes negligible compared to the time required to move data across the interconnect. The communication cost is then dominated by the bandwidth term, causing latency to scale approximately linearly with message size. In this bandwidth-bound regime, further increases in batch size directly translate into higher communication overhead, and the achievable performance becomes limited by the ef-fective inter-GPU bandwidth rather than synchronization latency. Effective Bandwidth: Figure 4a illustrates the achieved interconnect bandwidth during the prefill vs batch size for different TP degrees. Although the A100 NVLink interconnect provides a theoretical peak bandwidth of approxi-mately 600 GB/s, the measured bandwidth never approaches this limit and instead saturates at roughly 250 GB/s. This behavior reflects the fact that collective communication performance is constrained by more than the raw link bandwidth. In particular, AllReduce operations require synchronization among all participating GPUs, kernel launch overhead, protocol processing, and reduction operations before data transmission can fully utilize the interconnect. During prefill, the communicated activation tensors are relatively large (i.e., S = ISL ), allowing bandwidth utiliza-tion to increase rapidly with batch size. However, once the communication pipeline becomes saturated, additional increases in message size provide little improvement in effective bandwidth, resulting in the observed plateau. Inter-estingly, higher TP degrees consistently achieve higher bandwidth utilization. To explain this, we note that although the case where PTP = 8 communicates fewer bytes per GPU due to tensor partitioning, the collective involves more participating GPUs and therefore generates a larger aggregate communication volume across the NVLink fabric. The communication subsystem consequently remains active longer to accommodate the communication among more GPU participants, amortizing startup costs and enabling the interconnect to operate closer to its sustainable throughput than the cases of PTP = 2 and PTP = 4 .During decoding, as shown in Fig.4b, the achieved bandwidth is substantially lower, saturating at only approximately 150 GB/s, or about 25% of the theoretical NVLink bandwidth. Consequently, each of AllReduce oper-ation transfers significantly smaller messages, causing communication to remain dominated by startup latency and synchronization overhead rather than bulk data movement. The smaller message sizes also delay the transition into the bandwidth-bound regime, explaining why the bandwidth plateau occurs at much larger batch sizes than during prefill. 3.4 Hybrid Parallelism: Balancing Communication and Pipeline Bubbles 

In this section, we provide experiment results on HB. The results illustrate a complementary trade-off between compute and communication of TP and PP during both prefill and decode. Decoding is dominated by pipeline utilization because only one token is generated per request (i.e., S = 1 ), whereas prefill is dominated by computation and communication due to the long input sequence (i.e., S = ISL ). With HB, we would like to understand how much of this trade-off can be balanced out. Throughout the experiments we assume a fixed number of GPU resources (i.e., 

8 GPUs), and consider the following 4 parallelism configurations: TP8 representing pure tensor parallelism across GPUs, PP8 for pure pipeline parallelism, PP2TP4 for HB of 2 pipeline stages with 4 GPUs in each stage and PP4TP2 representing 4 pipeline stages with 2 GPUs in each stage. During decoding, as shown in Fig. 8a, ITL consistently decreases with increasing TP degree because eliminating pipeline bubbles outweighs the additional TP collective communication. We also observe that the PP-dominant con-figurations (i.e., PP8 and PP4TP2), especially at small batch sizes, the idle latency contributes a noticeable fraction of the ITL. However, bubble gradually disappears as the decode batch size increases. To explain this, we note that during decoding ( S = 1 ), the amount of work entering the pipeline is simply W = B. Consequently, for small decode batch sizes, the number of work units is insufficient to fully occupy all pipeline stages, resulting in a significant pipeline bubble as shown in (19). As the batch size increases, the amount of work grows proportionally while the fill/drain overhead remains fixed at PPP − 1 stages. Therefore, the bubble fraction rapidly decreases, allowing all pipeline stages to remain continuously occupied, which leads to vanishing pipeline bubble as the decode batch size increases. Another important observation in Fig. 8a is that the bubble becomes progressively smaller as the execution shifts from PP-dominant to TP-dominant configurations (i.e., from PP8 to TP8). This behavior follows directly from (18), since the bubble latency is proportional to the pipeline depth. Reducing the PPP decreases the number of fill/drain intervals and therefore shortens the idle latency. In the limiting case of TP8 (i.e., PPP = 1 ), the pipeline consists of a sin-gle stage, eliminating fill/drain entirely, resulting in zero pipeline bubble. # 4 Conclusion 

Distributed execution has become essential for serving modern LLMs, yet understanding the performance trade-offs among TP, PP, and HB remains a fundamental challenge. In this paper, we presented a unified analytical framework for modeling distributed LLM inference by decomposing end-to-end latency into computation, inter-GPU communica-tion, and pipeline bubble overhead. We derived analytical models for TP collective communication, PP point-to-point communication, and pipeline utilization, capturing their dependence on batch size, sequence length, hidden dimen-sion, message size, and parallelism degree. Unlike existing empirical studies, our framework provides a principled explanation of how these factors jointly determine inference performance across both the prefill and decoding phases. Our analysis and experimental evaluation demonstrate that the proposed framework accurately captures the contrast-ing behavior of different parallelism strategies, explaining why PP is more effective for the compute-intensive prefill phase, whereas TP achieves lower decoding latency by eliminating pipeline bubbles despite higher communication overhead. By providing a unified understanding of the compute, communication and bubble latency trade-offs, the proposed model enables systematic evaluation of parallelism strategies across diverse models, workloads, and hard-ware configurations. We believe this framework provides a practical foundation for performance modeling, capacity planning, and the design of future distributed LLM serving systems that can adapt parallelism strategies to evolving workload characteristics.
