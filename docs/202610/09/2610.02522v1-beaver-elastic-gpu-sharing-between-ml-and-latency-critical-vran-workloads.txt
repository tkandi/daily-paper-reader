# Beaver: Elastic GPU Sharing between ML and Latency-Critical vRAN Workloads 

# Yuncheng Yao 1, Zhenzhou Qi 2, Junyao Zheng 2, Chung-Hsuan Tung 2,Danyang Zhuo 1, Tingjun Chen 2

Departments of Computer Science 1 and Electrical & Computer Engineering 2, Duke University 

Abstract 

Within the shared industry vision of AI-RAN, AI-and-RAN seeks to co-locate virtualized radio access network (vRAN) workloads and AI services on shared GPUs. This sharing is inherently asymmetric: vRAN workload is latency-critical, whereas the machine learning (ML) workload is a throughput-oriented, best-effort co-tenant. We present Beaver, a GPU sharing system that jointly manages compute and memory resources while protecting the vRAN’s strict processing dead-line. Beaver sizes the vRAN’s streaming multiprocessor (SM) allocation from each slot’s scheduled workload, repartitions SM allocations at slot granularity, and rewrites compiled ML kernels to yield HBM bandwidth during the vRAN’s memory-critical phases. We implement Beaver and evaluate it using NVIDIA Aerial with heterogeneous multi-cell workloads, real-world cellular traces, production inference kernels, and full-stack LLM serving. On an H200 GPU, Beaver keeps the vRAN’s p99.9 latency within its 1.5 ms uplink deadline while retaining 74% of Llama-3.3-70B serving throughput. It also incurs no observed deadline misses under replayed cellular traces, protects a 375 μs downlink deadline, and generalizes to other GPUs including A100, GB10 and GH200. 

1 Introduction 

Co-locating virtualized radio access network (vRAN) work-loads and AI services on shared GPU infrastructure is be-coming an industry-wide objective with the AI-RAN vi-sion [4, 18]. NVIDIA Aerial makes GPU-based 5G layer-1 (L1) processing practical [23], motivating recent systems such as YinYangRAN [15] and RAN-LLM [43] to explore co-locating radio workloads (e.g., vRAN baseband processing) with ML workloads (e.g., LLM inference serving) on the same GPU. This sharing is inherently asymmetric : the latency-critical vRAN workload is the higher-priority tenant, whereas the AI service is a best-effort co-tenant that may use only the GPU resources that can be safely spared without violating the strict vRAN processing deadline. The central challenge is therefore to guarantee vRAN’s latency while preserving as much co-tenant throughput as possible. Despite this opportunity, existing GPU sharing systems cannot simultaneously preserve the latency-critical vRAN deadline and reclaim spare capacity efficiently. Recent GPU sharing designs for vRAN co-tenancy [15, 43] rely primarily on spatial GPU partitions through NVIDIA multi-instance GPU (MIG) [20] or multi-process service (MPS) [21] with two key limitations. First, SM partitioning isolates compute resources but not the shared L2 cache and HBM fabric (Fig. 1). Consequently, although disjoint SMs eliminate compute con-tention, a bandwidth-intensive ML co-tenant (e.g., LLM de-coding) can still contend with and delay vRAN’s memory-bound kernels. Second, existing SM partitions are mostly static and cannot be reconfigured at the radio slot timescale of 500 μs. The required SM budget varies from slot to slot (Fig. 2(a)) [6, 9], whereas reconfiguring MIG or MPS takes milliseconds to seconds (Table 1, §6.3). Achieving elastic, efficient, and safe GPU sharing between vRAN and ML workloads raises three challenges. First, the system must determine, for dynamic and heterogeneous multi-cell workloads, the minimum vRAN SM budget re-quired to meet the strict processing deadline. Second, it must repartition the SMs between the vRAN and its co-tenant at sub-slot timescales. Third, it must regulate the HBM band-width demand of opaque, compiled ML kernels without ac-cess to their source code or disrupting their synchronization. We present Beaver 1, a GPU sharing system that addresses these challenges through three mechanisms operating at complementary timescales (Fig. 2(c)). 1 For each slot, a 

workload-aware SM sizer predicts the smallest SM allocation that can complete the scheduled workload before its dead-line (§4.1). 2 Across slots, slot-granular SM repartitioning 

applies the selected SM allocation by switching the vRAN to the desired CUDA green context [16] within only microsec-onds (§4.2). 3 Within each slot, a cooperative HBM bandwidth guard rewrites the co-tenant’s compiled kernels at the par-allel thread execution (PTX) level, without requiring their source code, to poll a shared flag and back off whenever the vRAN workload signals a memory-critical phase. HBM access is therefore coordinated between the workloads over time rather than divided into fixed bandwidth slices (§4.3). We implement Beaver as a GPU sharing layer comprising a runtime that interfaces with Aerial to size and switch SM partitions at slot granularity, and a compile-time rewriter that instruments both tenants’ kernels at the PTX level (§5). We evaluate Beaver on an NVIDIA H200 using the Aerial uplink pipeline (§6). Co-tenants include single-axis stressors; production inference kernels such as general matrix multi-ply (GEMM), FlashAttention-3, and fused mixture of experts (MoE); and a full-stack vLLM [12] service running Llama-3.3-70B and Mistral-Small-24B. With 13 memory-heavy vRAN cells and LLM serving co-tenants, Beaver keeps vRAN’s p99.9 latency within the deadline while retaining 74% of the Llama-3.3-70B serving throughput. Under two real-world cellular traces [6, 9] capturing changes in cell configuration and traffic intensity, Beaver adapts the SM allocation every slot, incurs no deadline misses, and sustains LLM serving. Beaver also eliminates observed deadline misses with co-located production inference kernels, protects a 375 μs down-link processing deadline, and generalizes to A100, GB10 and GH200 GPUs while retaining 78%-85% of vLLM throughput. 2 Background 

5G vRAN baseband and GPU-accelerated L1. The physi-cal layer (L1) converts coded transport blocks into downlink radio symbols and recovers them from uplink symbols. These operations traditionally run on dedicated FPGAs or ASICs; software-defined basebands use commodity CPUs and GPUs, enabling flexible updates and compute allocation as configu-rations and load change. NVIDIA Aerial implements 5G L1 using GPU libraries and CUDA kernels [23]. A kernel com-prises thread blocks assigned to SMs [24]. Multiple blocks can share an SM when resources permit; L1 latency therefore depends on SM occupancy, memory traffic, and specialized execution units. Concurrent ML kernels can contend for these resources even across different CUDA streams. 

Uplink/downlink processing and hard deadline. In 5G, the physical downlink shared channel (PDSCH) carries user data from the base station to a user equipment (UE). Its transmit pipeline performs cyclic redundancy check (CRC) attachment, low-density parity-check (LDPC) encoding, rate matching, modulation, MIMO precoding, resource mapping, and orthogonal frequency-division multiplexing (OFDM) waveform generation. Conversely, the physical uplink shared channel (PUSCH) receive pipeline transforms incoming I/Q samples, estimates channel and noise, equalizes MIMO layers, produces log-likelihood ratios (LLRs), and performs de-rate matching, LDPC decoding, and CRC checking [1]. PUSCH is generally more compute-intensive : reception compensates for a time-varying channel, and LDPC decoding may re-quire multiple iterations. We therefore use PUSCH as the representative protected vRAN workload. Fig. 1 shows the eight stages of NVIDIA’s Aerial L1 uplink processing, where missing a slot’s deadline can trigger retransmission and con-sume additional air-interface capacity; safe GPU sharing must therefore protect tail latency. Following 3GPP timing constraints [2], we target a 1.5 ms uplink deadline. 

GPU resources: SM and HBM. A GPU groups execution into SMs [19] (see Fig. 1), and places kernel blocks on avail-able SMs in successive waves when they cannot all reside concurrently; a running block remains on its SM until com-pletion [7, 24], and a kernel waits when all SMs are full. 3.3 Design Challenges 

Safe and efficient vRAN co-tenancy requires both dynamic SM allocation and protection from contended HBM band-width, posing the following design challenges (see Fig. 4(d)).  

> C1

Partitioning SMs for heterogeneous vRAN work-loads. Cell count alone cannot determine the vRAN’s com-pute demand: heterogeneous cells require different SM bud-gets. Mapping each slot’s workload to a deadline-safe SM partition is challenging because GPU performance does not scale linearly with the allocated SM count, and kernels differ in grid size, occupancy, synchronization, and memory behav-ior. On Hopper GPUs, GC exposes partitions in increments of eight SMs, and Beaver must map dynamic workloads onto these discrete choices and select the smallest SM partition that meets the vRAN deadline. Allocating too few SMs causes deadline misses, while allocating too many unnecessarily reduces the resources available to the co-tenant.  

> C2

Adaptive SM partition at slot granularity. vRAN workload can change substantially from one slot to the next at the sub-millisecond level, making a static partition either unsafe under peak load or wasteful under light load. Beaver must therefore resize the vRAN partition at per-slot granu-larity while ensuring that every slot meets its hard deadline. However, existing GPU partitioning mechanisms such as MIG and MPS are not designed for such rapid adaptation (see §2). GC provides SM isolation, but the SM assignments are fixed when the context is created and cannot be resized in place. Changing a partition therefore requires creating or switching contexts and waiting for work in the old parti-tion to drain before its SMs can be reclaimed, making direct reconfiguration too expensive for sub-millisecond control.  

> C3

Controlling opaque ML co-tenants. On the other hand, ML workloads typically invoke kernels from vendor libraries, packaged frameworks, and runtime generators (e.g., Triton). These kernels are opaque to the application and often employ asynchronous copies and warp-specialized synchro-nization, making source modification impractical and poten-tially unsafe. Since SM partitioning alone—even through the use of GC—does not isolate the shared HBM subsystem, the vRAN workload’s memory demand can vary sharply across slots, while concurrent ML kernels can saturate HBM band-width and queues during these critical phases. Once issued, these memory requests cannot be recalled, so coarse-grained or reactive throttling cannot protect vRAN’s tail latency. Beaver must therefore regulate ML co-tenant’s execution transparently at sub-slot granularity, preserve kernel seman-tics, and protect the vRAN deadline without unnecessarily reducing ML co-tenant’s throughput. 

4 Beaver’s Design 

Beaver addresses these challenges by combining deadline-driven vRAN resource provisioning with low-overhead SM repartitioning and cooperative control of co-tenant memory traffic. We focus on uplink processing because its iterative LDPC decoding makes it substantially more intensive than downlink processing (see §2). Beaver comprises three func-tional modules, illustrated in Fig. 2 and described below.

4.2 Slot-Granular SM Repartitioning 

Beaver must adapt the SM allocation at slot granularity (0.5 ms in numerology 1) without violating the processing deadline, which cannot be achieved by existing SM parti-tion mechanisms such as MIG and MPS (see §3.3 and Fig. 5). CUDA GC provides finer-grained SM isolation, but its re-sources are immutable after creation [16]; creating one on H200 takes ≈1.6 ms, already exceeding the slot duration. Beaver addresses this mismatch with a slot-granular SM repartitioning module that separates slow SM partition con-struction from fast runtime selection, as shown in Fig. 5( bot-tom ). At startup, it pre-materializes a pool of complementary GC pairs, one context for the vRAN and one for the ML co-tenant. Each GC pair represents a supported split 𝑠 :(𝑀 − 𝑠 ),where 𝑀 is the GPU’s total SM count and 𝑠 is the vRAN allocation. 4.3 Cooperative HBM Bandwidth Guard 

SM partitioning does not partition the GPU’s HBM subsys-tem or bandwidth. A memory-intensive ML co-tenant can therefore delay the vRAN workload even when the two work-loads execute on disjoint SMs. Beaver addresses this inter-ference in the HBM domain with an asymmetric, cooperative HBM bandwidth guard module: the latency-critical vRAN workload announces short intervals in which its latency depends critically on HBM bandwidth, during which the 

best-effort co-tenant defers new memory traffic. 

Cooperative bitmap protocol. Fig. 6(a) shows the bitmap shared by the two tenants, b = [𝑏 1, . . . , 𝑏 𝑁 𝑏 ]. Each bit repre-sents one vRAN thread block, and 𝑁 𝑏 is sized for the maxi-mum number of thread blocks that can overlap in the vRAN pipeline. Profile-guided bitmap signaling. Protecting every vRAN kernel for its entire execution would preserve the radio deadline but unnecessarily suppress ML progress. Because the vRAN workload follows a fixed, slot-periodic pipeline, Beaver profiles each kernel’s time-resolved HBM demand offline and assigns it one of the three signaling schedules, as illustrated in Fig. 7. First, kernels such as channel estimation, CRC, noise estimation, channel filtering, and equalization generate relatively little HBM traffic, therefore requiring 

no protection and the ML co-tenant runs without interrup-tion. Second, streaming kernels such as soft demapping and rate de-matching sustain high HBM demand throughout ex-ecution. They set their bit at kernel entry and clear it at kernel exit, thereby receiving whole-kernel protection . While the bit is set, the co-tenant defers new HBM-intensive re-gions; HBM-light computation may continue. Third, LDPC decoding exhibits a bursty, wave-structured traffic pattern. As Fig. 7 shows, each execution wave contains an HBM-intensive peak followed by an HBM-light valley. A wave first reads LLRs from HBM, performs belief propagation with little HBM traffic, and then writes its decoded bits. For such a kernel, whole-kernel protection would also block the ML 

7tenant during the long belief propagation valleys. Beaver instead applies wave-aware protection : it sets the bitmap dur-ing each wave’s reads and writes but clears it during belief propagation so that the co-tenant can make progress. 

Injecting the guard without source. ML co-tenants com-monly invoke vendor GEMM, attention, and projection ker-nels whose CUDA source is unavailable. Beaver therefore rewrites kernels with available PTX (see §2) and substitutes the rewritten kernels at load time. Fig. 6 shows this five-stage pipeline: Stages 1–4 run once offline for each targeted ker-nel, while Stage 5 activates the rewritten kernel at load time. The vRAN workload profiling determines when protection is needed; this pipeline determines where the ML co-tenant should pause before issuing more HBM traffic. Specifically: (1) Attribute HBM traffic : PTX identifies instructions that ac-cess memory, but does not show how often they execute or how much HBM traffic they generate. Beaver profiles the unmodified kernel and attributes its measured HBM traffic to individual PTX lines, and the results identify the code that contributes most to the kernel’s HBM demand. (2) Select HBM-intensive regions : Polling before every mem-ory instruction would add excessive overhead. Beaver instead groups PTX lines into basic blocks —straight-line code regions with one entry and no internal branches [3]. It sums the traffic profiled from Stage 1 within each block and selects the smallest set of blocks that covers a con-figured fraction of the kernel’s HBM traffic. As a result, a small set of useful candidate locations is identified, at each of which Stage 3 inserts a safe gate. (3) Insert a safe polling gate : Beaver cannot use the same gate placement everywhere since GPU kernels organize their memory-producing threads differently. We call a gate safe when every thread that can issue the selected memory batch encounters it, without requiring unrelated threads to participate. Beaver classifies each selected region by its execution structure and chooses the corre-sponding gate. If the shared bitmap is clear, the gated threads issue the next memory batch; otherwise, they briefly back off and check again. (4) Build a drop-in GPU binary : Beaver reassembles the rewrit-ten PTX into a GPU binary using the original target ar-chitecture and compiler settings. The kernel retains the same arguments and launch interface. (5) Redirect and bind : At load time, a runtime shim redirects each targeted kernel to its rewritten binary and maps the shared bitmap into both tenants’ address spaces. The injected poll is thereby bound to the vRAN signal without changing the ML co-tenant’s framework or application.

Implementation and hardware platforms. We build Beaver on NVIDIA Aerial CUDA-accelerated RAN [23] (re-lease 25-3); our implementation adds 8.4K lines of code across Beaver’s three modules. Table 2 summarizes the hardware platforms used in our experimental evaluation. Most experi-ments are conducted on server #1 ( srv1 ) with an H200 (Hop-per) GPU, and our cross-GPU study considers server #2 ( srv2 )with an A100 (Ampere) GPU, a DGX Spark with a GB10 (Grace-Blackwell) Superchip, and a GH200 (Grace Hopper) Superchip. For a GPU with 𝑀 SMs, we use an 𝑠 :(𝑀 − 𝑠 )

SM split to denote an allocation of 𝑠 SMs to vRAN and the remaining (𝑀 − 𝑠 ) SMs to the co-tenant. 

vRAN test vectors and vRAN workloads. We consider a total of 28 cell configurations, or test vectors , summarized in Appendix A (Table 4), in 5G numerology 1 with 30 kHz sub-carrier spacing and 0.5 ms slot duration. To isolate compute and memory interference, we focus on a compute-heavy test vector ( tv-21 ) with the highest per-slot processing cost and a memory-heavy test vector ( tv-28 ) with the highest per-cell DRAM traffic. Both configurations use a 100 MHz channel with 273 physical resource blocks (PRBs) and four anten-nas, but differ in the MCS. Because tv-21 and tv-28 place the greatest observed pressure on the target GPU resources, they provide conservative stress tests for Beaver. 

Latency profiling and modeling. We derive the latency model in §4.1 offline using homogeneous vRAN uplink work-loads, in which all cells scheduled in a slot have the same configuration. For each of the 28 test vectors, we sweep six cell counts and six vRAN SM budgets, yielding 1,008 config-urations. We run each workload for 3,000 slots and use the resulting end-to-end latency measurements to fit the latency model coefficients and calibrate the p99.9 safety reserve, as described in §4.1. At runtime, Beaver composes the fitted coefficients to predict the tail (p99.9) latency of vRAN work-loads with heterogeneous cells and selects the minimum SM allocation that satisfies the deadline. Appendix A provides further details on the profiling and calibration procedure. 

Co-tenant workloads. We organize the co-tenant work-loads into three classes that provide progressively greater realism. First, the single-axis stress-test kernels isolate the two principal sources of GPU contention: FMA (fused multiply-add) Burn generates controllable compute pressure through a tunable kernel duration, whereas HBM Flooder streams 

> 8

data past the caches to generate sustained memory band-width pressure. Their controlled and independently tunable resource demands allow us to evaluate the effectiveness of in-dividual modules of Beaver. Second, the production inference kernels , including CUTLASS GEMM [17], FlashAttention-3 [31], Triton fused MoE [37], and FlashInfer [42], repre-sent common building blocks of modern AI inference, cover-ing dense matrix multiplication, self-attention, expert feed-forward computation, and paged attention. They expose real-istic combinations of compute and memory demand while re-maining sufficiently focused to explain kernel-level interfer-ence. Finally, the full-stack serving workloads use vLLM [12] to decode tokens with Llama-3.3-70B [14] and Mistral-Small-24B. These workloads combine compute- and bandwidth-intensive kernels with the scheduling, memory management, and runtime behavior of an end-to-end LLM serving stack. 

Baseline configurations. We evaluate Beaver using both controlled mechanism-level microbenchmarks and compar-isons with prior GPU sharing systems. We focus on the fol-lowing baseline configurations: 

• vRAN only , as a contention-free reference, runs the vRAN workload in its assigned GC partition without a co-tenant ;

• GC only assigns the vRAN and co-tenant disjoint SM par-titions via GC but disables the Cooperative HBM Bandwidth Guard , thereby leaving HBM bandwidth shared. 

• Beaver uses the same SM split enabled by GC and addi-tionally enables the cooperative HBM bandwidth guard. To microbenchmark Beaver’s protection mechanisms, we in-clude two further ablations: Unprotected co-schedules both workloads on the same SMs without either protection mech-anism, whereas HBM guard only enables the cooperative HBM bandwidth guard without GC-based SM isolation. 

Performance metrics. For the vRAN workload, we mea-sure per-slot GPU execution time from device-side traces collected with NVIDIA Nsight Systems [22]. For each exper-iment, we collect per-slot processing latency measurements over 50,000 slots, from which the processing latency distri-bution, including the median (p50) and tail (p99.9), is derived. We also report the deadline miss rate , defined as the fraction of slots violating the 1.5 ms processing deadline for the uplink (3-slot in numerology 1). For production inference kernels, we report throughput as the number of completed kernel executions per second (kernels/s). For full-stack serving ap-plications using vLLM, we report the serving throughput as the number of output tokens produced per second (tok/s).

7 Related Work 

Baseband processing and virtualization. Sora [35] demon-strated high-performance wireless processing on general-purpose CPUs, and BigStation [41], Agora [5, 26], and Hy-dra [8] scaled software MIMO processing within and across servers. Savannah [28, 29] and Nexus [27] explore heteroge-neous CPU–eASIC processing, while Intel FlexRAN, srsRAN, and OpenAirInterface demonstrate vRAN on commodity in-frastructure [11, 25, 33]. These systems make software L1 feasible and scalable, but do not protect a latency-critical GPU L1 pipeline from concurrent ML workloads. 

GPU-accelerated L1. Prior work accelerates individual L1 operations, particularly LDPC decoding, on GPUs [13, 36, 46]. DecodeX [30] benchmarks LDPC decoding across CPU, GPU, and ASIC platforms, exposing the effects of parallelism, work-load granularity, data movement, and offload. NVIDIA Aerial instead implements complete, production-grade PDSCH and PUSCH pipelines with CUDA kernels [23]. 

GPU sharing systems (see Table 1). Gandiva, AntMan, and HiveD schedule or resize training jobs [39, 40, 45]; Salus, Orion, and TGS share GPUs at iteration, operator, or kernel granularity [34, 38, 44]. REEF provides μs-scale preemption, but requires a custom AMD ROCm driver and is incompat-ible with our CUDA pipeline [10]. MIG isolates SMs and memory slices, whereas MPS and CUDA green contexts iso-late compute but leave HBM shared [16, 20, 21]. For vRAN systems, YinYangRAN shares compute through MPS, while CAORA and RAN-LLM use MIG to colocate vRAN with AI or LLM workloads [15, 32, 43] (Fig. 4(d)). 

8 Conclusion 

We present Beaver, a GPU sharing system that enables elas-tic co-tenancy between ML and latency-critical vRAN work-loads. Beaver combines a workload-aware SM sizer for het-erogeneous multi-cell workloads, slot-granular SM reparti-tioning across pre-created GC pairs, and a cooperative HBM 

> 12

bandwidth guard injected into compiled co-tenant kernels. Evaluated using NVIDIA Aerial, Beaver keeps the vRAN’s p99.9 processing latency within its deadline while co-located with full-stack vLLM serving, and extends effectively to the downlink pipeline and other GPUs. Future directions include coordinating multiple ML co-tenants, extending resource management across multi-GPU deployments, and exploring hardware support for finer-grained HBM bandwidth control.

A Aerial Uplink Test Vector Selection 

What is a test vector? An Aerial test vector is a repro-ducible input for one execution of the radio pipeline. Each uplink vector represents one 0.5-ms slot and stores the re-ceived frequency-domain samples, the radio configuration— including its resource blocks, symbols, modulation and cod-ing scheme (MCS), code rate, MIMO layers, and reference-signal configuration—and reference outputs for checking the decoded result. The benchmark replays this slot and repli-cates it across cells. A test vector therefore defines the work for one cell in one slot; it is not a multi-slot traffic trace. 

Which vectors are available? Aerial provides uplink vec-tors with different bandwidth allocations, antenna configura-tions, user and layer counts, symbol allocations, and MCS val-ues. We choose the four-receive-antenna, four-layer family because it is the only supplied family that covers every MCS value, 0–27, while also using the maximum carrier bandwidth and maximum single-user layer count. This family contains 35 vectors on a 100-MHz carrier with 273 resource blocks and one user. Each vector captures one complete 0.5-ms uplink slot. With 30-kHz subcarrier spacing and a normal cyclic prefix, one slot has 14 OFDM symbols, and these vectors assign all 14 to PUSCH. The 16-symbol configuration instead counts uplink symbols across a DDDSU pattern: 14 from the full uplink slot and two from the special slot. Because our benchmark replays only the full uplink slot, each test vector contains 14 symbols. Table 4 lists the family in MCS order, one row per MCS. The first group of columns describes each radio configuration. The remaining columns report its measured compute and HBM demand; darker shading means greater demand within that column. We profile every vector using the same 20-cell run and a fixed 72-SM uplink partition. The two highlighted rows, vectors 21 and 28, are the compute-heavy and memory-heavy vectors used in our evaluation. 

Why these two vectors? We use two vectors because com-pute and HBM have different worst cases. Compute demand is the median GPU execution time per slot divided by 20 cells. By this measure, vector 21 is the suite’s most compute-intensive vector at 113.5 μs per cell. Its MCS-20, 256-QAM configuration has a relatively low code rate and creates sub-stantial LDPC parity work. Vector 28 represents the suite’s most memory-intensive MCS-27 configuration. It has the largest transport block and, in our profiling, moves 17.2 MB through HBM per cell, sus-tains 217 GB/s across the slot, and reaches 660 GB/s in its busiest kernel. We therefore use vector 21 for head-of-line blocking, com-pute contention, and SM-partitioning experiments, where the radio’s compute demand should be largest. We use vec-tor 28 for HBM-contention and bandwidth-protection exper-iments, where the radio’s memory demand should be largest. Each mechanism is tested with the vector that places the most pressure on the resource it controls.

D SM Sizing Model Calibration 

This appendix describes how we calibrate the sizing model in §4.1. Instead of profiling the combinatorial space of heteroge-neous cell sets, we measure a structured set of homogeneous workloads, in which every cell uses the same test vector. We then compose the resulting per-vector coefficients to pre-dict slots containing different test vectors. No heterogeneous workload is used for fitting; heterogeneous cell sets are used only to evaluate the model in §6.3. 

Structured homogeneous sweep. For each of the 28 test vectors, we cross six cell counts, 𝐶 ∈ { 6, 10 , 14 , 18 , 22 , 26 },with six vRAN SM budgets, 𝑠 ∈ { 40 , 56 , 72 , 88 , 104 , 120 }. At each combination, all cells use the same test vector, mak-ing the workload homogeneous. This produces 6 × 6 = 36 combinations per test vector and 28 × 36 = 1,008 combina-tions in total. We run each combination for 3,000 consecutive slots, launching one CUDA graph per slot. We discard the first 50 warm-up slots and compute the median and p99.9 latency from the remaining 2,950 slots. The complete sweep therefore executes 3.024 million slots. 

Fit each test vector. For each test vector, least-squares regression fits the three coefficients 𝜏 0, 𝜏 1, and 𝜏 2 from its ho-mogeneous measurements. We fit only combinations whose measured median is at most 2.2 ms. Combinations far beyond the 1.5 ms deadline are already infeasible and cannot affect the selected partition; including them would reduce fitting accuracy near the deadline, where sizing decisions matter. 

Predict heterogeneous slots. At runtime, a slot may con-tain cells using different test vectors. The controller looks up the coefficients fitted for each cell’s test vector, takes the largest per-slot setup term, and sums the two per-cell terms as described in Equation (2). Thus, the structured ho-mogeneous combinations provide the coefficients needed to predict previously unseen heterogeneous cell sets with-out profiling those combinations directly. §6.3 evaluates this composition on held-out heterogeneous workloads. 

Safety reserve. The composed model predicts median la-tency, whereas partition sizing must protect the p99.9 tail. Our calibration uses a fixed 24.6 μs median-to-p99.9 offset and a fitting-error allowance of 60–190 μs, depending on the test vector. The resulting reserve 𝑅 p99.9 is therefore 85–215 μsfor a vRAN-only slot. A heterogeneous slot uses the largest reserve among its test vectors and adds it once to the com-posed latency estimate.
