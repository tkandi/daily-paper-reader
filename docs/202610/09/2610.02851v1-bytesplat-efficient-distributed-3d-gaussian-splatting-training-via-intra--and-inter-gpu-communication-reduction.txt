# ByteSplat: Efficient Distributed 3D Gaussian Splatting Training via Intra- and Inter-GPU communication reduction

3D Gaussian Splatting (3DGS) enables photorealistic scene reconstruction, but training large-scale scenes requires substantial memory and computation. Distributing training across multiple GPUs increases available memory capacity, yet its performance is strictly constrained by data movement. We identify two dominant bottlenecks: repeated off-chip memory accesses during forward and backward rasterization, and inter-GPU communication of partial Gaussian gradients. To address these bottlenecks, we present ByteSplat, a distributed 3DGS training framework that jointly reduces intra- and inter-GPU data movement. First, ByteSplat fuses forward rasterization and backward rasterization into a single GPU kernel, retaining intermediate results on-chip to eliminate redundant off-chip transfers. Second, to alleviate the increased on-chip storage by fused rasterization, ByteSplat introduces hardware-aware pruning that considers both rendering quality and per-tile shared-memory constraints, increasing the overall performance of fused execution. Lastly, ByteSplat exploits gradient sparsity to eliminate zero partial gradients from inter-GPU communication. GPU-efficient encoder and decoder kernels compact the remaining records and directly aggregate the received gradients on their owner GPUs, reducing both communication volume and local aggregation overhead. We evaluate ByteSplat across six datasets. Compared with the baseline, ByteSplat reduces intra-GPU off-chip traffic and backward inter-GPU communication volume by 63.4% and 65.8%, respectively. On eight GPUs, ByteSplat achieves up to 6.1 × training speedup while preserving reconstruction quality.

More recently, 3DGS has also become the foundational substrate for world models, including Spark [22] and HY-World 2.0 [40]. However, scaling 3DGS training to large scenes [20, 33, 39, 47] requires optimizing an increasingly large number of Gaussians. Therefore, distributing Gaussians across multiple GPUs is necessary to provide sufficient memory capacity. In mainstream distributed 3DGS training frameworks [18, 53], Gaussians are typically partitioned across multiple GPUs. At the beginning of each training iteration, each GPU retrieves the Gaussians required to render its assigned image regions from other GPUs. After completing its local computation, each GPU then sends partial Gaussian gradients back to the corresponding owner GPUs for gradient aggregation and parameter updates. Our characterization in Sec. 3 shows that this computing paradigm leads to major performance bottlenecks. Thus, it is important for us to revisit its training dataflow. 

Bottlenecks. In Sec. 3, we identify two dominant bottlenecks during 3DGS training. The first bottleneck is the data movement within local GPU computation. Specifically, forward and backward rasterization introduce substantial off-chip memory traffic. During tile-based forward rasterization, each thread block loads the related Gaussian attributes from off-chip DRAM into on-chip SRAMs. Later on, backward rasterization reloads the same attributes again and writes a large volume of partial Gaussian gradients to off-chip memory for aggregation. Consequently, forward and backward rasterization account for 29.9% of the end-to-end training time and 37.9% of the off-chip memory traffic. The second bottleneck is from the inter-GPU communication. Before rasterization, Gaussian attributes are transferred from the owner GPUs to the rendering GPUs responsible for rendering the corresponding image regions and computing partial gradients. After backward rasterization, the partial gradients are then returned to the owner GPUs for aggregation and parameter updates. We show that this gradient aggregation stage is one of the bottleneck stages and accounts for 23.9% of the total training time. These observations show that distributed 3DGS training is constrained by both intra-GPU off-chip traffic and inter-GPU gradient communication. This motivates us to design ByteSplat, which optimizes data movement at both levels. 

Fused Rasterization. To reduce intra-GPU data movement, we propose a mega-kernel that fuses forward ras-terization, loss computation, and backward rasterization into a single mega-kernel. Doing so, the kernel retains all necessary attributes on-chip throughout these stages and eliminates off-chip transfers of intermediate data between stages. In the conventional pipeline, forward and backward rasterization execute as separate kernels and exchange data through off-chip memory. Consequently, Gaussian attributes accessed during the forward rasterization must be reloaded during the backward rasterization, while the intermediate results between these two must be written back to DRAM. In contrast, our mega-kernel retains Gaussian attributes and intermediate results on-chip across both stages. For each tile, it loads the Gaussian data once, performs forward rasterization and the corresponding backward computation within a single kernel invocation. This way, it only writes the final outputs required by subsequent stages to off-chip memory. In Sec. 8.2, we show that this fused dataflow reduces off-chip memory traffic by 63.4%. 

Hardware-Aware Pruning. Although fused rasterization reduces off-chip memory traffic, a naive mega-kernel implementation can impose substantial pressure on on-chip memory. Because fusing forward and backward rasterization extends the lifetime of Gaussian attributes and intermediate results, it increases the shared-memory requirement of each thread block. This pressure can be further exacerbated by the uneven distribution of Gaussians across image tiles. Those tiles whose working sets exceed the per-block shared-memory limit cannot use the fused kernel. Even below this limit, large shared-memory allocations can reduce thread-block occupancy and offset the benefits of fusion. To address this challenge, we propose a hardware-aware Gaussian pruning strategy that explicitly accounts for the on-chip resource constraints of the target GPU. During training, our strategy first identifies image tiles for which fused execution is infeasible without pruning. For overloaded tiles, we then progressively remove low-importance Gaussians associated with these tiles and increase the fraction of tiles that satisfy the mega-kernel’s register and shared-memory

Unlike conventional pruning methods that primarily target model size, our method incorporates hardware resource constraints and directly optimizes the execution efficiency of fused rasterization. By integrating pruning into the training process, the remaining Gaussians can adapt to compensate for the removed primitives. Zero-Eliding Gradient Communication. To reduce inter-GPU data movement, we exploit the sparsity of partial Gaussian gradients. Existing distributed systems return the partial Gaussian gradients of every Gaussian received for local rendering, regardless of whether that gradient contributes to the final parameter update. Our characterization shows that up to 72.0% of these partial gradients are zero. Communicating zero gradients consumes interconnect bandwidth without contributing to gradient aggregation. Based on this observation, we propose zero-eliding gradient communication, which transfers only nonzero partial gradients required for gradient aggregation. Before communication, ByteSplat identifies partial gradients whose components are all exactly zero and compacts the remaining gradient results. After receiving the compacted data, each owner GPU decodes the representation and accumulates the nonzero gradients into the corresponding Gaussian param-eters. Result. We integrate these three techniques into ByteSplat, an efficient distributed 3DGS training framework. We evaluate ByteSplat across six datasets with up to 21.6 million Gaussians. Compared with other baselines, ByteSplat reduces intra-GPU and backward inter-GPU data traffic by 63.4% and 65.8%, respectively. Together, ByteSplat achieves up to 6.1 × speedup, while retaining reconstruction quality. The contributions of this paper are as follows: 

• We characterize the intra- and inter-GPU data movement in distributed 3DGS training and identify that data movement is the main performance bottleneck. 

• We propose fused rasterization, which fuses forward rasterization, loss computation, and backward rasterization, reducing off-chip transfers of intermediate results. 

• We propose hardware-aware training to maximize the performance benefits of the fused rasterization. 

• We introduce zero-eliding gradient communication, which avoids transmitting zero-valued partial gradients between rendering and owner GPUs with low processing overhead. 

• We integrate these techniques into ByteSplat and achieve up to 6.1 × speedup over 8-GPU baseline.

2.2 Distributed Training Pipeline 

To support large-scale scenes, distributed 3DGS training partitions both Gaussians and rendering workloads across multiple GPUs [53]. As shown in Fig. 1, different stages expose different execution granularities. Thus, distributed training adopts different data partitions for different stages. 

Overview. At initialization, all Gaussians are divided into subsets and distributed across the GPUs. Each Gaussian is assigned to an owner GPU , which stores its trainable parameters and optimization state. This Gaussian-wise partition is used in projection and Gaussian update, where each GPU processes its locally owned Gaussians independently. For the remaining stages, each selected training image is divided into fixed-size tiles, e.g., tiles of 16 × 16 pixels. The tiles are partitioned into subsets. Each subset is assigned to a rendering GPU . Each rendering GPU performs sorting, forward rasterization, loss computation, and backward rasterization for its assigned tiles. Because different tiles may have different Gaussian workloads, the tile assignment can be periodically adjusted to mitigate workload imbalance. Note that, the owner GPUs and the rendering GPUs are two logical roles of the same physical GPUs. Because the Gaussians required for a tile may be distributed across multiple owner GPUs, a rendering GPU generally does not locally store all the Gaussians for its assigned tiles. Therefore, Gaussian data need to be exchanged in distributed training when transitioning between Gaussian-wise and tile-wise stages. Next, the following paragraphs describe the two communication points introduced by these transitions. 

Forward Gaussian Communication. During this stage, for each selected camera view, each owner GPU projects its locally owned Gaussians and identifies the rendering GPUs responsible for their intersected tiles. Based on their projected results, the owner GPU determines which image tiles each Gaussian intersects and identifies the rendering GPUs responsible for those tiles. The owner GPUs then perform sparse all-to-all communication to send the required Gaussians to the corresponding rendering GPUs. Here, sparsity refers to the subset of Gaussians exchanged between GPU pairs. For each Gaussian, we transfer its Gaussian depth, transmittance, and color attributes required by the subsequent stages. After receiving these attributes, each rendering GPU constructs the tile-Gaussian intersection lists and performs sorting, forward rasterization, and loss computation for its assigned tiles. This communication transitions the pipeline from the Gaussian-wise execution to the tile-wise execution.

3 Motivation 

With the distributed training pipeline described in Sec. 2, we characterize the performance of the original 3DGS training [53] across six datasets [1, 16, 21, 29, 31, 42] and show four key insights that motivate our ByteSplat design. 

Insight 1: Forward/backward rasterization, gradient aggregation, and parameter update dominate training time. 

First, Fig. 2 shows the per-iteration latency of distributed 3DGS training as the GPU count increases from one to eight. We profile training on six datasets, including three large-scale datasets [29, 31, 42] and three small-scale datasets [1, 16, 21]. On a single GPU, iteration latency ranges from hundreds of milliseconds to over one second. Although additional GPUs reduce this latency, the overall per-iteration latency is still significant. For example, Mill-19 [29] requires approximately 1.2 seconds per iteration on one GPU and 0.15 seconds on eight GPUs. These results motivate us to examine the execution-time breakdown and identify the bottleneck stages in the entire pipeline. Fig. 3 shows the execution breakdown of distributed training. Here, we evaluate over the six datasets on eight Nvidia A6000 GPUs [41]. Here, gradient aggregation includes the communication of partial gradients and their subsequent accumulation on the owner GPUs. Across the evaluated scenes, forward rasterization, backward rasterization, distributed gradient aggregation, and the following parameter updates take 17.0%, 13.0%, 23.8%, and 18.1% of the training time, respectively. Together, these four stages consume over 71.9% of the total execution time. Although their individual contributions vary across scenes, these stages collectively account for most training time. Therefore, accelerating all these stages is critical to improving the end-to-end performance of distributed 3DGS training.

To understand the root cause of the rasterization overhead, Fig. 4 shows the roofline analysis of two bottleneck stages, forward rasterization and backward rasterization. The numbers are measured on eight Nvidia A6000 GPUs. Overall, these two stages are primarily memory-bound. 4 Fused Rasterization 

As discussed in Sec. 3, forward rasterization and backward rasterization account for a large fraction of the training time. Meanwhile, existing pipelines execute them as separate GPU kernels. Consequently, intermediate results must be repeatedly written back to off-chip memory and reloaded by subsequent kernels. To eliminate this intra-GPU data movement, ByteSplat proposes a fused rasterization kernel. 

4.1 Issues of the Existing Pipeline 

Fig. 7a illustrates the dataflow of the original pipeline. Forward rasterization processes each tile by loading its tile-Gaussian list and the corresponding Gaussian attributes from off-chip memory into on-chip shared memory. Since a tile can contain more Gaussians than shared memory can accommodate, the kernel loads and processes these Gaussians in batches. The kernel then performs front-to-back alpha blending and writes the rendered pixel colors required by the backward rasterization to off-chip memory. Then, loss computation is launched as a separate kernel after forward rasterization. This kernel reloads the rendered pixel colors from off-chip memory and compares them with the ground-truth image to compute the L1 and SSIM losses. It then writes the pixel-wise loss values and per-pixel gradients, 𝜕 L/ 𝜕 C(p), back to off-chip memory. Lastly, backward rasterization is launched as a standalone kernel. For each tile, it reloads the tile-Gaussian list, the Gaussian attributes, and the per-pixel gradients from off-chip memory. The kernel then traverses the tile-Gaussian list in reverse order and computes the partial gradient of each pixel-Gaussian pair. Finally, it writes the partial Gaussian gradients to off-chip memory for subsequent gradient aggregation. As shown in Fig. 7a, this execution paradigm introduces substantial redundant memory traffic. In particular, the Gaussian attributes are loaded twice in both forward and backward rasterization. Moreover, intermediate results, e.g., the rendered pixels and per-pixel gradients, are written to off-chip memory by one kernel only to be immediately reloaded by another. These redundant memory accesses make both rasterization stages memory-bound.

4.2 Fused Rasterization Dataflow 

Overview. To address this inefficiency, ByteSplat fuses forward rasterization, loss computation, and backward rasterization into a single mega-kernel, as shown in Fig. 7b. For each tile, the fused kernel loads the relevant Gaussian attributes from off-chip memory and retains them on-chip across the forward and backward rasterization. After forward rasterization produces the rendered pixels, the kernel immediately computes the corresponding per-pixel gradients. These intermediate gradients remain on-chip and are directly consumed by backward rasterization without being written back to off-chip memory. Backward rasterization then computes the partial Gaussian gradients and writes only the outputs to off-chip memory for gradient aggregation. This fused dataflow eliminates the round trips of rendered pixels, intermediate rasterization states, and per-pixel gradients through off-chip memory. It also allows the forward and backward rasterization to reuse Gaussian attributes while avoiding redundant attribute loading. 

Tile-Local Loss Computation. One caveat here is that fusing loss computation with rasterization is challenging. Because the conventional SSIM loss introduces dependencies across image tiles. SSIM loss computes the structural similarity over a spatial window surrounding each pixel. For pixels near a tile boundary, this window can contain pixels processed by other thread blocks or rendering GPUs. A thread block therefore cannot compute SSIM loss using only the pixel values generated for its own tile. To eliminate these dependencies, ByteSplat adopts the stochastic structural similarity loss, S3IM [48], and applies it independently within each image tile. 5 Hardware-Aware Training 

Although fused rasterization eliminates substantial off-chip memory traffic, it introduces a new challenge: increased on-chip resource pressure. In this section, we first explain why applying fused rasterization to all image tiles is impractical. We then present a novel hardware-aware training that increases the fraction of tiles that can benefit from fusion. 

5.1 Challenges 

In the conventional pipeline, forward rasterization, loss computation, and backward rasterization execute as separate GPU kernels, as shown in Fig. 7a. Because each thread block processes one image tile, its on-chip memory requirement depends on how many Gaussians intersect with that tile. A tile intersected by more Gaussians requires the fused kernel to retain a larger amount of data on-chip. Even when the requirement remains below the hardware limit, a large per-block shared-memory allocation can reduce the number of concurrently resident thread blocks on each streaming multiprocessor. This could weaken the GPU’s ability to overlap compute and memory latency, potentially offsetting its benefits of reduced off-chip traffic. Therefore, directly applying fused rasterization to every tile is neither practical nor efficient. ByteSplat must determine which tiles can execute efficiently within the shared-memory budget of the target GPU. Tiles that exceed this budget must follow the conventional execution path and store their intermediate data back to off-chip memory. Thus, we introduce a training strategy in Sec. 5.2 to maximize fusion coverage while preserving reconstruction quality. 

5.2 Training Strategy 

To alleviate shared-memory pressure, ByteSplat proposes a three-stage training strategy in Fig. 8 that increases the fraction of tiles eligible for fused rasterization. The first stage follows the classic 3DGS training procedure to construct an initial Gaussian representation. Then, the second stage removes insignificant Gaussians that make no major contribution to any image in the training set. Lastly, the final stage accounts for the GPU shared-memory capacity and progressively removes unimportant Gaussians from overloaded tiles. Thus, more tiles can leverage fused rasterization. 

Stage 1: Initial Training and Densification. Starting from the initial point cloud, ByteSplat first trains a Gaussian representation using the classic 3DGS training procedure. 6 Zero-Eliding Gradient Communication 

As discussed in Sec. 3, the gradient aggregation in the backward pass dominates the inter-GPU communication latency of distributed 3DGS training. Existing systems communicate all partial gradients of every Gaussian processed by a rendering GPU, regardless of the value of that gradient. However, our characterization shows that up to 72.0% of these gradients are zeros. This motivates ByteSplat to eliminate zero-value gradients before communication. We first present Zero-Gradient Elision that identifies and removes zero-value gradients without changing the training semantics. Then, we present an efficient Sparse Gradient Transmission for effective encoding and communication. 

6.1 Zero-Gradient Elision 

As described in Sec. 2.1, projection associates a Gaussian with every intersecting image tile. However, a tile-Gaussian intersection does not guarantee that this Gaussian contributes to any pixel in this image tile. This is because a Gaussian may be skipped by a pixel when the accumulated transmittance of the pixel reaches the early-termination threshold. Consequently, many Gaussians communicated to a rendering GPU do not contribute to any pixel assigned to that GPU. Naturally, those skipped Gaussians will obtain zero gradient during the gradient backpropagation. However, existing distributed 3DGS systems use the same indexing during forward Gaussian communication to return partial gradients to owner GPUs. This symmetric indexing simplifies gradient routing because the owner GPU can recover the destination Gaussian directly from the original communication order. However, it also forces the rendering GPU to return gradients of every Gaussian, including those whose partial gradients are zero. To avoid this redundant data communication, ByteSplat examines each partial-gradient record after backward rasterization and flags those Gaussians whose gradients are zero. After flagging, ByteSplat removes these zero-value gradients from the reverse-communication buffer without deleting the corresponding Gaussians from the model. It retains only the Gaussians and their partial gradients containing at least one nonzero gradient value. Because every omitted partial gradient is exactly zero, this optimization preserves the result of gradient aggregation and does not affect training semantics. By avoiding these redundant gradients, ByteSplat reduces both the inter-GPU communication volume and the local gradient aggregation overhead. The next subsection describes how ByteSplat efficiently packs and transmits the remaining nonzero gradient records.

Encoding Scheme. In Fig. 9, after backward rasterization, ByteSplat first groups the partial gradients according to their destination owner GPUs, and then divides each partition into fixed-size chunks. The encoder assigns one thread block to each chunk and one thread to a subset of gradients in a chunk. Each thread checks whether the gradients are nonzero, and the threads collectively construct a bitmap that identifies the nonzero gradients. The thread block then performs an on-chip prefix sum to determine the packed position of each nonzero gradient. Finally, the encoder writes the nonzero gradients contiguously into a compact array. 

Sparse Communication and Local Aggregation. After encoding, the rendering GPUs exchange their compact array with the corresponding bitmap and perform a variable-length sparse all-to-all communication. On each owner GPU, the decoder assigns each received chunk to one thread block and uses its bitmap to recover the original positions of the retained gradients. The decoder then uses the forward communication indexing to map those partial gradients to its local Gaussian indices. Rather than reconstructing the original dense gradient array, the decoder directly accumulates each nonzero gradient into the corresponding Gaussian gradient buffer. Because a Gaussian may receive gradients from multiple rendering GPUs, these updates use atomic operations to ensure correctness. By completely skipping the zero-value gradients, this design reduces the workloads in both inter-GPU communication and local aggregation. 

7 Experimental Setup 

Hardware platforms. To evaluate the robustness of our training framework, we evaluate ByteSplat on two GPU servers with different interconnect configurations. One is an A6000 server with eight Nvidia A6000 GPUs. Each has 48 GB GDDR6 memory. The other one is an A100 server with eight Nvidia A100 GPUs. Each has 80 GB HBM memory. We then measure the end-to-end performance of distributed training, including the kernel launch time. 

Datasets. Our evaluation covers six widely-used datasets, including Mill-19 [42], UrbanScene3D [31], Matrix-City [29], Mip-NeRF 360 [1], DeepBlending [16], Tanks&Temples [21]. The first three datasets are for large-scale scene reconstruction, including various urban scenes. The remaining scenes are well-known 3DGS datasets with different indoor and outdoor scenes. For both training and evaluation, we all use the native image resolution. For testing, we reserve every eighth view in the dataset, following the standard 3DGS protocol [53]. 

Training Settings. We train every scene at its native resolution on eight GPUs with one camera per GPU. Because the scenes differ substantially in scale, we do not terminate initial training at a common iteration count. Instead, we resume densification from intermediate checkpoints until the Gaussian population and validation quality stabilize, and then disable densification and opacity reset for a final convergence phase. Across our workloads, initial training takes approximately 200k iterations on average, with the exact stopping point adjusted to scene scale and convergence. We apply pruning only after this initial model has converged. The retained Gaussians are then fine-tuned for another 30k iterations with densification disabled; all quality and performance results use the checkpoint at the end of this fine-tuning phase. The unpruned and pruned runs otherwise use the same data split, image resolution, loss, and optimizer hyperparameters.

9 Related Work 

3DGS GPU Optimization. Existing studies improve GPU-based 3DGS efficiency through various techniques, including compact representations, finer-grained culling, and optimized rasterization. For instance, CompactGS [27], EAGLES [12], and Compressed 3DGS [37] reduce representation size through compact parameter encodings and quantization. On the other hand, HAC [3] and HAC++ [4] further improve compression through context-based coding. These approaches primarily reduce model storage requirements, while another line of work optimizes runtime execution. For example, AdR-Gaussian [44] and Speedy-Splat [14] tighten the regions processed for each Gaussian to avoid unnecessary rasterization. FlashGS [10] combines precise intersection tests, workload-aware scheduling, and software pipelining. Balanced 3DGS [13] improves workload distribution through Gaussian-wise parallelism and fine-grained tiling, while TC-GS [30] maps Gaussian evaluation to matrix operations on Tensor Cores. Seele [54] combines scene preprocessing, online filtering, and contribution-aware rasterization to improve mobile GPU efficiency. In contrast to representation compression and optimizations within rendering stages, ByteSplat reduces repeated off-chip accesses across forward rasterization, loss computation, and backward rasterization through fused execution. 

3DGS Training Acceleration. Numerous approaches reduce training cost by controlling Gaussian population, optimizing densification, or simplifying parameter updates. For instance, LightGaussian [7] and MiniSplatting [8] use importance-driven pruning or simplification to obtain compact representations. LP-3DGS [51] and MaskGaussian [34] 

> Manuscript submitted to ACM

Taming 3DGS [36] combines budget-constrained densification with efficient gradient computation and parameter updates. Mini-Splatting2 [9] accelerates convergence through aggressive densification and visibility culling, while DashGaussian [2] jointly schedules rendering resolution and Gaussian growth. ContraGS [5] enables training directly on compressed Gaussian representations to reduce memory requirements and data movement. Unlike methods that primarily control representation size or optimization complexity, ByteSplat incorporates per-tile shared-memory constraints into pruning to increase the fraction of tiles eligible for fused rasterization. System-level approaches further address the memory and execution demands of large-scale training. GS-Scale [23] and CLM [52] leverage host-memory offloading and pipelined execution to reduce GPU memory requirements. GS-Scale additionally defers optimizer updates for Gaussians with zero gradients. Grendel [53] distributes Gaussian parameters and rendering workloads across multiple GPUs, exchanging Gaussian attributes and partial gradients between partitions. Splaxel [18] instead uses local rendering and global composition through pixel-level communication. ByteSplat targets a different combination of opportunities: retaining intermediate data on-chip within each rendering GPU and eliminating exactly zero partial-gradient records from inter-GPU communication. Its sparse decoder directly aggregates the retained gradients without reconstructing dense communication buffers. 

3DGS Accelerators. Dedicated architectures exploit the computation and memory-access characteristics of Gaussian rendering and training. GSCore [26], GBU [49], and GCC [38] introduce specialized hardware for Gaussian rendering. Lumina [11] reduces repeated computation through caching, while StreamingGS [50] introduces a memory-centric streaming dataflow to reduce DRAM traffic. VR-Pipe [25] augments GPU graphics hardware for Gaussian rasterization, and GRTX [24] combines streamlined acceleration structures with hardware-supported traversal reuse for Gaussian ray tracing. For training, GSArch [15] addresses memory-system bottlenecks, while ARC [6] reduces atomic operation overhead through adaptive warp-level reduction. Cambricon-GS [45] combines Gaussian-pixel hybrid parallelism with tiled SSIM-based pipelining. ByteSplat instead implements its optimizations on existing GPUs and jointly addresses on-chip data residency, tile-level resource constraints, and distributed gradient communication. 

Acceleration of Other 3DGS Applications. Application-specific systems exploit additional opportunities in SLAM, virtual reality, and large-scale mobile rendering. GauSPU [46], REACT3D [43], and RTGS [28] accelerate 3DGS-based SLAM through specialized processing and reductions in redundant computation. Splatonic [17] combines adaptive sparse pixel sampling and Gaussian-parallel pixel rendering to accelerate tracking and mapping. For virtual reality, MetaSapiens [32] exploits foveated rendering, while Nebula [55] combines cloud-client collaborative rendering with accelerated stereo rasterization. Hierarchical 3DGS [20] and Octree-GS [39] organize Gaussians into multiresolution representations to support efficient rendering at different levels of detail. Voyager [35] further targets city-scale Gaussian rendering on resource-constrained mobile devices. These systems primarily address the latency, memory, and energy constraints of online tracking or interactive rendering. In contrast, ByteSplat focuses on distributed scene training and reduces data movement both within individual GPUs and across the GPUs participating in gradient aggregation. 

10 Conclusion 

Distributed training is increasingly important for scaling 3D Gaussian Splatting to large scenes, but its efficiency remains constrained by data movement. We present ByteSplat, which reduces intra-GPU off-chip traffic and inter-GPU gradient communication via three techniques: fused rasterization, hardware-aware pruning, and zero-gradient elision. With these techniques, ByteSplat achieves up to 6.1 × training efficiency while maintaining the same reconstruction quality. Nevertheless, ByteSplat represents one step toward addressing the data-movement challenges of distributed
