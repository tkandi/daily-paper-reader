Abstract 

Sparse expert activation reduces MoE models’ computation, yet expert weights can exceed limited device memory. Offloading makes in-ference feasible on a compact AI appliance but exposes host-to-device transfers to the in-ference path. We present MoE-CORE, a sys-tem that coordinates expert offloading and resi-dency for memory-constrained MoE inference. It stages complete expert layers in alternating buffers during prefill. During decode, it com-bines nonuniform layer-wise cache capacity, domain-informed initialization, routing-history-aware replacement, and cross-layer prefetch-ing. The main configuration executes router-selected experts exactly; an optional score-based substitution path handles eligible low-score misses. The main comparison uses 1K-and 128-token output caps for MoE-CORE and vLLM Prefetch, respectively. Across five work-loads per model, MoE-CORE records a mean time per output token (TPOT) of 38.0–44.8 ms versus 1268.9–1269.1 ms for the evaluated vLLM Prefetch configuration on DeepSeek-V4-Flash-W4A8; the corresponding values on GLM-5.2-W4A8C8 are 206.6–220.5 and 5941.5–5941.8 ms. Under an 84-GB NPU-memory cap, the best measured DeepSeek GSM8K configuration achieves a TPOT of 21.5 ms with approximate expert substitution and multi-token prediction (MTP) at depth 2. These results support coordinated expert residency and transfer scheduling under a device-memory constraint. The code is here.

1 Introduction 

Mixture-of-Experts (MoE) architectures (Shazeer et al., 2017) have become an important approach to scaling the capacity of large language mod-els (LLMs) without proportionally increasing per-token computation. By routing each token to acti-vate only a small subset of experts, MoE models offer a favorable computation-to-capacity ratio and are therefore attractive for resource-constrained lo-cal and edge inference (Yi et al., 2025). Sparse activation, however, reduces computation rather than the amount of model weights that must remain accessible. Recent model families make this dis-tinction increasingly consequential: DeepSeek-V4-Flash and DeepSeek-V4-Pro (DeepSeek-AI, 2026) contain 284B and 1.6T total parameters while acti-vating 13B and 49B parameters per token, respec-tively; GLM-5.2 (GLM-5 Team, 2026) contains 744B total parameters with 40B active; and Kimi K3 scales to 2.8T total parameters with 104B active (Kimi-Team, 2026). These models also increas-ingly target Agent workloads such as long-horizon coding, tool use, and knowledge work. Deploying them as local Agent services therefore requires at-tention not only to sparse computation, but also to the storage capacity and memory cost of the full expert set. This memory burden is particularly acute on compact, single-node AI appliances. These appli-ances integrate a host processor and memory with one or a small number of GPUs or NPUs, provid-ing enterprises with a practical on-premises plat-form for deploying AI services close to private data while retaining local control over the serving en-1arXiv:2610.01950v1 [cs.DC] 1 Oct 2026 vironment. However, the device-memory capacity of these systems is under increasing pressure from two directions: Growing MoE parameter counts increase the volume of weights, while long-context Agent tasks expand the KV cache with conversa-tion histories, retrieved documents, tool outputs, and intermediate task state. Thus, these demands can prevent the complete set of model weights from residing in device memory, even though an AI ap-pliance provides substantially more resources than a mobile or embedded edge device. Deploying LLMs on such systems therefore requires placing some of the model weights in host memory. Expert offloading makes such deployments feasi-ble by keeping some routed expert weights in host memory and transferring them to the device mem-ory (GPUs or NPUs) when needed. Because expert weights account for a large fraction of an MoE model’s parameters while each token activates only a small subset of experts, retaining inactive ex-perts in host memory can substantially increase the model size supported by a fixed device-memory budget. This technique reduces device-memory demand, but loading a selected expert after rout-ing places host-to-device (H2D) communication on the inference critical path. Existing techniques for reducing this cost can be grouped into four categories. (i) Activation-locality methods use routing traces or semantic history to identify ex-perts that are likely to be reused and retain them in a device cache, as in MoE-Infinity and FineMoE (Xue et al., 2025; Yu et al., 2025). (ii) Auxiliary-predictor methods train a separate lightweight model to predict expert activations before the origi-nal router is reached. SiDA-MoE (Du et al., 2024) uses an offline-trained hash predictor, while Pro-MoE trains predictors for individual layers (Song et al., 2025). (iii) Cross-layer and phase-aware methods obtain expert information from later gates or reorganize transfers according to layer and in-ference phase. Fate (Fang et al., 2026b) and Adap-MoE (Zhong et al., 2024a) use cross-layer gat-ing with layer-aware expert management, whereas DuoServe-MoE (Zhang et al., 2026) separates pre-fill and decode transfer pipelines. (iv) Expert-substitution methods , including SMoE and Bud-dyMoE (Zhu et al., 2026; Wang et al., 2025a), avoid selected transfers in eligible cases by executing an already resident alternative expert. Together, these systems show that offloading latency can be ad-dressed through caching, prediction, scheduling, and fallback mechanisms. However, these methods have been evaluated on short-context workloads, leaving the interaction between long contexts, ex-pert residency, and offloading latency insufficiently characterized. For memory-constrained MoE inference on a compact AI appliance, however, these mechanisms must operate as parts of a coordinated runtime rather than as isolated policies. This requirement raises three design questions: 

Does equal per-layer cache allocation yield comparable expert hit rates across MoE layers? 

Do expert popularity rankings differ across domain-specific workloads? 

Are routing scores near the Top-k boundary close enough to motivate a resident alternative for an eligible low-score cache miss? 

To address these questions, we present MoE-CORE, a coordinated expert-offloading system for memory-constrained MoE inference on compact, single-node AI appliances. MoE-CORE assigns prefill and decode to differ-ent expert-offloading paths and reserves a separate device-memory region for each phase. For prefills, it stages the complete routed-expert weights of two consecutive MoE layers in two alternating buffers, overlapping the transfer of the next layer with com-putation of the current layer. Decode is the primary optimization target because it activates a sparse ex-pert set at each iteration, yet an uncovered expert transfer can directly delay token generation. During decoding, MoE-CORE first distributes the expert cache budget across all MoE layers according to a dynamic layer-wise cache alloca-tor. Given a workload-domain hint from the serv-ing application, an offline profile initializes each layer’s cache with the corresponding hot experts. A routing-history-aware online cache then adapts this initial placement to the routing behavior ob-served during the current session. Because ex-pert residency alone cannot eliminate all misses, a cross-layer prefetcher evaluates the next layer’s router early and overlaps predicted expert transfers with useful computation. For residual misses, high-score experts remain on the exact loading path, whereas only eligible low-score experts may use resident substitutes selected by routing-score prox-imity. Relative to Load on Demand (LoD), which allo-cates static per-layer expert caches only for decode and synchronously loads non-resident selected ex-perts, MoE-CORE achieves mean time per out-put token (TPOT) speedups of up to 1.75 × on 2DeepSeek-V4-Flash-W4A8 and 1.15 × on GLM-5.2-W4A8C8. MoE-CORE also records lower TPOT than the evaluated vLLM Prefetch configu-rations. Its best measured configuration achieves 21.5 ms TPOT with multi-token prediction (MTP) at depth 2. Our contributions are as follows: • We characterize layer-wise differences in cache coverage and workload-dependent ex-pert rankings under a fixed cache configura-tion, and examine routing scores near the Top-

k activation boundary. • We design phase-specific expert-offloading paths for prefill and decode within a shared device-memory budget. • We coordinate layer-aware cache allocation, domain-informed initialization, online cache adaptation, and cross-layer prefetching; op-tional score-based substitution handles eli-gible low-score residual misses while high-score misses retain exact loading. • We demonstrate MoE-CORE’s effectiveness on five workloads with DeepSeek-V4-Flash-W4A8 and GLM-5.2-W4A8C8 on their re-spective AI-appliance platforms. It records lower TPOT than both LoD and the eval-uated vLLM Prefetch configurations, while ablations isolate the effects of its cache-management and prefetching mechanisms.

Expert-offloading performance therefore depends on both the resident expert set and the ability to hide remaining transfers. 32.3 Inference Phases and Memory Pressure 

Long-context Agent workloads illustrate the mem-ory pressure of MoE inference: multi-step tasks accumulate conversation turns, retrieved evidence, tool outputs, and intermediate task state. Agent-LongBench (Fang et al., 2026a) models such work-loads with trajectories ranging from 32K to 4M tokens, spanning long-turn interactions and high-information-density tool responses. MoE inference follows a prefill–decode work-flow. Prefill processes the input context, typically in parallel, and constructs the KV cache; its latency is a major component of time to first token (TTFT). Decode reuses the cache to generate subsequent to-kens iteratively, with time per output token (TPOT) characterizing its per-token latency (Agrawal et al., 2024; Zhong et al., 2024b). In MoE models, prefill may access a broad expert set across many context tokens, whereas each decode step routes only the newly generated token or token group. Longer trajectories expand the KV cache, leav-ing less device memory for resident experts. Con-text length and KV-cache representation therefore jointly determine the expert-residency budget. 

2.4 Observation and Motivation 

Memory constraints limit how many experts can remain resident on the device, while routing deter-mines which experts are needed during inference. The resulting offloading cost depends on the inter-action between expert residency and routing deci-sions, particularly when a selected expert is absent from device and its transfer cannot be hidden.

3 MoE-CORE 

MoE-CORE is a coordinated expert-weight offload-ing system for memory-constrained MoE inference on an AI appliance. Its design follows the phase asymmetry described in Section 2.4: prefill can collectively access a broad expert set, whereas each decode iteration exposes a sparse but latency-sensitive working set. Applying one offloading policy to both phases would either transfer unneces-sary experts during decode or incur repeated expert-selection and loading overhead during long prefills. MoE-CORE therefore provides separate prefill and decode paths within a unified runtime; Figure 4 shows the process. The two paths use physically disjoint device-memory regions that remain allocated throughout inference. The prefill region contains two expert-weight buffers, each holding all routed experts from a single MoE layer. When the prefill token count reaches the cache-dependent threshold N ∗ 

> l

defined in Section 3.6, the buffers stage the expert weights of two consecutive layers and alternate as execution advances through the model. The decode region stores routed-expert weights across all MoE lay-ers, organizing its capacity into per-layer caches with Kl resident expert slots assigned to layer l.Both regions share the device-memory budget with the fixed model state, KV cache, activations, and runtime workspace. During decode, MoE-CORE first establishes ex-pert residency before attempting to hide communi-cation. A layer-wise allocator determines Kl. The serving application then specifies the workload do-main through a request hint, and MoE-CORE fills the allocated slots with the corresponding hot ex-perts identified by offline profiling. As decoding proceeds, a Routing-History-Aware Cache (RHAC) policy updates expert residency using recent activa-tions, longer-term routing history, router scores, and access recency. These mechanisms reduce cache misses but cannot eliminate them. MoE-CORE then adopts a cross-layer prefetcher that predicts the next layer’s routed experts and asyn-chronously transfers predicted experts during the intervening computation. If a selected expert re-mains unavailable when required, the exact path loads it from host memory. When optional approx-imate expert substitution is enabled, an eligible low-score miss may instead use a score-similar, unselected expert already resident on the device; high-score misses remain on the exact path. Thus, the decode path combines miss reduc-tion with communication overlap before consider-ing approximate execution. Layer-wise allocation, domain-profile initialization, RHAC, and cross-layer prefetching preserve the routed expert com-putation; only optional expert substitution changes the selected expert set.

4 Evaluation 

We evaluate end-to-end inference performance, the contributions of the expert-cache components, and compatibility with multi-token prediction (MTP). The main comparisons and cache-component abla-tions use exact expert execution; AES and MTP are evaluated separately. Unless otherwise specified, Deepseek is the default model. 

4.1 Implementation Settings Datasets and Models. We use GSM8K (Cobbe et al., 2021), AIME 2026 (Dekoninck et al., 2026), HumanEval (Chen et al., 2021), MMLU (Hendrycks et al., 2021), and LongBench v2 (Bai et al., 2025). They cover mathematical reason-ing, code generation, knowledge, and long-context tasks. The evaluated models are DeepSeek-V4-Flash-W4A8 (DeepSeek-AI, 2026) and GLM-5.2-W4A8C8 (GLM-5 Team, 2026). 

Platforms. Both models are evaluated on an As-cend 950PR NPU platform with 128 GB of physi-cal NPU memory and a 150-GB host-memory limit. Inference is subject to an 84-GB NPU-memory cap. 

Baselines. We compare MoE-CORE with two expert-offloading baselines: • Load on Demand (LoD). The baseline allo-cates static per-layer expert caches only dur-ing decode and synchronously loads a selected expert on a cache miss. • vLLM Prefetch. We use the layer-wise prefetch strategy in vLLM (vLLM Project, 2026). The DeepSeek configuration uses two resident layer buffers and two rotating layer buffers; the GLM configuration uses one resi-dent layer and five offloaded layers. 

Request Protocol. All configurations in Table 1 use batch size 1. LoD and MoE-CORE allow up to 1K output tokens, whereas vLLM Prefetch is capped at 128 output tokens to bound the runtime of its substantially slower decoding. DeepSeek and GLM use 200 requests per dataset. 

Metrics. We report time to first token (TTFT), time per output token (TPOT), end-to-end latency (E2EL), and output throughput in tokens per sec-ond (TPS). For a response containing n > 1 output tokens, TPOT is the generation time after the first token divided by n − 1. TTFT-P90 and TPOT-P90 are the reported request-level 90th percentiles. Be-cause the output caps differ, E2EL and TPS compar-isons are restricted to LoD and MoE-CORE. Exact cache-hit rate measures the availability of the origi-nally selected experts, including prefetched experts ready before execution. For AES-enabled results, effective expert availability additionally counts se-lected invocations served by resident substitutes. Means computed from the provided layer-rate ar-rays weight the recorded layers equally. 

4.2 Implementation Details 

MoE-CORE reserves separate expert-weight re-gions for prefill staging and decode caches. Of the 84-GB NPU-memory cap, 95% is budgeted for model inference, including model weights, ex-pert caches, KV cache, activations, and inference workspace; the remaining 5% is reserved for other necessary system allocations. The maximum con-figured prompt-plus-output length is 368K tokens. In the DeepSeek configuration, the uniform decode allocation uses 84 expert slots per layer. The evalu-ated nonuniform allocation uses 78 slots for most layers and 120 slots for layers 7, 9, 19, 31, and 37. GLM’s larger expert weights limit its single-NPU decode cache to 28 expert slots per layer. Domain profiles initialize the resident experts, and RHAC updates residency once per generated token. Cross-layer prefetching fetches one predicted ex-pert per layer by default. The substitution threshold is α = 0 .1. AES is disabled in the main compari-son and for the cache-component ablations.

4.5.4 Effect of Approximate Expert Substitution 

Approximate expert substitution (AES) resolves an eligible residual miss with an unselected ex-pert that is already resident on the NPU. We de-fine effective expert availability as the fraction of selected expert invocations that can proceed with-out a demand-triggered H2D transfer. With AES disabled, it reflects availability from caching and completed prefetches; with AES enabled, it also counts invocations served by resident substitutes. The recorded mean over 40 layer rates rises from 92.43% without AES to 99.972% with AES. Fig-ure 10 visualizes this near-complete effective avail-ability. This near-unity layer mean shows that, on average across the evaluated layers, MoE-CORE serves nearly all selected expert invocations with-out a demand-triggered H2D transfer. At batch size 1 with MTP disabled, enabling AES increases TPOT from 36.0 to 37.7 ms, a 4.7% overhead, due to the CPU-side computation re-quired for expert substitution. 

5 Related Work 

Expert offloading and heterogeneous execution. 

MoE-Infinity exploits activation traces to guide ex-pert caching and prefetching, while FineMoE uses fine-grained routing patterns and prompt-derived hints for expert management (Xue et al., 2025; Yu et al., 2025). Fiddler and KTransformers use host computation as well as device execution to ac-commodate experts under limited device memory (Kamahori et al., 2025; Chen et al., 2025). HOB-BIT uses lower-precision versions of less critical missed experts to reduce transfer cost (Tang et al., 2024). MoE-CORE instead focuses on exact-expert residency and transfer scheduling within a single-NPU memory budget. 

Transfer scheduling and routing-guided resi-dency. MoE-Lightning pipelines host, device, and I/O work with paged weights, whereas Klot-ski uses an expert-aware multi-batch pipeline to increase computation–transfer overlap (Cao et al., 2024; Fang et al., 2025). DuoServe-MoE explic-itly separates prefill and decode scheduling and applies phase-specific expert prefetching (Zhang et al., 2026). SiDA-MoE and ProMoE train auxil-iary predictors for proactive expert loading; Fate and AdapMoE exploit cross-layer routing informa-tion (Du et al., 2024; Song et al., 2025; Fang et al., 2026b; Zhong et al., 2024a). DoMoE additionally uses domain-specific routing tables to constrain semantic expert prediction (Mu et al., 2026). In contrast to predictor-centered designs, MoE-CORE uses a workload-domain profile to initialize cache contents, updates residency from observed routing history, and evaluates the original next-layer router early for prefetching. 

Approximate execution and speculative decod-ing. SMoE and BuddyMoE examine expert sub-stitution when selected experts are unavailable, while SpecMoEOff uses speculative decoding to en-large expert workloads and hide offloading latency (Zhu et al., 2026; Wang et al., 2025a,b). MoE-CORE’s optional substitution is restricted to res-ident, unselected experts close to the selected ex-pert’s routing score at the Top-k boundary; it does not assert functional equivalence from score prox-imity. Our MTP experiment assesses the compati-bility of the expert-offloading path with multi-token prediction rather than introducing a new specula-tion algorithm. More broadly, long-context serving systems such as Sarathi-Serve and DistServe sched-ule prefill and decode to improve serving efficiency (Agrawal et al., 2024; Zhong et al., 2024b); our fo-cus is expert-weight residency and transfer within a single appliance. 

6 Conclusion 

MoE-CORE addresses expert-weight offloading for memory-constrained MoE inference on compact, 13 single-NPU AI appliances. Measurements across five workloads reveal recurrent low-hit layers un-der uniform per-layer cache sizes and workload-dependent expert rankings; the examined routing-score distribution also illustrates a narrow gap near the Top-k boundary. These observations moti-vate a runtime that separates prefill staging from decode-time expert management and coordinates nonuniform cache capacity, domain-informed ini-tialization, routing-history-aware replacement, and cross-layer prefetching. Across five workloads per model and under the stated output caps, the main exact-execution configuration records mean TPOT of 38.0–44.8 ms on DeepSeek and 206.6–220.5 ms on GLM, versus 1268.9–1269.1 and 5941.5– 5941.8 ms, respectively, for the evaluated vLLM Prefetch configurations. Under an 84-GB NPU-memory cap, a separate DeepSeek experiment with optional approximate substitution reaches the best measured TPOT of 21.5 ms at MTP depth 2. The results support coordinated residency and transfer scheduling as a practical means of reducing decode latency when the full MoE expert set cannot remain on the device. 

Limitations 

MoE-CORE does not jointly optimize expert-weight offloading and KV-cache management, and its evaluation is limited to a single NPU; multi-NPU expert-weight placement remains future work. Further study of longer MTP depths may clarify when their additional H2D transfers can be offset by accepted predictions. We also plan to coordi-nate the currently separate prefill and decode ex-pert caches across consecutive sessions. Experts retained by RHAC after one session may be reused in the next session’s prefill, allowing layer-wise staging to transfer only experts that are not already resident. Finally, RHAC’s CPU-side updates add latency; reducing this computation is another direc-tion for optimization.
