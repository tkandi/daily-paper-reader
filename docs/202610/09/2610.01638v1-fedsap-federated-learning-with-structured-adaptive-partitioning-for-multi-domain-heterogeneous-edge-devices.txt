# FedSAP: Federated Learning with Structured Adaptive Partitioning for Multi-Domain Heterogeneous Edge Devices 

## Wentao Yue, Tianyou Lai, Hongji Li, Qingyu Mao, Qilei Li 

Abstract 

Federated learning (FL) on heterogeneous edge devices must jointly accommodate unequal resource budgets and domain-shifted local data. Existing resource-adaptive methods decide how much of a model each client trains but not where retained capacity should reside or how it should be shared, whereas federated domain-generalization methods usually assume a shared full architecture. Uniform compression can therefore discard high-utility channels, and a single aggregation path can mix transferable features with domain-sensitive updates. We propose FedSAP , a domain-aware heterogeneous FL framework that casts structured pruning as budget-constrained tri-state channel allocation. FedSAP converts each keep ratio into non-uniform layer budgets, assigns stable channels to a Global pool, useful domain-sensitive channels to pseudo-domain-specific Private pools, and low-utility channels to a 

Dropped state. This partition lets broadly useful features ben-efit from cross-client pooling while isolating domain-sensitive updates from incompatible clients. Domain-Guided Assign-ment infers pseudo-domains from shallow-gradient similarity, while Type-Matched Aggregation restricts each channel to its intended sharing scope. Across three random seeds, FedSAP reaches 76 .00% and 72 .67% mean global accuracy on Digits and Office-Caltech, exceeding the strongest baseline by 1.70 

and 4.92 percentage points while supporting client pruning ratios of up to 80% across heterogeneous clients. 

## Introduction 

Federated learning (FL) trains a shared model across edge devices without centralizing their raw data (McMahan et al. 2017; Huang et al. 2024). Real deployments, however, couple two forms of heterogeneity (Yue et al. 2026; Ye et al. 2023). Devices differ in computation, memory, and energy bud-gets (Ilhan, Su, and Liu 2023), so a model that fits a server-grade client may be infeasible on a low-end device (Yang et al. 2026). Their data also arise from different environ-ments (Li et al. 2025): digit images vary from grayscale handwriting to street-view crops and synthetic renderings, while object images vary in camera, background, resolution, and style (Figure 1). Consequently, local updates encode both task knowledge and domain bias; indiscriminate aggregation can transfer the former while amplifying the latter. The two problems cannot be solved independently. Resource-adaptive FL assigns smaller subnetworks to weak devices (Bouacida et al. 2021; Diao, Ding, and Tarokh 2021; Jiang et al. 2022b), but efficiency-oriented pruning does not identify which retained features should transfer across do-mains. Federated domain-generalization methods improve robustness to distribution shifts (Caldarola, Caputo, and Ci-ccone 2022; Huang et al. 2023; Pourpanah et al. 2025), but usually require every client to train the same full model. DapperFL (Jia et al. 2024) connects these settings through model-fusion pruning and domain-adaptive regularization, enabling differently compressed clients under domain shift. Yet a compression ratio specifies only how much capacity re-mains; it does not specify where that capacity should reside or with whom each retained feature should be shared. This missing allocation decision creates three concrete failure modes. A uniform layer policy can spend scarce ca-pacity on low-utility layers; a single aggregation path can overwrite domain-sensitive channels with incompatible up-dates; and shape reconstruction can align tensors without determining which clients should share those channels. Ad-dressing coupled heterogeneity therefore requires a joint de-cision over layer budget, channel role, and sharing scope. We therefore propose FedSAP (Fed erated learning with 

Structured Adaptive Partitioning), a domain-aware pruning framework that turns a fixed client budget into a structured allocation across layers and sharing roles. FedSAP first as-signs more active channels to layers with higher estimated utility. It then partitions channels into three states: stable and useful channels are Global , useful but domain-sensitive channels are Private , and low-utility channels are Dropped .Domain-Guided Assignment (DGA) infers pseudo-domain groups from shallow-gradient similarity, and Type-Matched Aggregation shares Global channels across all clients while restricting Private-channel aggregation to the corresponding pseudo-domain group. Under client pruning ratios from 0% 

to 80% , FedSAP improves mean global accuracy on both evaluated benchmarks. ## Related Work 

Resource-adaptive federated learning (FL). FL on edge devices is challenged by large heterogeneity in computa-tional, memory, and communication resources across clients. A common solution is to assign clients subnetworks of dif-ferent sizes. HeteroFL (Diao, Ding, and Tarokh 2021) trains width-scaled local models under a shared global architecture, while FjORD (Horvath et al. 2021) uses ordered dropout to obtain nested subnetworks for clients with different bud-gets. FedRolex (Alam et al. 2022) rotates partial subnetworks across rounds to improve the coverage of global parameters. Others construct personalized or importance-aware submod-els, such as FedP3 (Yi et al. 2024), FIARSE (Wu et al. 2024), and pFedGate (Chen et al. 2023). These methods improve the practicality of FL in the presence of system heterogene-ity, but their primary focus is efficiency or personalization in conventional non-IID settings. They generally overlook explicitly model domain shifts in which clients differ in vi-sual style, background, viewpoint, or acquisition conditions. Thus, a compact model optimized for one domain may dis-card channels that are important for another domain. 

FL under domain shift. Domain generalization aims to learn representations transferable to unseen domains. In FL, this problem is even harder because source domains are distributed across clients, and raw data cannot be cen-tralized. Existing federated domain generalization methods mainly use representation regularization, prototype align-ment, contrastive learning, or gradient-based aggregation. FedSR (Caldarola, Caputo, and Ciccone 2022) regularizes latent representations to reduce domain-sensitive variation, and FPL (Huang et al. 2023) aligns class-level semantics through prototypes. FedDAR (Zhong et al. 2023) learns a shared encoder with domain-wise prediction heads, while recent methods such as FedOMG (Nguyen et al. 2025) and FedGaLA (Pourpanah et al. 2025) exploit gradient signals to improve domain robustness without server-side data. Al-though these methods address domain shifts, they are devel-oped under an architecture-homogeneous full-model setting, assuming that all clients train the same backbone with iden-tical capacity. This limits their applicability to model-size heterogeneous edge systems, where weak clients may only support compact subnetworks. Our work instead studies do-main robustness under client-specific pruning budgets. 

Federated pruning with domain-aware aggregation. 

Model pruning is widely used in FL to reduce computation and communication costs. FedDrop (Bouacida et al. 2021), PruneFL (Jiang et al. 2022a), and FedMP (Jiang et al. 2022b) generate compact models under client resource constraints, but mainly treat pruning as a compression problem. They do not explicitly decide whether the retained capacity should support globally transferable or domain-specific knowledge. DapperFL (Jia et al. 2024) is the closest work to our set-ting. It combines Model Fusion Pruning with Domain Adap-tive Regularization and reconstructs heterogeneous pruned models before aggregation, so clients with different prun-ing ratios can participate under domain shifts. However, its pruning ratio mainly controls how much capacity each client retains, rather than where this limited capacity should be placed across layers. As a result, layers with different util-ity may still be compressed under a similar budget pattern. In addition, all retained channels follow the same aggrega-tion path after reconstruction, although some channels may encode globally transferable features while others capture domain-sensitive patterns. This makes it difficult to preserve both local specialization and global transfer simultaneously. CA-HFP (Hu et al. 2026) uses curvature-aware scores and model reconstruction to stabilize heterogeneous pruning, but its focus is on pruning robustness under statistical hetero-geneity. It does not explicitly model pseudo-domains or as-sign retained channels to domain-aware aggregation paths. In contrast, FedSAP allocates active channels non-uniformly across layers, assigns them to Global or Private aggregation paths, and Drops low-utility channels to satisfy each client’s capacity constraint during local training.

Adaptive Layer Budget and Selective Global Protection 

A client-level pruning ratio specifies how much capacity a client can retain, but it does not determine where this capac-ity should be preserved. This is important because different layers do not contribute equally after pruning: some lay-ers contain features that are broadly useful across clients, whereas others can absorb more compression with less im-pact on the final prediction. FedSAP therefore treats each client’s pruning ratio as a total-capacity budget, rather than as a fixed layer-wise pruning rule. For client i, the pruning ratio ρi gives the target keep ratio κi = 1 − ρi. Instead of applying the same κi to every layer, FedSAP allocates this budget across layers according to estimated utility. Main Results 

Superior global accuracy under system heterogeneity and domain shifts. Table 1 compares methods under heteroge-neous device capacities, with client pruning ratios ranging from 0% to 80% . Averaged over three random seeds, FedSAP obtains the highest global accuracy on both benchmarks. It reaches 76 .00% on Digits, 1.70 percentage points above Dap-perFL, and 72 .67% on Office-Caltech, a 4.92 -point gain. All gains are absolute points. The central result is that FedSAP improves average accuracy under aggressive heterogeneous compression and domain shift. 

Cross-domain gains and trade-offs. The global improve-ment is not uniform across domains. Relative to DapperFL, FedSAP gains 6.59 points on SYN, 10 .72 on Caltech, and 

6.66 on DSLR, but loses 5.94 on SVHN, 2.11 on Amazon, and 3.45 on Webcam. Other baselines also exceed FedSAP on some individual domains; for example, FPL is 7.23 points higher on SVHN, and NeFL is 6.90 points higher on Web-cam. This trade-off is expected because a fixed compression budget forces capacity to be redistributed: protecting chan-nels that improve transfer or specialization for one group can reduce the capacity available to another. FedSAP optimizes the reported cross-domain mean rather than worst-domain accuracy, so the main claim is improved global performance at heterogeneous compression rates—not per-domain domi-nance. The supplementary domain-wise budget experiment further shows that adaptive allocation helps most on SYN, the most budget-sensitive Digits domain. 

Layer-adaptive and role-aware pruning as a better design choice. The gains in the global mean support a more struc-tured view of pruning than client-level compression alone. Under the same budget, globally stable channels reduce es-timation variance by pooling data, Private channels reduce cross-domain bias by restricting aggregation, and Dropped channels satisfy the resource constraint. The supplemen-tary Conditional Analysis formalizes this bias–variance– compression interpretation, derives the role-selection con-dition, and states its assumptions and failure modes without asserting a universal neural-network guarantee.

## Conclusion 

FedSAP reframes heterogeneous federated pruning as a joint decision over layer capacity and sharing scope. Its Global, Private, and Dropped states respectively pool transferable evidence, preserve pseudo-domain specialization, and meet client budgets. Across three seeds, this allocation improves the global mean on Digits and Office-Caltech under client pruning ratios up to 80% , although it does not dominate every domain. These results support role-aware capacity allocation while leaving important boundaries for future study. 

Limitations First, DGA is a heuristic proxy for latent do-mains, not a domain-identification guarantee. First-layer gra-dients are well matched to shifts in texture, color, back-ground, or stroke, but may not separate domains defined by high-level semantics, label mechanisms, or long-range con-text. Second, transmitting per-client shallow gradients and channel statistics introduces privacy exposure beyond ordi-nary model updates: an honest-but-curious server may in-fer domain identity, class proportions, or sample attributes, and gradient inversion is possible in some regimes. Fed-SAP currently provides no formal privacy guarantee; secure aggregation, clipping with noise, statistic quantization, and attack-based privacy audits are necessary extensions. Third, the tri-state interpretation relies on estimated utility, channel stability, and sufficiently accurate pseudo-domain groups. In-correct assignments can replace useful global transfer with private estimation noise, or mix incompatible private up-dates. Finally, our evidence is limited to two vision bench-marks and ResNet-style backbones. The reported global mean can also hide worst-domain regressions, as Table 1 shows. Evaluation on semantic domain shifts, larger architec-tures, partial participation, imbalanced domains, and worst-domain or fairness-aware objectives is therefore needed. The supplementary Pseudo-Domain Error analysis bounds grouping error under stated assumptions, while Privacy Risk of Uploaded Signals discusses leakage channels, compatible defenses, and attack-based auditing requirements. ## A Comparison Frameworks 

We briefly introduce the comparison frameworks used in our experiments. 

FedAvg (McMahan et al. 2017) is the standard federated learning baseline. Each client performs local training and uploads its model update to the server, which then computes a data-size-weighted average to update the global model. 

FedProx (Li et al. 2020) extends FedAvg by adding a proxi-mal regularization term to the local objective. The proximal term constrains local models from drifting too far away from the global model, which is useful under statistical hetero-geneity. 

MOON (Li, He, and Song 2021) uses model-contrastive learning to reduce representation drift between local and global models. It encourages the current local model to stay close to the global model representation while moving away from previous local representations. 

FedSR (Caldarola, Caputo, and Ciccone 2022) improves fed-erated domain generalization by regularizing latent represen-tations. It penalizes domain-sensitive variations in feature representations and encourages robust features across source domains. 

FPL (Huang et al. 2023) introduces prototype-based learn-ing for federated domain generalization. It aligns class-level semantic prototypes across clients and improves generaliza-tion under domain shifts. 

FedDrop (Bouacida et al. 2021) reduces local computation and communication cost by training selected subsets of the global model on resource-constrained clients. It is the Adap-tive Federated Dropout baseline used in our comparison. 

FedMP (Jiang et al. 2022b) uses adaptive model pruning to support heterogeneous edge clients. It adjusts local model footprints according to client resource constraints. 

NeFL (Kang et al. 2025) scales nested subnetworks in both depth and width, allowing clients with different resource bud-gets to participate in FL. It is a representative heterogeneous FL method based on nested model scaling. 

DapperFL (Jia et al. 2024) is the closest baseline to our set-ting. It combines Model Fusion Pruning with Domain Adap-tive Regularization and reconstructs heterogeneous pruned models before aggregation. It supports clients with different pruning ratios under domain shifts, but does not explicitly allocate channel capacity across layers or separate retained channels by sharing role. 

FedSPU (Niu, Dong, and Qin 2025) is a personalized fed-erated learning method for resource-constrained devices. In-stead of training a smaller submodel through dropout-based pruning, FedSPU keeps the full model architecture and ran-domly freezes a subset of neurons during local training. We include it as an additional resource-constrained FL baseline. Since FedSPU is not designed for federated domain gener-alization, we evaluate it under the same multi-domain client split and report its global accuracy for comparison.

Network architectures. For Digits, we use a ResNet10 vari-ant with four residual stages. For Office-Caltech, we use ResNet18. Both networks include batch normalization and ReLU activations. The classifier dimension is adjusted ac-cording to the number of classes in each benchmark.

E.2 Pseudo-Domain Error and the Boundary of DGA 

The preceding benefit depends on the quality of π(k). If the per-channel risk is Lc-Lipschitz in its parameter and the diameter of candidate group parameters is at most Dc,assigning an ε fraction of domain mass to an incompatible Private group adds at most εL cDc to the weighted channel risk. This bound makes the failure mode explicit: a noisy grouping can erase the bias reduction that justified the Private state. Repeated grouping and exponential smoothing improve temporal stability, but they do not remove systematic proxy error. First-layer gradients are plausible domain signals when the shift changes local image statistics. Texture, color, back-ground, illumination, and stroke style directly affect early filters, which matches Digits and much of Office-Caltech. A semantically defined domain can violate this premise. For example, two domains may share the same low-level ap-pearance but differ in object co-occurrence, label semantics, scene context, or the conditional distribution P (Y | X).Their first-layer gradients can be similar even when later rep-resentations or classifier gradients differ. Conversely, a nui-sance appearance change can split clients that should share semantic features. The shallow-layer ablation in the main pa-per establishes robustness only for the evaluated appearance-driven benchmarks; it does not validate DGA for high-level semantic domains. Multi-layer similarity, representation pro-totypes, or uncertainty-aware soft grouping are appropriate extensions for that setting. 

E.3 What the Analysis Does and Does Not Establish 

The analysis supplies a mechanism-level explanation for im-proved average accuracy under domain shift: channel-wise sharing can reduce variance where domains agree, restrict negative transfer where they disagree, and allocate the re-maining budget to channels with the largest estimated utility. It also predicts the observed trade-off. Because the active-channel budget is fixed, improving the weighted mean can re-allocate capacity away from a particular domain, and pseudo-domain error can amplify that loss. Accordingly, FedSAP targets the global mean used by the evaluation protocol; it offers neither a monotonic improvement for every domain nor a worst-domain guarantee. 

## F Privacy Risk of Uploaded Signals 

FedSAP avoids transmitting raw examples, but this fact alone does not establish privacy. DGA exposes a client-indexed first-layer gradient, and SGP exposes client- or group-level activation and channel-utility statistics. Prior work shows that gradients can support reconstruction attacks (Zhu, Liu, and Han 2019; Geiping et al. 2020). In FedSAP, even when exact reconstruction is unsuccessful, an honest-but-curious server could use repeated shallow gradients to infer visual domain identity or coarse sample attributes. Channel activa-tion means and gradient magnitudes may also reveal class imbalance, feature prevalence, or changes in a client’s local population. Pseudo-domain membership itself is sensitive metadata in applications where a domain corresponds to a hospital, sensor, region, or demographic group. The current protocol assumes a trusted server for these auxiliary signals and provides no differential-privacy, cryp-tographic, or empirical attack-resistance guarantee. Its pri-vacy claim must therefore remain limited to avoiding di-rect raw-data transfer. The risk depends on batch size, local-epoch aggregation, the dimensionality and precision of the transmitted signal, the server’s prior knowledge, and whether signals are observable per client or only after aggregation; communication size is not a proxy for privacy. Several defenses are compatible with FedSAP but change its utility. Secure aggregation within candidate groups can hide individual statistics but requires a preliminary group-ing mechanism. Gradient clipping and calibrated noise can provide differential privacy, although noise can corrupt co-sine similarity and destabilize DGA. Quantization, random projection, infrequent updates, and transmitting similarity sketches rather than raw gradients can reduce exposure with-out constituting a formal guarantee. A complete deployment study should report domain/class inference and gradient-inversion attacks, privacy–utility curves, and the effect of each defense on grouping accuracy and worst-domain per-formance. 

## G Computation and Communication Overhead 

FedSAP introduces additional operations for DGA, channel-statistic collection, mask update, and type-matched aggrega-tion. We analyze their overhead relative to standard FedAvg under the Digits setting. 

Computation overhead. The dominant cost still comes from local training. For ResNet10 on 32 × 32 inputs, let F denote the FLOPs of one forward pass. With 10 clients, 10 local epochs, about 30 mini-batches per epoch, and batch size 64, the per-round local training cost is approximately 

10 × 10 × 30 × 64 × 3F, (14) where the factor 3 approximates forward and backward com-putation. DGA requires an additional gradient-based pass only every reassignment period. Since reassignment is per-formed every five rounds, its amortized overhead remains small compared with the full local training cost. In our es-timate, the total additional computation of FedSAP is about 

3% –5% over FedAvg. 

Server-side aggregation overhead. Type-matched aggrega-tion adds lightweight mask-based indexing and separate av-eraging for Global and Private channels. This affects aggre-gation rather than local model training. Since aggregation is much cheaper than repeated local forward-backward passes, its contribution to the total computation is minor. 

Communication overhead. FedSAP does not upload raw data or full additional models. Its extra payload consists of first-layer gradients for DGA, channel-level statistics, and tri-state masks. The gradient is transmitted only at DGA rounds, and the remaining statistics scale with the number of channels rather than parameters. For ResNet10, masks and channel statistics contain on the order of 10 3 values, whereas a model update contains millions of parameters. The resulting byte overhead is therefore small relative to standard model-update transmission. This size comparison is not a privacy guarantee; Appendix F analyzes the information that these signals may expose.
