## ABSTRACT 

Federated learning (FL) is a communication-efficient distributed learning paradigm. However, client drift remains one of the most critical challenges, hindering the efficient training of a global model. In this study, we propose a novel latent information sharing scheme that directly mitigates data heterogeneity across clients. Our theoretical and empirical results show that sharing a small amount of hidden-layer activations significantly improves training efficiency while preserving convergence guarantees and data privacy. Furthermore, we compare our method with existing FL approaches designed to address client drift, including FedProx, SCAFFOLD, FedPVR, FedProto, and SplitFed, and demonstrate superior model accuracy under a fixed round budget without incurring excessive communication overhead. Overall, this work presents a promising new knowledge aggregation scheme and provides a comprehensive analysis of the impact of activation sharing on federated optimization. 

## 1 Introduction 

While federated learning (FL) enables privacy-preserving and communication-efficient aggregation across multiple data holders, referred to as clients, its training efficiency largely depends on the degree of data heterogeneity among local datasets. Severe data heterogeneity leads to client drift, one of the most well-known challenges in FL, which significantly hinders convergence. To enable practical large-scale FL applications, it is crucial to address client drift without sacrificing the benefits of communication-efficient distributed learning. To mitigate data heterogeneity, prior works such as FedProx [1] and FedDyn [2] introduce proximal regularization to stabilize local updates. While these regularization approaches alleviate client drift to some extent, they do not fundamentally resolve discrepancies across clients, as they operate independently without explicit coordination. Other studies address data heterogeneity by reducing the stochastic variance of gradients, such as SCAFFOLD [3], FedVARP [4], and FedMDVR [5]. Although these methods effectively alleviate client drift, they often incur substantial memory and communication overhead, limiting their practicality in large-scale deployments. FedLAMA [6] proposes a structural approach based on layer-wise adaptive aggregation to implicitly mitigate client discrepancy. However, it still relies on parameter-space coordination and does not explicitly align intermediate representations across clients. These limitations motivate exploring alternative forms of collaboration beyond parameter-space coordination. In this study, we propose Activation Sharing Federated Learning (ASFL), a latent information sharing approach that tackles client drift from a novel representation-based perspective. Building upon the conventional model aggregation scheme, our approach additionally exchanges intermediate activations from a critical hidden layer. By directly mitigating data heterogeneity through this exchange, our method accelerates convergence, thereby achieving higher model accuracy within a fixed round budget. Specifically, our study addresses two key questions: (i) which layer generates the most

informative latent representations for exchange and (ii) how can we effectively leverage remote latent information without incurring prohibitive memory / communication overhead? Furthermore, we provide a theoretical analysis of the impact of activation sharing on the convergence properties of FedAvg for non-convex and smooth objective functions. To bridge the gap between theory and practice, we also discuss how to exploit activation sharing with minimal additional communication cost by reusing remote activations multiple times. Our extensive empirical study substantiates the practicality of activation sharing, demonstrating its superiority not only in convergence speed but also in system efficiency. On representative benchmarks, including CIFAR-10/100 [7], FEMNIST [8], and SVHN [9], under non-IID settings, ASFL consistently achieves higher accuracy than state-of-the-art FL methods, including FedProx [1], SCAFFOLD [3], FedPVR [10], FedProto [11], FedImpro [12], and SplitFed [13]. These results indicate that ASFL effectively mitigates client drift by allowing each client to learn from the latent representations of other clients, thereby achieving higher accuracy within a fixed round budget. In addition, our wall-clock time analysis shows that ASFL reduces the per-round training time by up to 33% compared to the other FL methods under the same number of local steps per round. Finally, we empirically show that, under model inversion attacks, ASFL exhibits privacy leakage comparable to that of existing representation-sharing methods such as SplitFed.

FedAvg [14] is a pioneering distributed learning method designed for federated optimization, utilizing local SGD with periodic model aggregations. It has been widely adopted to train global models without explicitly sharing decentralized data. However, it suffers from slow convergence when data heterogeneity is high. 

Client drift – Client drift is a well-known phenomenon in FL. In federated optimization, each client performs local updates using only its own data, which are often drawn from heterogeneous (non-IID) distributions. As a result, local updates become biased with respect to the global objective, leading to a mismatch between the expected local gradient and the true global gradient: 

E[git] = ∇Fi(xit)̸ = ∇F (xit), (2) where git, ∇Fi(xit), and ∇F (xit) denote the stochastic gradient, the local full-batch gradient, and the global full-batch gradient, respectively. This deviation becomes more pronounced as the degree of data heterogeneity increases or the number of local update steps grows, ultimately slowing down the convergence of the global model. To mitigate this issue, a variety of approaches have been proposed in the literature such as FedProx, SCAFFOLD, and SILVER [15]. 

## 3 Related work 

Variance reduction methods – Recent federated optimization methods mitigate client drift using variance reduction techniques, including FedProx, SCAFFOLD, FedPVR, FedVARP, and SILVER. Although effective, these methods typically introduce additional memory and communication overhead to maintain auxiliary states such as control or tracking variables, which becomes increasingly burdensome for large-scale models. FedProto [11] shares class-wise prototypes computed by averaging embeddings within each class and regularizes local training toward the aggregated global prototypes to reduce cross-client discrepancies in class representations. MOON [16] reduces client drift using a model-contrastive objective, at the cost of additional memory and computation overhead for maintaining auxiliary models and computing contrastive losses. In this work, we compare ASFL with these representative variance-reduced FL methods in terms of both model accuracy under a fixed round budget and communication overhead. 

Split learning methods – Split Federated Learning (SFL) integrates Split Learning (SL) [17] with Federated Learning (FL) to alleviate the computational burden on the client side. The most prominent difference between SFL and conventional FL is that SFL transmits intermediate activations from a specific ‘cut layer’ to a central server. The server then executes the forward and backward passes for the remaining layers. This split training mechanism not only reduces client-side computational overhead but also mitigates client drift, as the server-side sub-model is updated using aggregated knowledge from all participating clients. While SplitFed aggregates latent representations on the server and trains a subset of network layers there, our proposed method requires neither data centralization across clients nor server-side computation. 4.2 Controlling the degree of information sharing 

Activation Sharing is governed by two critical factors that determine its impact on local training: (i) which layer’s latent information is shared, and (ii) the volume of activation outputs being shared. 

4.2.1 Selecting the sharing layer via cost-variance trade-off 

The tail round exposes the sub-model z to latent information from other clients. These remote activations can be utilized to update z, thereby reducing the variance of stochastic gradients. Sharing activations at earlier layers allows a larger portion of the model to benefit from this variance reduction. However, this incurs higher computational overhead, as backpropagation must traverse more layers during the tail rounds. To address this trade-off, we propose a practical layer selection scheme that balances variance reduction and system efficiency.

4.2.2 Communication-efficient activation sharing 

Depending on the input dimensionality and layer connectivity, intermediate activations can incur substantial communi-cation overhead. To mitigate this cost, we propose a practical scheme that exchanges a minimal subset of activations and reuses them across multiple iterations within each communication round. In Algorithm 1, the size of Ij is B · τ ′/2, where B is the mini-batch size. Then, the tail round updates z using random mini-batches drawn from Ii ∪ Ij (lines 6–7). ## 5 Experiments 

Experimental settings – We evaluate ASFL on four benchmarks: CIFAR-10 (ResNet18 [20]), CIFAR-100 (Mo-bileNetV2 [21]), SVHN (ResNet20), and FEMNIST (CNN). Experiments involve 100 total clients, with 10 randomly activated per round. For non-IID settings, we use a Dirichlet distribution with α = 0 .1. Our framework is based on PyTorch 2.7.0 and runs on a server with two NVIDIA RTX 4070 GPUs. See Appendix A.1 for detailed settings. 

5.1 Comparative study FL benchmarks – Table 1 compares ASFL with SOTA FL methods, where PixelDP is not applied to the transmitted activations to isolate the effect of activation sharing on optimization efficiency. Drift-mitigation methods, including SCAFFOLD, FedProto, MOON, FedPVR, and FedVARP, improve accuracy over FedAvg on most benchmarks, but SCAFFOLD nearly doubles communication cost through control variate aggregation, and the other four offer relatively limited gains. SplitFed achieves higher accuracy by exchanging activations at every update step, but at the cost of substantially increased communication cost. In contrast, ASFL attains higher accuracy across all benchmarks while introducing only marginal additional communication cost. E.g., on CIFAR-10, ASFL improves accuracy by approximately 7.5% over FedAvg while exchanging 8 MB more activations per round. Extra experimental analyses supporting these findings are provided in Appendix A.5. 7t-SNE analysis – Figure 3 provides a t-SNE visualization of per-client class centroids in the latent space. Under FedAvg, representations of the same class are widely scattered, whereas SCAFFOLD shows only limited concentration. In contrast, ASFL forms noticeably tighter and more coherent clusters across different clients, suggesting superior cross-client representation alignment. Overall, these results demonstrate that activation sharing effectively alleviates data heterogeneity by promoting consistent representations, directly leading to the higher validation accuracy shown in Table 1.

## 6 Conclusion 

We propose ASFL, an activation sharing framework for FL that mitigates data heterogeneity across clients. Our theoret-ical analysis provides insights into its convergence behavior, and extensive experiments demonstrate its effectiveness across diverse FL benchmarks. While activation sharing may raise privacy concerns, we investigate this aspect using PixelDP, a recently proposed representative DP method, and provide empirical insights. Overall, our results suggest that activation sharing is a practical and effective approach for achieving fast and accurate FL across a wide range of applications. As the present study focuses on a practical gossip-based communication pattern, future work could extend this framework by aggregating activations at a central server and exploring alternative strategies for their utilization. 9References

A.1.2 Activation-sharing positions 

ASFL defines candidate activation-sharing positions at the level of backbone-specific layer blocks, using the same indexing convention as the x-axis of Figure 2. Each layer block is defined as the smallest architectural unit that preserves the backbone’s block structure and ends after a nonlinearity. The candidate position is indexed by the layer block at which the tail-side partial update starts. For a selected layer block b, ASFL forwards a mini-batch through the preceding blocks and exchanges the activation at the input of block b. The selected block is therefore included in the partial training range. The input boundary is excluded from the candidate set because it corresponds to exchanging the original mini-batch input rather than an intermediate activation. A.1.3 Activation-sharing quantity 

The number of shared batches controls the size of the peer activation pool exchanged during the tail round. Table 12 summarizes the effect of this quantity on accuracy and communication cost. In this ablation, the activation-sharing position is fixed to the selected layer block for each dataset–backbone pair, and only the number of shared batches is varied. One shared batch denotes the activations generated from one mini-batch, with the mini-batch size set according to Table 4. As described in the communication-efficient activation sharing scheme in Algorithm 1, the received peer activation infor-mation Ij is reused when constructing the random mini-batches for the tail round. Thus, the number of shared batches in Table 12 controls the amount of peer activation information exchanged through the directed gossip communication pattern, rather than the total number of tail-round mini-batches used to update the tail subnetwork. 

A.1.4 Communication and computational cost 

For each candidate activation-sharing position, we compute the communication cost as the number of activation elements exchanged at that position. Specifically, for a candidate layer block b, the transmitted activation is the input to block b, and its communication cost is measured as the product of its tensor dimensions. For the computational cost, we estimate the backpropagation cost of the tail subnetwork starting from each candidate layer block. The estimate focuses on the convolutional and linear layers included in the tail subnetwork, which are the main parameterized computational components of the backbone. We use this layer-wise estimate to compare the relative cost of tail-side partial training across candidate positions. Figure 2 visualizes the resulting normalized cost curve together with the cumulative gradient-variance curve for layer-block selection.

27 A.3 Communication pattern A.3.1 Discussion of gossip-style communication 

We implement latent-space information sharing through a gossip-style communication pattern [23]. Rather than relying on a centralized collector or broadcasting latent information to all clients, each active client receives latent-space information from one randomly selected active client at each communication round. Specifically, at round r, each active client i uniformly samples one source client j, where j̸ = i, and receives the latent-space information I(r) 

> j

generated by client j. Here, the directed communication link j → i indicates that latent-space information flows from the sampled source client j to the receiving client i.This communication pattern is particularly suitable for federated learning scenarios with a large number of edge devices. In such environments, clients are naturally distributed and can transfer latent-space information through local client-level connections rather than persistent global connectivity. Our method organizes the transfer of Ii over randomly formed directed communication links while preserving the standard federated training procedure. Thus, the proposed method should be understood as latent-space information sharing over a directed gossip-style communication graph within the standard federated training procedure. This gossip-style communication pattern has two practical advantages over centralized communication. First, it does not incur blocking. In contrast, when activations are aggregated at a central server and redistributed to clients, blocking is inevitable and increases with the number of clients. Therefore, our design offers better scalability than typical centralized approaches. Second, it enables more robust communication in FL environments. Even if a client is disconnected, gossip-style latent information sharing does not stall the entire system, resulting in a more robust training process. Nevertheless, exploring alternative communication patterns for latent information sharing remains a promising direction for future work. 28 A.4 Additional discussion on privacy

A.4.1 Impact of activation sharing layer on performance 

Beyond the choice of noise mechanism and budget, the selection of the network layer at which activations are extracted and shared has a significant impact on the utility of ASFL. To investigate this, we vary the partition point across layers of the network and evaluate three configurations: (i) ASFL (no noise), (ii) ASFL+Laplace ( ϵ = 0 .2, highest privacy), and (iii) ASFL+Gaussian ( ϵ = 0 .5, δ = 0 .001 ). The results across all datasets are reported in Tables 7–10. For each configuration, we additionally report the Laplace Drop and Gaussian Drop, defined as the absolute accuracy degradation incurred by adding Laplace or Gaussian noise relative to the noise-free ASFL baseline, respectively. As the partition layer is moved deeper into the network, the performance of ASFL generally degrades. For example, on CIFAR-10, the accuracy of ASFL decreases from 79 .6% at the earlier partition point to 66 .0% at a deeper layer. This behavior can be attributed to two complementary factors. First, deeper layers produce more task-specific, lower-dimensional representations that carry less transferable information across heterogeneous clients. Second, the activations of deeper layers lie in a lower-dimensional manifold, reducing the diversity benefit of activation sharing from a paired client. In contrast, earlier layers produce richer representations that are more amenable to cross-client transfer, thereby better alleviating data heterogeneity. Moreover, the performance drop induced by noise injection is reported in Tables 7–10. Both the Laplace Drop and Gaussian Drop decrease as the partition point moves deeper into the network. On CIFAR-10, the Laplace Drop reduces from 11 .47% at the earliest layer to 0.66% at the deepest layer, and the Gaussian Drop similarly narrows from 12 .47% 

29 to 0.73% . A consistent pattern is observed on CIFAR-100, where the Gaussian Drop decreases from 5.04% at the input layer to 0.89% at the final layer. This behavior can be explained by the reduction of the progressive compression of representations across network depth as representations become more compressed and task-specific. The injected noise has a proportionally smaller disruptive effect on the downstream task. In other words, activations in deeper layers exhibit reduced sensitivity to privacy perturbations. This finding suggests a practical trade-off that earlier layers offer higher utility for activation sharing but require more careful noise calibration to preserve privacy, while deeper layers are inherently more noise-tolerant but yield diminishing collaboration benefits.

A.4.2 Model inversion attack on shared activations 

We examine the privacy behavior of ASFL under an optimization-based model inversion attack to assess whether Gaussian perturbation reduces visually recoverable information in shared latent representations. An adversary observing these representations may attempt to reconstruct the corresponding private inputs. 30 Model inversion attacks in the image domain are commonly categorized into optimization-based and generative approaches. Optimization-based methods directly optimize an input image so that its model response matches either a target intermediate representation [24] or a target model output, such as a class confidence score [25]. In contrast, generative methods introduce a learned image prior and perform inversion in the latent space of a generator, thereby constraining the reconstruction toward the natural image manifold [26, 27]. In this experiment, we consider an optimization-based model inversion attack that reconstructs inputs by matching shared intermediate features. We provide an additional ablation study on the construction of the augmented activation pool used in communication-efficient activation sharing. As described in Section 4.2.2, ASFL exchanges only a small subset of remote activations and reuses them within each communication round to reduce communication overhead. Since the number of received remote activations can be smaller than the desired number of remote samples in the augmented pool, the construction strategy influences how effectively the model incorporates newly received remote information without being dominated by local activations. We compare three strategies for handling the received remote activations. The first strategy, denoted as Random , samples remote activations uniformly at random with replacement until the desired number of remote samples is reached. This is the strategy adopted in ASFL. The second strategy, denoted as Replication , repeats the received remote activation pool until it reaches the required size and then uses the leading portion. The third strategy, denoted as Over-sampling ,directly uses the received remote activation pool without additional resampling, even when the number of remote activations is smaller than the target size.
