# FAIL BENCH : Evaluating Fault Tolerance Across Distributed Training Architectures 

Khaled Aljbab and Amine Barrak 

Abstract —Distributed deep learning relies on data, pipeline, tensor, and hybrid parallelism, yet fault-tolerance mechanisms are typically evaluated only on the architecture for which they were designed. This leaves practitioners with little guidance when choosing mechanisms across architectures. FAIL BENCH 

provides a unified evaluation harness covering seven distributed training architectures, eight crash-fault-tolerance mechanisms and a no-FT baseline, and single, concurrent, and cascading fail-stop failures. We evaluate 142 (architecture, mechanism, trace) combinations on an 8 ×V100 cluster, with per-rank checkpoint states ranging from 205 MB to 2.7 GB. Three findings emerge. First, no mechanism is universally best: on A2, disk checkpointing has the lowest steady-state overhead (0.5%), in-memory replication restores fastest ( ∼17 ms), and just-in-time checkpointing avoids periodic steady-state checkpoint cost but incurs ∼0.9 s upon failure. When process-group re-formation takes seconds, mechanisms differ more in runtime overhead than restore speed. Second, gossip training increases sample throughput by 17.9% after losing a worker, yet shows no detectable improvement in loss progress over a matched no-fault baseline. Third, mechanism cost depends strongly on architecture: in-memory replication overhead ranges from 3.7% to 176%. We translate these findings into a decision framework for selecting fault-tolerance mechanisms and release F AIL BENCH 

as an open artifact. 

Index Terms —Distributed deep learning, fault tolerance, checkpointing, redundancy, empirical study, benchmark. 

I. I NTRODUCTION 

# TRAINING large neural networks has become an exercise in endurance computing. Modern language and vision models have grown from millions to hundreds of billions of parameters in under a decade [1], [2], and neither the model nor the dataset fits on a single accelerator any longer. Prac-titioners therefore rely on distributed training: the model, the data, or both are partitioned across many GPUs that exchange gradients or activations on every training step. The dominant parallelism strategies (data, pipeline, and tensor parallelism, often composed into three-dimensional hybrids [3], [4]) each carry their own communication pattern, memory layout, and failure topology. Frontier-scale runs span weeks of wall time on thousands of GPUs [5], consume megawatts of power, and cost millions of dollars per training cycle. At this scale, failure is routine rather than exceptional. Meta’s OPT-175B log reports roughly one hundred hardware failures over a two-month run [1]; the Llama-3 405B campaign was interrupted more than 400 times in 54 days, dominated by GPU and HBM failures [2]; and reports from ByteDance [5]

and Alibaba [6] document crashes, link drops, fail-slow strag-glers, and silent data corruption. In response, the systems and machine learning communities have produced a collection of fault tolerance (FT) mechanisms, cataloged by several surveys [7]–[9]. The common approach periodically saves model and optimizer state and replays from the latest checkpoint after a failure; recent work accelerates it through in-memory replication [10], asynchronous and lazy disk writes [11], [12], and just-in-time capture triggered by an impending preemption [13]. A second family duplicates the work, running each pipeline stage on more than one rank so a surviving peer takes over immediately [14]–[16]. A third applies coding theory, distributing redundant computation so that any sufficient subset of workers reconstructs the aggregate update [17]. A fourth accepts convergence cost in exchange for graceful degradation: decentralized parallel SGD [18], PyTorch Elastic [19], and Horovod Elastic [20] keep training alive at a smaller cluster size instead of crashing. Despite this diversity, the literature shares a methodological limitation: each mechanism is evaluated only inside its own design space . Checkpoint systems are compared against other checkpoint variants [10], [11], redundancy systems against vanilla pipeline parallelism [14], [15], and gossip-SGD inside the peer-to-peer overlay [18], [21]. No published study mea-sures these mechanisms head-to-head across the spectrum of training architectures on the same cluster under a common fault schedule. A practitioner who has chosen an architecture for performance reasons therefore has no quantitative basis for picking the FT strategy that minimizes expected wall time given the cluster’s mean time between failures. We propose F AIL BENCH , a harness for evaluating crash-fault tolerance across distributed training architectures under controlled fault schedules. It covers 133 of the 171 viable (architecture, mechanism, trace) tuples plus all nine struc-turally infeasible tuples. Four conditions bound what follows. We inject fail-stop crashes only, so Byzantine behavior, silent data corruption, and fail-slow stragglers are out of scope. The mechanisms span checkpointing, state replication, and reconfiguration, but not coded computation, which needs an aggregation topology we do not implement. The schedule is fixed across architectures except for injection time, which scales with per-rank state size so reactive mechanisms can finish an emergency save before the crash. We measure at 8 GPUs on 0.2–2.7 GB of per-rank checkpoint state, parameters plus optimizer buffers, so the transferable results are structural: which architectures halt, which pairings are infeasible, and the shape of the overhead-versus-recovery trade-off, rather than absolute overheads at frontier scale. arXiv:2610.07688v1 [cs.DC] 6 Oct 2026 2

Contributions. This paper makes four contributions. 1) FAIL BENCH , a unified harness that pairs seven distrib-uted training architectures with eight crash-fault toler-ance mechanisms and a no-FT baseline under a shared fault injector, enabling the first cross-cutting comparison on identical hardware and a common trace family. 2) A compatibility map of 63 architecture–mechanism pairs that distinguishes viable, degenerate, and structurally infeasible configurations, including three infeasible pairs yielding nine tuples across the trace family. 3) A 142-tuple empirical study showing that (i) six config-urations halt completely on a single worker crash, while A1-async and A6 gossip degrade gracefully and gos-sip’s raw throughput rises post-fault without improving learning efficiency, and (ii) disk, in-memory, and JIT checkpointing form a Pareto frontier ranging from zero periodic checkpoint work to 3.8% measured steady-state overhead, with 17 –900 ms restore time, on which no mechanism dominates. 4) A measurement-grounded decision framework that translates the empirical results into principled fault-tolerance selection, minimizing expected job completion time under user-supplied failure rate, state size, check-point interval, and service-level constraints. We release F AIL BENCH , all configurations, fault schedules, and the harvesting and analysis scripts, together with the rollup outputs behind every table, as an open artifact. 1

II. B ACKGROUND 

This section summarizes distributed training architectures and fault-tolerance mechanisms considered in F AIL BENCH .

A. Distributed Training Architectures 

Modern DDL parallelizes SGD through data , pipeline ,and tensor parallelism. F AIL BENCH studies seven resulting architectures that differ in communication pattern and failure exposure. 

(A1) Parameter Server, synchronous and asynchronous. 

Workers compute local gradients and send them to a pa-rameter server, which applies the optimizer update and dis-tributes weights. Rank 0 hosts the optimizer and ranks 

1, . . . , N −1 are workers. The synchronous variant uses 

torch.distributed.gather and broadcast over NCCL and waits for all workers. The asynchronous variant uses per-worker communication over gloo/TCP and applies updates without waiting for every worker. Both are built on 

torch.distributed , without ps-lite or BytePS. Failure impact therefore depends both on whether the failed rank is the server or a worker and on the synchronization policy. 

(A2) Ring All-Reduce. Workers form a ring and exchange gradient shards through a bandwidth-efficient collective. We use PyTorch DDP over NCCL 2.20. Because all ranks partic-ipate in every collective, a single worker failure stalls training until the ring is repaired. 

> 1https://zenodo.org/records/23025990

(A3) Hierarchical / Tree All-Reduce. We use the same PyTorch DDP/NCCL compute path as A2 but force 

NCCL_ALGO=Tree . The resulting logical tree reduces gra-dients upward and broadcasts them downward. Unlike a ring, failure impact depends on tree position, with internal-node loss generally more disruptive than leaf loss. 

(A4) Pipeline Parallelism (PP). The model is parti-tioned by layer depth across workers, while micro-batches flow through the resulting pipeline. Our implementation uses 

torch.distributed point-to-point communication: acti-vations move forward through dist.send /dist.recv and gradients backward through the same primitives. Each rank hosts one stage of a custom feed-forward chain rather than ResNet-50 because variable-shape convolutional activations do not pipeline cleanly. Losing any stage halts the pipeline. 

(A5) Tensor Parallelism (TP). Individual layers are split across workers using a hand-rolled torch.distributed 

implementation of the Megatron transposed-MLP pattern: a column-parallel linear layer, GELU, and a row-parallel linear layer whose partial outputs are combined with all_reduce .We use a four-block transposed-MLP stack. Every rank in the TP group participates in each step, so losing one rank breaks the distributed matrix operation. 

(A6) Decentralized Peer-to-Peer (D-PSGD). Workers maintain local models and average parameters with neighbors over a gossip topology [18]. We implement D-PSGD using raw 

torch.distributed with per-edge Gloo subgroups. Each rank performs a local SGD update and exchanges post-update parameters with live ring neighbors under a per-round timeout, averaging parameters that arrive in time. Timed-out neighbors are removed from the live set and the overlay reconfigures without a central coordinator. The lack of a global collective enables graceful degradation after worker loss. 

(A7) 3D Hybrid (DP ×PP ×TP). Large-model training commonly combines data, pipeline, and tensor parallelism. We compose A4’s pipeline implementation, A5’s tensor paral-lelism, and a data-parallel all_reduce across replicas. At world size 8, the configuration is DP × PP × TP = 2 × 2 × 2.Its failure topology therefore combines all three axes, and recovery cost depends on the axis containing the failed worker. 

B. Fault Tolerance Mechanisms 

We study eight crash-fault-tolerance mechanisms, plus a no-FT baseline (FT9), spanning checkpointing, state replication, and runtime reconfiguration. Table I summarizes them. 

Checkpoint-class recovery (FT1–FT4): (FT1) Periodic disk checkpoint. Every T steps, model, optimizer, and RNG state are serialized to a shared file system and restored after failure. Persistence cost scales with model size, while lost work is bounded by T steps; Young–Daly theory gives the optimal interval under exponential failure arrivals [22], [23]. 

(FT2) In-memory replication. The latest checkpoint is mirrored to a peer worker’s GPU or host memory [10], avoiding file-system access during recovery at the cost of peer memory and communication. 

(FT3) Asynchronous / lazy disk checkpoint. State is staged in a CPU-side buffer and persisted by a background 3

thread, removing the synchronous disk flush from the train-ing step [11], [12]. This allows shorter effective checkpoint intervals at comparable throughput cost. 

(FT4) Just-in-time checkpoint. State is captured only when a preemption signal arrives [13], requiring no periodic steady-state checkpoint work. Our implementation captures the lead-time-reactive behavior of Bamboo/Gemini-JIT but not shadow-rank lineage replay. It therefore incurs emergency state-transfer cost and cannot protect against unannounced crashes. 

State replication (FT5–FT6): (FT5) Continuous state replication. The full model and optimizer state are mirrored to a peer at every training step. FT5 serves as a replication-cost proxy for compute-redundant systems such as Bamboo and Oobleck [14]–[16]: it captures their steady-state bandwidth and memory costs without duplicating shadow-rank computa-tion. Consequently, it is faithful on steady-state overhead but pessimistic on recovery latency. 

(FT6) k-replica state redundancy. State is written to k peer replicas on each save ( k = 2 ), allowing any surviving replica to supply recovery state. FT6 provides state redundancy rather than Reed–Solomon gradient coding [17], [24]; our harness therefore does not perform coded-gradient reconstruction or a head-to-head gradient-coding comparison. 

Reconfiguration and baseline (FT7–FT9): (FT7) Data re-distribution. After failure, surviving ranks rebalance their data partitions and continue at a smaller world size. This applies only to architectures with per-rank data shards. 

(FT8) Elastic process group. PyTorch Elastic [19] and Horovod Elastic dynamically re-form the process group after worker loss, allowing training to resume on the reduced cluster without application-level intervention. 

(FT9) No-FT baseline. FT9 attaches no fault-tolerance ma-chinery at all: every hook is a no-op and recovery is declined, so the runner propagates the underlying training exception. It is the control against which the other eight mechanisms are measured, and it is what exposes each architecture’s inherent failure behaviour in RQ1. Asynchronous gradient submission without a barrier [25], [26] is a property of the A1-async 

architecture rather than an FT mechanism, and is treated as such throughout. We call FT1–FT8 mechanisms and FT9 the baseline; the compatibility matrix records which architecture–mechanism pairs are viable, degenerate, or structurally infeasible. III. R ELATED WORK 

We group prior crash-FT work into checkpointing, redun-dancy and coding, and decentralized or elastic optimization. 

A. Fault Tolerance in Distributed Deep Learning 

Survey treatments catalog the design space without re-porting empirical comparisons [7]–[9]; large-scale production reports [1], [2], [5], [6] motivate the problem but do not themselves compare mechanisms. 

Checkpoint-based recovery. Checkpoint–restart has been the dominant fault-tolerance strategy in HPC since the 1970s, and the classical theory of optimal checkpoint placement under exponential failure arrivals is due to Young and Daly [22], [23]. General-purpose transparent checkpointing tools such as DMTCP [27] predate the deep-learning era and serve as baselines for DDL-specific systems. Within DDL, modern work targets the multi-terabyte state of large models. Check-Freq [28] adapts the checkpoint interval to keep persistence cost near zero. Gemini [10] replicates the checkpoint into peer GPU and host memory to make restoration near-instantaneous. DataStates-LLM [11] and ByteCheckpoint [12] push the save path off the critical path through asynchronous and lazy persistence, and just-in-time checkpointing [13] captures state only when a preemption signal arrives. Each of these systems is benchmarked against other checkpoint variants on the ar-chitecture and workload that showcase its design choice. 

Proactive redundancy and coded computing. A second group of systems pre-provisions redundancy so that a failed worker’s role is taken over immediately instead of being recovered from a checkpoint. Bamboo [14] and Oobleck [15] replicate pipeline stages onto pre-warm backups. Varuna [16] layers redundant scheduling on top of cloud preemption, and SWIFT [29] explores redundancy in the tensor-parallel setting. At the algorithm layer, gradient coding [17] and coded matrix multiplication [24] apply erasure codes to worker gra-dients so that any sufficient subset reconstructs the aggregate update, providing straggler tolerance with provable bounds. Each of these systems is evaluated against vanilla parallelism in the regime the system targets; cross-comparison against checkpoint-class mechanisms is uncommon. 

Benchmarks and fault injection. Community benchmarks such as MLPerf [30] standardize performance comparison across training systems but do not inject faults or char-acterize recovery; general-purpose chaos-engineering tools such as Chaos Mesh [31] inject faults but are agnostic to training semantics and report no training-specific metrics. FAIL BENCH occupies the intersection: MLPerf-style fixed configuration disclosure combined with controlled fault sched-ules and recovery-specific metrics. Orthogonally, a line of work addresses non-crash failure models: Byzantine-robust aggregation [32]–[35] and silent data corruption [36], [37]. These we deliberately scope out: F AIL BENCH injects fail-stop crashes only, and its mechanisms assume sane survivors. 

Decentralized, asynchronous, and elastic optimization. 

A third group avoids tightly-coupled synchronization in the first place, accepting some convergence cost in exchange for graceful degradation. Decentralized parallel SGD [18] replaces 4

the all-reduce with peer-to-peer averaging over a gossip over-lay, and subsequent theoretical work bounds the convergence penalty under topology changes and worker failures [21], [38]. Asynchronous parameter servers [25] and Hogwild! [26] tolerate stragglers and crashes by design at the cost of stale gradients. PyTorch Elastic [19] and Horovod Elastic [20] ex-pose explicit process-group reconfiguration APIs so the cluster can grow and shrink during training; production frameworks such as Megatron-LM [3] and DeepSpeed [4] layer elasticity hooks on top of these primitives. The works above typically evaluate mechanisms within the design space they target: checkpoint work against other checkpoint variants, redundancy work against vanilla paral-lelism, and gossip-SGD within the peer-to-peer overlay. In this paper, we instead vary both the training architecture and fault-tolerance mechanism on the same cluster (the framework sec-tion describes the 7 ×9 compatibility matrix and 142 reported tuples). Gradient coding requires coded-gradient arithmetic and a compatible aggregation topology that our harness does not implement, so we do not report a head-to-head comparison. The 7 ×9 matrix further distinguishes viable, degenerate, and structurally infeasible (S) architecture–mechanism pairs. The latter are explicitly considered but cannot execute without redesigning the architecture or mechanism, exposing com-patibility constraints that per-mechanism evaluations do not systematically characterize. IV. T HE FAIL BENCH FRAMEWORK 

FAIL BENCH pairs distributed-training architectures with fault-tolerance mechanisms under a common fault injector and metric collector. The implementation is released as an artifact. 

A. Design Goals 

Four goals guide the design: common interfaces , with ar-chitectures and mechanisms implemented as plug-ins behind two abstract base classes; determinism , where a seed and fault schedule reproduce a run up to CUDA floating-point nondeterminism; architecture-agnostic injection , where faults target ranks rather than framework internals; and comparable metrics , collected under consistent definitions.

This section describes the execution platform, fault-injection protocol, workloads, sweep parameters, metrics, and statistical procedures used in the evaluation. 

A. Execution Platform: Matilda HPC 

All experiments run on Oakland University’s Matilda HPC cluster. Each compute node has four NVIDIA Tesla V100 PCIe 16 GB GPUs, 32 CPU cores, and 192 GB of host memory, interconnected by HDR InfiniBand and BeeGFS. NCCL ring all-reduce achieves 9.7 GB/s effective bus bandwidth on 8 GPUs across 2 nodes (16 MiB tensors, 100 iterations). Jobs are submitted through SLURM, with one array task per (architecture, mechanism, fault-trace, seed) cell. The software stack comprises PyTorch 2.3.0, CUDA 12.1, NCCL 2.20.5, and Python 3.10. All architectures run as FAIL BENCH plug-ins over this stack.

C. Workloads 

Workloads are chosen per architecture family to preserve relevant inter rank tensor shapes. Data parallel architectures (A1, A2, A3, A6) train ResNet 50 on synthetic data with micro batch 32 per rank and fp32 SGD with momentum 0.9, yielding approximately 200 MB of state per rank. To exercise A4, A5, and A7 under realistic pipeline, tensor, and 3D parallel settings, we use Megatron style MLP stacks with 2.5 to 2.7 GB of state per rank. The MLPs reproduce the Transformer feed forward sublayer through column parallel expansion, element wise nonlinearity, and row parallel contraction. They preserve properties rele-vant to fault tolerance, including per rank state size, tensor shapes, communication volume, collectives, and process group topology, while omitting attention, embeddings, and sequence processing. Thus, they capture the communication and state behavior of large Transformer training without implementing a complete language model. 

D. Sweep Parameters 

All experiments use 8 V100 GPUs across two nodes. Across the study, viable configurations are evaluated under crash traces F1, F2, and F3, with architecture specific layouts but fixed GPU count: 1 PS and 7 workers for A1, an 8 rank ring for A2, an 8-rank tree for A3, an 8 stage pipeline for A4, a TP group of 8 for A5, an 8 peer gossip overlay for A6, and a DP ×PP ×TP = 2 ×2×2 mesh for A7. 

E. Statistical Protocol 

Sample sizes range from n = 1 to 7, depending on wall clock cost and experiment type. Structurally infeasible ( S) cells are confirmed by smoke runs and provide no viable Trestore .FT7 and FT8 activate only after a failure and thus add no steady state overhead. Their Trestore values are analytical lower bounds rather than direct measurements because recovery depends on external rendezvous and process reinitialization that the runner cannot execute, such as PyTorch Elastic or a c10d rendezvous server. Crash experiments with no fault tolerance mechanism en-abled report the mean with a 95% Student’s t confidence interval. Fault tolerance mechanism experiments report the mean and half range, (max − min) /2, because their smaller sample sizes do not support a reliable normality assumption. Outliers are retained, and runs using the same seed replay the same deterministic fault schedule.

We report four metrics: steady state overhead ( SS overhead ), time to detect ( TTD ), effective state restore cost Trestore , and recovery time Trecover :

G. Restore Time Model and Validation 

On the halting architectures, we cannot observe Trestore after a worker crash. Surviving ranks block on communication with the failed rank until PyTorch’s 600 s process-group watchdog fires. The watchdog invokes std::terminate from a C++ thread, killing the process before the Python recovery path can log the restore event. We therefore estimate Trestore from the mechanism’s dominant state transfer path. For in memory FT2 and the restore component of FT4, the estimate is state size divided by the PCIe Gen 3 band-width ( ∼12 GB/s). For disk based FT1 and FT3, it is state size divided by the measured filesystem read bandwidth. For FT4, the measured emergency save cost is added separately when reporting its effective restore cost. When the recovery supervisor observes training resume, Trecover is measured di-rectly. Otherwise, recovery is reported as a lower bound by combining analytical Trestore with the estimated process group reformation cost TPG and mechanism reinitialization cost Tinit .

A. Motivation 

Before evaluating fault-tolerance mechanisms, we first char-acterize each architecture’s inherent response to worker loss: whether surviving ranks can continue training and how quickly they observe the failure. This no-FT baseline establishes what a recovery mechanism must improve upon and reveals how the communication topology itself shapes failure exposure. 

B. Approach 

For each architecture, we run the corresponding workload without an FT mechanism and inject F1, a single SIGKILL of one worker rank. The fault is injected at t = 30 s, except for A6, where it is delayed to t = 180 s so its per-edge subgroups reach stable pre-fault throughput. We record whether surviving ranks continue training, time-to-detect (TTD), post-fault throughput, and final job outcome. TTD is the interval from SIGKILL to the first failure indication observed by a surviving rank or failure detector. Sample sizes are n = 5 for the six halting configurations, n = 3 for A1-async, and n = 7 for A6. Table V summarizes the F1 measurements: pre-fault throughput, TTD, post-fault throughput, aggregate slowdown, and whether training halts or degrades gracefully. 

C. Results 

Configurations that depend on the failed rank halt after a single worker failure. A1-sync, A2, A3, A4, A5, and A7 halt under F1, with no surviving seed resuming training. In each case, surviving ranks eventually block on communication that requires the failed rank: parameter-server synchronization in A1-sync, all-reduce in A2/A3/A5, pipeline communication in A4, and one or more of the DP, PP, and TP communication paths in A7. VII. RQ2: R ESILIENCE –C OST TRADE -OFFS 

A. Motivation 

Once an architecture is fixed, choosing an FT mecha-nism is a two-axis trade-off: continuous steady-state overhead 

(storage, network, redundant compute) during normal training versus recovery latency (Trestore ) once a fault fires. Without a quantitative view of how alternative mechanisms compare on these two axes, the practitioner cannot tell whether two mechanisms are substitutes or complements, or whether a given mechanism is even Pareto-competitive. Existing FT papers each report a favorable single point on this trade-off for the mechanism they propose [10], [13], [14], [17]; head-to-head comparisons across mechanism families on the same workload are uncommon. RQ2 reconstructs this frontier empirically on a fixed deployment. 

B. Approach 

We hold every choice except the FT mechanism constant. Workload: ResNet-50 on a synthetic dataset, micro-batch 32, fp32 SGD with momentum ( ∼205 MB per-rank state). Cluster: 8×V100 across 2 nodes. Architecture: A2 Ring-AR; disk-based mechanisms use BeeGFS scratch. Fault trace: F1, a single SIGKILL fail-stop at t = 30 s. On this deployment we evaluate every configuration marked V in Table II. Per-cell seed count n is scaled to wall-clock cost: n = 6 for FT2, FT3, FT5 and FT6, where seed variance is informative, and n = 3 for FT1 and FT4. FT7 (data redistribution) and FT8 (elastic reconfiguration) are reactive-only and reported analyt-ically. We log SS overhead (relative throughput loss vs. the failure-free RQ1 baseline, Table V), effective Trestore , per-save cost, and expected lost work, then place each configuration on the (SS, Trestore ) plane and identify the frontier. We run two nested experiments. First , FT1 and FT2 use the same 100-step full-state checkpoint but different persistence paths: BeeGFS for FT1 and peer pinned host memory via gloo for FT2. This tests whether in-memory replication (Gemini-style [10]) dominates disk or forms a Pareto trade-off. Second ,all remaining V configurations are included, with FT7 and FT8 reported analytically. Table VII summarizes both. 

C. Results 

FT1 (disk) vs FT2 (in-memory) on A2 + F1. Both mechanisms checkpoint every 100 steps over a 1500-step run and persist the same 204.7 MB state, model parameters plus SGD momentum buffers post-serialization. BeeGFS scratch delivers ∼1.37 GB/s write and ∼1.32 GB/s read, comparable to the gloo-over-IB ring bandwidth FT2 uses, so the comparison is not confounded by raw transport speed. The two sit on a genuine Pareto trade-off, not a dominance relation. FT1 achieves ∼8× lower SS overhead ( 0.48% vs 

3.78% ) despite only ∼1.5 × lower per-save cost ( 283 ms vs 

423 ms), because their foreground coupling differs: FT1 writes only from rank 0 with no barrier, so the other seven ranks overlap the write with compute, whereas FT2’s coordinated ring send/recv puts all eight ranks on the critical path. FT2 achieves ∼9× lower Trestore (∼17 ms vs ∼155 ms), restoring from a pinned host buffer in a single PCIe transfer rather than re-reading 204.7 MB from BeeGFS. The 9× is a ratio of analytical bounds; M1’s cold-cache measured values ( 20 .5

vs 340 .2 ms, Table III) widen it to ∼17 ×, so the ordering is robust. Pareto-incomparability does not by itself settle which to deploy. Both checkpoint at the same interval and so lose the same work on a fault, leaving a 3.3-point overhead gap against a 138 ms restore gap; scalarizing by expected completion time therefore favors FT1 unless faults arrive every few seconds. We make that threshold explicit in the decision framework. 

FT4 emergency save on A2. FT4 inherits Gemini’s [10] peer-pair in-memory storage but with Bamboo’s [14] reactive trigger, conceptually closest to the JIT-checkpoint mechanism of Gupta et al. [13]: it performs a single save inside the lead-time window opened by a predictive fault signal. We use a 2.0 s lead time. Across three seeds the emergency save completes in 884 .7 ± 89 .8 ms, well inside the window; the dominant cost is pinned-buffer allocation, which FT2 amortizes at attach time and FT4 must pay on the critical path. Restore reuses FT2’s peer-pinned-to-GPU path, so FT4 inherits FT2’s ∼17 ms 

Trestore .

Multi-mechanism Pareto cross-section on A2. Table VII places FT4 alongside FT1, FT2, and the FT9 no-FT baseline on the A2 + F1 axis, and adds FT3, FT5–FT8 for a complete cross-section. The per-save column is the warm amortized cost for periodic mechanisms (FT1–FT3, FT5, FT6) and the one-shot pre-fault cost for FT4. FT4 performs no periodic per-step checkpoint work, so its SS entry is a design value rather than a measured throughput difference. The effective Trestore column adds the save-at-failure cost to the restore cost for FT4 because the emergency save is on the critical path, whereas FT1–FT3 and FT5–FT6 amortize theirs off it. The lost work column is the expected re-execution under uniform fault arrival: half the checkpoint interval for periodic mechanisms and ∼0 for FT4, undefined for FT7/FT8, which keep no checkpoint and reconfigure rather than restore, and unbounded for FT9; every reported cell fired its fault after the first completed save. The FT7/FT8 Trestore entries are analytical rendezvous / reinit bounds; FT8’s applies a fixed 700 ms rendezvous + NCCL communicator-setup budget identically to every architecture. We construct the Pareto frontier over FT1–FT6, whose reported values represent comparable state-preservation paths. FT7 and FT8 appear in Table VII for completeness but are excluded from the frontier: recovery requires external recon-figuration the harness does not execute, so their latencies are analytical lower bounds rather than comparable measurements. On that basis the A2 frontier carries three non-dominated points: FT1 disk (low SS, mid Trestore ), FT2 in-mem (mid SS, low Trestore ), and FT4 JIT (zero periodic SS work, high 

Trestore ). FT4 does not dominate FT2 because its Trestore is about 50 × higher, and FT2 does not dominate FT4 because FT2 incurs 3.78% steady-state overhead and FT4 incurs no periodic checkpoint work. The lost-work column adds a third consideration the two axes do not capture: at the 100-step interval and A2’s ∼112 ms/step (Table V), a periodic mechanism re-executes ∼50 steps ≈ 5.6 s per fault under uniform arrival [22], [23], whereas FT4 re-executes almost nothing when the warning fires early enough. D. From Restore Time to End-to-End Recovery 

The times above exclude process-group re-formation. End-to-end recovery is Trecover = Trestore +TPG +Tinit , and TPG is not measurable on the halting architectures. We therefore examine 

TPG ∈ [2 , 10] s as a sensitivity range. This scale is consistent with rendezvous and restart costs: the FT8 estimate in Ta-ble VII assigns ∼700 ms to the rendezvous and NCCL setup alone, while Bamboo [14] reports multi-second teardown and restart. Section VIII provides direct multi-fault reconvergence measurements at the same order of magnitude. Holding the mechanism-specific Tinit fixed or negligible, adding the common TPG term, identical for FT1, FT2 and FT4 since the process group is re-formed once per fault regardless of which mechanism populated the survivors, preserves the ordering FT2 ≺ FT1 ≺ FT4 and the absolute gaps (138 ms and 

∼747 ms), but compresses the ratios: the FT1/FT2 recovery ratio collapses from ∼9× to 1.01 –1.07 × as TPG dominates. Once TPG dominates, the recovery axis becomes less discrim-inating between mechanisms and steady-state cost becomes decisive: FT4 has no periodic checkpoint work, while FT1 and FT2 incur 0.48% and 3.78% measured SS overhead. On small-state workloads, signal coverage and storage tier decide the choice rather than the shape of the frontier: FT2 trades 3.69 –3.91% SS overhead for ∼17 ms restore on A1–A3, while FT4 removes periodic checkpoint overhead at ∼0.9–1.5 s effective restore. Internal validity. Injector timing skew is below 20 ms, and we discard the first 50 steps of each run. Sub-20 ms analytical restore bounds are not treated as measured end-to-end recovery. The A1-sync/A1-async comparison changes both backend and synchronization policy, and A1-async uses responder threshold one, so the server proceeds on a single reply and its graceful degradation under one fault is close to guaranteed by construction; its result therefore characterizes this configuration rather than asynchronous parameter servers generally. On NCCL-primary architectures, Trestore is an ana-lytical lower bound; Table III shows measured costs 20–120% higher. Fault victims are fixed by trace, limiting generalization of position-dependent effects. 

Construct and recovery validity. SS overhead measures amortized FT cost relative to the failure-free baseline. FT4’s zero SS entry is structural, not a throughput estimate: it does no periodic checkpoint work, and its emergency save is charged to effective Trestore . FT7/FT8 report only pre-fault SS overhead, having no state-preservation path. On the halting architectures (A1-sync, A2–A5, A7), we establish recover-ability but not completed end-to-end recovery because the runner does not perform survivor election and process-group reconstruction. A6 reconfigures natively, while A1-async is native graceful continuation rather than FT recovery. 

Coverage and implementation fidelity. Exploratory F2/F3 cells use n ≤ 2, while cells used by the decision framework are boosted to n = 5 ; multi-fault conclusions are therefore limited to the tested configurations. FT4–FT6 are simplified produc-tion proxies, and A5 omits Megatron’s full autograd wrappers. They preserve the state-transfer and failure structures targeted by our metrics but are not end-to-end convergence evaluations. For small-n FT experiments, we report mean ± half-range. 

External validity. Measurements use 8 V100 GPUs across two nodes with HDR InfiniBand and BeeGFS scratch (∼1.37 GB/s write, ∼1.32 GB/s read), whereas the cited pro-duction runs operate at much larger scale, so absolute over-heads and crossover points may change with filesystem, in-terconnect, and world size. We use ResNet-50 and Megatron-style feed-forward stacks rather than a full Transformer; the workloads preserve key determinants of our metrics, including per-rank state size and communication structure, but activa-tion checkpointing, larger Adam-style optimizer state, and sequence-parallel communication remain outside our mea-surements. Structural results are less timing-dependent, but crossover points should be re-derived on the target system. 

A6 learning efficiency. A6’s post-fault throughput increase does not imply improved optimization. Truncating both groups to a 1123 s window from the fault instant, neither loss reduc-tion nor time to target differs detectably against the matched no-fault baseline ( p = 0 .67 and p = 0 .83 ; n = 7 fault, n = 3 

baseline), despite roughly 18% higher sample throughput. Validation on a real training workload remains open. 

Fault-model scope. F1–F3 inject fail-stop worker crashes and never fail the A1 parameter-server coordinator. Coordina-13 

tor failure, stragglers, and network partitions are outside scope; they interact differently with architectures and FT mechanisms and require dedicated fault traces. XI. C ONCLUSION 

We presented F AIL BENCH , a unified evaluation of crash fault tolerance across seven distributed-training architectures, eight FT mechanisms, and a no-FT baseline, covering 142 (architecture, mechanism, trace) tuples. Three findings stand out. First, six configurations halt after a single worker failure, whereas A1-async and A6 gossip degrade gracefully; A6’s post-fault sample throughput rises 17.9% without a detectable improvement in loss progress. Second, disk, in-memory, and JIT checkpointing form a Pareto frontier on A2, but their operating points shift strongly with architecture and state size. Third, compatibility itself limits mechanism transfer: some architecture–mechanism pairs are structurally infeasible rather than merely unevaluated. We translate these results into a measurement-grounded decision framework that selects among characterized candi-dates using failure rate, state size, checkpoint interval, signal availability, and service-level constraints.
