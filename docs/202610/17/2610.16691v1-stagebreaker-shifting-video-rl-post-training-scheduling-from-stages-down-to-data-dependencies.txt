## Abstract 

Reinforcement learning (RL) post-training has emerged as the key paradigm for improving autoregressive video models. Emerging vision-language reward models further strengthen post-training with accurate rewards, but their long chain-of-thought inference substantially raises the associated costs, while the pursuit of long videos prolongs policy training. De-spite these rising costs, existing video RL systems still adopt coarse-grained stage-centric execution, which stalls policy training until all reward evaluation finishes, replays every trajectory twice due to a further barrier within policy train-ing, and induces load imbalance across GPUs. To address the challenges, we propose StageBreaker, a fine-grained data-dependency-driven scheduling framework, which schedules the true dataflow of an RL step rather than its stages. Specif-ically, StageBreaker overlaps policy training with reward evaluation by exploiting reward-separable policy training, and merges the two trajectory replays into one by leverag-ing loss-independent back-propagation. It further balances GPU load by scheduling computation at the finer granularity. Our evaluation results show that StageBreaker achieves a speedup of up to 1.41 × over stage-centric execution. 

## 1 Introduction 

Autoregressive video models (AR models), which generate long videos chunk by chunk (i.e., multiple video frames), now power long-form and streaming video applications [6, 7, 9, 18, 25, 40, 44, 55, 59, 62]. As these models enter real-world deployment, generation quality becomes the decisive require-ment, in particular alignment with human preference, aes-thetic quality, and temporal coherence [12, 13, 26, 36, 46, 67]. By delivering substantial gains across all these dimensions, reinforcement learning (RL) post-training has emerged as the key paradigm to meet this requirement [23, 58, 66]. RL realizes these gains through a training loop of repeated steps, each of which comprises rollout, reward evaluation, and policy training (cf. Figure 1a) [28, 38, 72]. In the rollout stage, the video model being trained ( the policy ) generates multiple candidate videos for each prompt in a batch [16, 38]. A reward model then scores each candidate on desired dimensions [14, 24, 47, 53], and policy training replays each candidate’s trajectory (i.e., its sequence of generation steps) and combines the result with reward scores to compute the loss [49, 58]. The continued pursuit of high-quality and long videos [13, 23, 26, 58, 67] raises the cost of each RL step. Unlike earlier lightweight reward metrics [53, 54, 56], which can-not comprehensively and reliably assess video qualities, re-cent reward models employ large vision-language models (VLMs) to examine multiple video frames and perform long chain-of-thought (CoT) reasoning to produce holistic judg-ments for video quality [14, 24, 45, 47, 52]. Longer videos, in turn, prolong policy training, whose trajectory replay re-runs the video model for each video generation step [23, 58]. Together, reward evaluation and policy training now account for 63 ∼75% of an RL step. These rising costs unfortunately expose a mismatch with current video RL systems. As illustrated in Figure 1a, these systems [16, 28, 38, 42, 43] organize each RL step into suc-cessive rollout, reward, and policy training stages (i.e., stage-centric execution ). Each stage serves as the unit of com-putation scheduling and GPU assignment, processing the entire batch on a fixed set of GPUs before the next stage starts [16, 38, 73]. However, these stage boundaries impose coarser constraints than the true data dependencies within an RL step, prolonging each step and underutilizing GPUs. For example, while the reward model can score a candidate upon its generation, stage-centric execution starts reward evaluation only after the entire rollout stage finishes, expos-ing its cost entirely in the latency of each RL step. To take this cost off the critical path, as shown in Figure 1b, recent works [11, 41, 50] deploy reward models on dedicated GPUs (reward GPUs) and score each candidate upon generation,

leaving the remaining GPUs (actor GPUs) to run rollout and policy training. In practice, this mechanism is only a pre-liminary exploration that falls short of the expected gains and even slows RL post-training by 20 ∼29%, as three key challenges remain unaddressed. 

(1) Reward-blocked policy training. Existing RL systems stall policy training until the reward stage scores the entire batch, as policy training needs the complete set of scores to derive the advantages (the relative quality of each candidate) for the subsequent loss computation [23, 38, 65, 71, 72]. Even under asynchronous reward evaluation, the actor GPUs stay idle for 13% of each RL step before starting policy training, waiting for the last reward score of the batch ( 1 in Figure 1b). (2) Redundant trajectory replay. As in conventional model training, existing RL systems first compute the loss over the entire batch, then back-propagate it to derive the policy gra-dient , and finally update the policy. Policy training therefore runs a forward pass for the loss, in which it replays all tra-jectories to derive their probabilities [23, 66]. The backward pass, which back-propagates this loss, starts only after the forward pass finishes, imposing a further batch-level barrier within policy training. Although the backward pass requires these probabilities as well, GPU memory cannot retain the activations of all trajectories until then. The backward pass thus repeats the costly replay ( 2 ), resulting in redundant replay for 19 ∼26% of each RL step. (3) Stage-induced load im-balance. Existing RL systems bind computation to GPUs at the coarse granularity of stages, making it difficult to balance the load evenly across GPUs ( 3 ). For example, asynchro-nous reward evaluation, following this binding, dedicates GPUs to the reward stage but overlooks their potential to execute other computation [38, 41, 42]. The reward GPUs thus sit idle for 64% of each RL step once scoring finishes, while the actor GPUs carry all rollout and policy training. As these limitations all stem from the coarse granularity of stages, our key insight is to schedule the true dataflow, which uncovers potential to shorten each RL step and fill idle GPUs. (1) Reward-separable policy training. Policy training can in fact proceed asynchronously with reward evaluation, as the loss it computes is simply the product of the advantage and a reward-independent part. Policy training can thus com-pute and back-propagate the reward-independent part while reward evaluation is still in progress, and combine it with the advantages after both are complete. (2) Loss-independent back-propagation. Within policy training, back-propagation can likewise proceed without waiting for the loss, as the policy gradient is simply the product of a loss-dependent scalar and the gradient of the trajectory’s probability. Back-propagation can therefore replay each trajectory to compute this probability gradient in advance and apply the scalar afterwards. As this replay also yields the trajectory’s proba-bility required by the loss, the loss computation can reuse it rather than replaying the trajectory again, merging the two trajectory replays into one. (3) Task-level load balancing. Astage can further decompose into independent computation tasks, each inherently binding to its own input data rather than to the stage, which allows decoupling their GPU place-ment from the stage. VAE decoding [17, 64] within rollout, for example, which converts generated latents into video frames, operates solely on its input latents, while the loss computation and back-propagation require only the trajec-tory itself. Once its inputs are ready, a task can thus run on any available GPU rather than the fixed GPUs of its stage, which makes load balancing possible at the finer granularity (i.e., tasks). Inspired by our insights, we propose StageBreaker , a fine-grained data-dependency-driven scheduling framework for AR video RL post-training, which schedules the true dataflow of an RL step rather than its coarse stages (Figure 1c). Specif-ically, building on asynchronous reward evaluation, we pro-pose reward-asynchronous policy training , which computes and back-propagates the reward-independent part of the loss without waiting for the advantages. RL algorithms, how-ever, clip the loss for training stability [37], which confines the reward-independent part to two possible forms depend-ing on the sign of the advantage. StageBreaker therefore retains both forms during back-propagation, which occupy negligible GPU memory under LoRA. Once the advantage ar-rives, StageBreaker applies it to the matching form, yielding a policy gradient identical to that of stage-centric execu-tion. StageBreaker thus overlaps policy training on the actor GPUs with reward evaluation on the reward GPUs, elimi-nating the idle wait of the actor GPUs. Moreover, within policy training, we design merged trajectory replay , which further breaks the barrier between the loss computation and the back-propagation and thus replays each trajectory only once. By exploiting the aggregation granularity of the tra-jectory’s probability (e.g., chunk), StageBreaker feeds the probability of each chunk into the loss computation upon replaying it and then back-propagates this probability for its gradient before replaying the next chunk. After replay-ing the entire trajectory, StageBreaker applies the derived loss-dependent scalar to the probability gradient, completing both the loss computation and the back-propagation within a single replay. Rather than binding computation to GPUs by stage, our task-level placement balances the load across the two GPU groups at the granularity of tasks. Through a profiling-based search for the optimal placement, Stage-Breaker strategically moves VAE decoding and part of the policy training tasks from the overloaded actor GPUs to the underutilized reward GPUs. Our evaluation results show that StageBreaker achieves a speedup of up to 1.41 × over stage-centric execution and 1.81 × over asynchronous reward evaluation. • We characterize the challenges of video RL post-training under emerging VLM-based reward models and long videos.

2.1 Autoregressive Video Generation Model architecture. Video models generate a video by iterative denoising in a latent space [40, 44, 55, 59]. As Fig-ure 2a shows, a text encoder embeds the prompt, a diffusion transformer (DiT) model estimates the clean latent from an initial noisy latent under this embedding, and a varia-tional autoencoder (VAE) decodes the clean latent into video frames [17, 32]. At the core of the video model, the DiT stacks multiple transformer blocks, each of which applies at-tention over the latent and the prompt embedding, followed by a feed-forward network (FFN). With these blocks, the DiT denoises the latent from initial noise 𝑥 𝐾 to the clean latent 𝑥 0 in 𝐾 steps, at each of which the DiT removes a portion of the noise from 𝑥 𝑘 to obtain 𝑥 𝑘 −1. Each denoising step is compute-intensive with little throughput gain from batching, as the attention and FFN of every block run over a long sequence of spatiotemporal latent tokens. Video models therefore typically generate one video at a time, each taking a nearly constant time given the fixed number of denoising steps.

2.2 RL Post-Training for Video Models 

Reinforcement learning (RL) post-training optimizes a video model for qualities such as human preference, motion quality, and prompt adherence [12, 13, 21, 26, 36, 46]. RL pursues this goal over repeated steps, each of which consists of three stages. Within a step, the current video model (i.e., policy) generates candidate videos for a batch of prompts in rollout, and the reward stage scores the qualities of candidates [23, 49, 58, 66, 67]. Policy training then updates the policy to favor candidates of higher quality. Existing video RL systems typically execute the three stages sequentially (i.e., stage-centric execution), each over the whole batch [21, 38, 42, 57]. Reward-blocked policy training. Although asynchronous reward evaluation removes the stage boundary of stage-centric execution between rollout and the reward stage, it still keeps the boundary between the reward stage and policy training, making policy training wait for the reward stage to finish scoring the entire batch. This boundary remains since policy training relies on the advantages derived from all reward scores of the entire batch (Equation 2). Under light-weight reward models, this boundary costs little, as scoring the batch finishes nearly with rollout. The current long VLM inference, however, prolongs reward evaluation and keeps a substantial part of the reward cost on the critical path ahead of policy training. The actor GPUs thus finish rollout and sit idle until the last score of the batch arrives, which takes 13% of each RL step in our measurement and leaves 36% of the reward cost on the critical path (§5.5).

3.3 Key Insights 

The three challenges all stem from stage-centric execution, whose boundaries and GPU binding impose constraints coarser than the true data dependencies within an RL step. In prac-tice, while most of the computation after a boundary can proceed without the awaited result, the boundary still stalls all of it. As Figure 5 shows, in policy training, for example, the boundaries stall the entire policy gradient computation due to its dependence on the advantage and the importance ratio. This dependence, however, involves only the final mul-tiplication, which reveals the separability of the two from the rest of the computation. Similarly, each computation task depends only on its inputs, which suggests a placement be-yond the GPUs of its stage. Our key insight is therefore to

schedule the true dataflow, not the stages, shortening the critical path and filling the idle GPUs. 

Reward-separable policy training. As Equation 2 and Figure 5 show, the surrogate loss comprises two factors, of which only the advantage 𝐴 𝑖 depends on reward scores. The other factor, the importance ratio 𝜌 𝑖,𝑘 , relies solely on the tra-jectory and the policy. Given their distinct dependencies, the loss computation is separable into these two factors. Policy training can therefore compute and back-propagate the im-portance ratio in advance, and apply the advantage through a single scalar multiplication once it arrives. It thereby over-laps with reward evaluation, which removes the idle wait of the actor GPUs and takes the reward cost entirely off the critical path. 

Loss-independent back-propagation. Equation 5 and Fig-ure 5 illustrate that the policy gradient computation is like-wise separable, as the loss computation contributes only 𝜌 𝑖,𝑘 ,a scalar multiplier of the gradient ∇𝜃 log 𝜋 𝜃 (𝜏 𝑖,𝑘 ). This gra-dient itself depends on nothing but the trajectory and the policy, and back-propagation can thus compute it in advance and apply 𝜌 𝑖,𝑘 afterwards, rather than waiting for the loss. This gradient requires replaying each trajectory, which also yields the probabilities log 𝜋 𝜃 (𝜏 𝑖,𝑘 ) for loss. The loss com-putation can therefore reuse these probabilities rather than recompute them, merging the two trajectory replays. 

Task-level load balancing. A stage further decomposes into independent computation tasks, each depending only on its own inputs rather than on the entire stage, which makes the stage-level GPU binding coarser than necessary. As Figure 5 shows, VAE decoding, for example, depends only on the clean latents of a candidate, but the rollout stage binds it to the actor GPUs while the underutilized reward GPUs could run it instead. As VAE decoding takes about 30% of the rollout time (§5.5), moving it alone would shift a substantial load from the actor GPUs to the reward GPUs. Moreover, reward scoring starts once these VAE decodings finish and thus runs on a large batch, raising the throughput of the memory-bound CoT decoding without delaying the comple-tion of reward evaluation. Similarly, the loss computation and back-propagation of a trajectory depend only on the tra-jectory itself and the policy, but still stay on the overloaded actor GPUs. Such tasks can thus strategically move from the overloaded actor GPUs to the idle reward GPUs, mitigating GPU underutilization.

4.4 Task-Level Load Balancing 

StageBreaker refines the current GPU binding from stage granularity to task granularity, placing each task according to the load of the two GPU groups rather than its stage. Fol-lowing this principle, StageBreaker moves two kinds of tasks from the overloaded actor GPUs to the underutilized reward GPUs, VAE decoding in rollout and the loss computation and back-propagation in policy training (policy training tasks). As VAE decoding depends only on the clean latents of a candidate, StageBreaker transfers these small latents to the reward GPUs and decodes them there before scoring. The reward GPUs then score the decoded videos as one batch rather than individually upon arrival, which accelerates the memory-bound CoT decoding and thus does not delay re-ward evaluation. Similarly, the policy training tasks of a trajectory (§4.3) depend only on the trajectory itself and the current policy. StageBreaker therefore assigns policy train-ing tasks to the reward GPUs once they finish scoring, and transfers the resulting 𝑔 ± 

> 𝑖

back to the actor GPUs. Moreover, the loss computation of some RL algorithms involves addi-tional forward passes under frozen policies (e.g., the replay under the reference policy for the KL term [23, 37, 66]), which StageBreaker likewise moves to the reward GPUs. 

Scheduling. To minimize the RL step latency, StageBreaker must determine the split of VAE decodings and policy train-ing tasks between the two GPU groups. To this end, Stage-Breaker builds a profiling-based simulator to search for the optimal placement. StageBreaker profiles the execution time of every computation task on the target setup, and the simu-lator executes the dataflow of an RL step under a given split to estimate the end-to-end step latency. Owing to the small search space and the fast simulation [69, 72], StageBreaker enumerates all possible placements and adopts the one with the best performance. Our controller then dispatches tasks by the derived placement at runtime. 

4.5 Implementation 

We implement StageBreaker on top of verl-omni [42], a widely used RL post-training framework. We reuse the roll-out, reward, and training engines of verl-omni for all com-putation, while implementing our controller above these engines to schedule computation tasks. For task-level load balancing (§4.4), we further equip the reward GPUs with a VAE decoder and a lightweight replica of the training engine, 

> 8StageBreaker

holding only the DiT and a LoRA copy and receiving the updated LoRA weights after each optimizer step. Moreover, StageBreaker integrates two RL algorithms, DanceGRPO and KVPO, without modifying either of them. StageBreaker also readily extends to other AR video RL algorithms, as it reschedules only the execution of the RL step rather than the algorithm itself.

5.4 Training Fidelity 

StageBreaker reschedules only the execution of an RL step and keeps the gradient of every weight update mathemati-cally identical to that of stage-centric execution. This identity holds in exact arithmetic, while floating-point accumulation on GPUs introduces small numerical differences [39, 74]. To verify that these differences do not alter training, we adopt the official recipe of DanceGRPO [57, 58], which trains Sta-ble Diffusion 1.4 [5] with the HPS-v2.1 [56]. We run this RL post-training for 500 RL steps under Colocated and 

StageBreaker , and Figure 15 plots the training reward, the mean reward score of the candidates generated at each step (light lines), with its 10-step moving average (dark lines). The two curves overlap throughout RL training without any sys-tematic gap, both rising from about 0.24 to 0.26 over the 500 steps with only negligible differences at any step. These dif-ferences stem from floating-point rounding on GPUs, which makes the same computation yield slightly different values when its operations run in a different order. StageBreaker 

reorders these operations by applying the advantage and the importance ratio after back-propagation, but the resulting differences do not compromise the algorithmic fidelity of RL training.

## 6 Related Works and Discussions 

Video generation. Numerous efforts have built DiT-based video generation models [6, 7, 9, 18, 25, 40, 44, 55, 59, 62]. Bidirectional models such as HunyuanVideo [55], Wan [44], CogVideoX [61], and Open-Sora [68] attend over all video frames at once, which achieves high visual quality but con-fines generation to short clips of fixed length and precludes streaming. To overcome these limitations, recent works turn to AR models [2, 6, 40], which instead generate videos chunk by chunk under causal attention, supporting long-form and streaming generation. To further reduce the generation cost, CausVid [62], Self-Forcing [18], and their successors [7, 9, 25, 59] distill few-step AR generators from bidirectional models, cutting the denoising steps of each chunk from tens to a few (e.g., from 50 to 4) and thus achieving real-time long video generation. StageBreaker readily accommodates all these AR models, as it reschedules only the execution of the RL step and modifies neither their architectures nor generation procedures. 

RL for video generation. Numerous works [13, 23, 26, 46, 58, 66, 67] post-train video models with RL to improve human preference alignment, motion quality, and temporal coher-ence. DanceGRPO [58] and Flow-GRPO [23] extend GRPO to video diffusion and flow-matching models through SDE sampling. Building on this general formulation, KVPO [66], CM-GRPO [26], and other recent works [13, 46, 67] further customize exploration and the surrogate policy for the few-step AR models. To provide these algorithms with more accurate rewards, numerous works propose VLM-based re-ward models [14, 24, 45, 47, 52–54], such as VideoAlign [24], UnifiedReward-Flex [53], and VR-Thinker [47], which re-place lightweight metrics with multi-dimensional judgments through CoT reasoning. These works improve RL post-training and are orthogonal to StageBreaker, which accelerates its execution without modifying the algorithms or reward mod-els. 

RL post-training systems. A growing number of systems accelerate RL post-training of LLMs by orchestrating its stages across GPUs [10, 11, 16, 38, 70–72]. Frameworks such as HybridFlow [38] and OpenRLHF [16] execute each RL step stage by stage over the entire batch, while RLHFuse [71] and RLinf [63] decompose the stages into sample-level subtasks and micro-batches and recompose them across GPUs to fill the bubbles between stages. To further shorten the critical path, StreamRL [70] and AReaL [10] turn to off-policy train-ing, which overlaps rollout with policy training on stale rollouts at the cost of the on-policy semantics [11, 34]. Roll-Packer [11] and DistRS [72] keep these semantics and overlap only reward evaluation with rollout. All of them, however, back-propagate a sample only after its advantage arrives. StageBreaker instead back-propagates ahead of the reward scores and applies the advantage afterwards, which overlaps policy training with reward evaluation while preserving on-policy semantics exactly. Moreover, these systems optimize LLM rollout, whose memory-bound decoding and long-tail output lengths motivate batching and tail-aware schedul-ing [10, 11, 34, 70]. Video rollout, in contrast, gains little from batching and runs a fixed number of denoising steps for every video, which raises neither issue. 

RL systems for diffusion and video. Systems for diffusion RL mostly accelerate rollout, for example by offloading seed exploration to spot GPUs [21] or sharing generation prefixes across candidates [22]. DigenRL [48] further overlaps rollout with training and lets the idle trainer assist generation, but still back-propagates after the scores arrive. LeanGRPO [49], a concurrent work, removes the redundant trajectory replay by back-propagating during rollout itself. It applies, however, only when rollout and training run on the same engine under the same policy, a narrow setting for current RL systems. In contrast, StageBreaker merges the two replays into one only on the training engine under the current policy, which enables StageBreaker to accommodate separate rollout and 

> 12 StageBreaker

training engines as well as every policy update. More impor-tantly, StageBreaker observes the rising cost of VLM-based reward models, which brings new challenges to stage-centric execution overlooked by these systems built around light-weight rewards. StageBreaker breaks stage-centric execution into a dataflow of computation tasks and schedules them by their true data dependencies to address these challenges. 

Dependency-driven scheduling. A wide range of ML sys-tems schedule computation by its data dependencies rather than by program boundaries [1, 4, 19, 29, 30, 33]. Ray [29] runs a dynamic task graph in which each task starts once its inputs arrive, and zero-bubble pipeline parallelism [33] further splits the backward pass into input-gradient and weight-gradient computations to fill the remaining bubbles. StageBreaker applies the same principle to video RL post-training, scheduling the true dataflow of an RL step, in which the loss and its gradient separate into independent factors and each stage decomposes into individually placeable tasks. 

## 7 Conclusion 

This paper presents StageBreaker, a data-dependency-driven scheduling framework for AR video RL post-training in resource-constrained settings, where a small and fixed GPU budget hosts rollout, VLM-based reward evaluation, and policy training. StageBreaker overlaps policy training with reward evaluation, merges the two redundant trajectory re-plays, and balances the load across GPUs through task-level placement. Evaluation results show that it achieves consis-tent speedups over stage-centric execution and asynchro-nous reward evaluation. In the future, we plan to explore further RL optimizations in resource-constrained settings, including placement across heterogeneous hardware devices and scheduling based on their performance characteristics.
