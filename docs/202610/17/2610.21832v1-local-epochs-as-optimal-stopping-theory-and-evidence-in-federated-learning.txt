## Abstract 

How long should each client train before communicating? Federated learning usually answers with a fixed, hand-tuned number of local epochs, yet an extra epoch helps only while the loss it removes outweighs the client drift it adds. We cast local training as finite-horizon optimal stopping: a client trains while the next epoch’s expected loss reduction exceeds its priced squared drift. We prove that this one-stage look-ahead threshold is optimal whenever stopping decisions are nested, derive closed forms for geometric and power-law returns, and show that an estimated rule loses utility only through wrong-signed decisions. Epoch-level evidence supports the model’s structure (geometric diminishing returns and additive drift) and reveals a simple regularity: the stage of training, not the client, sets the budget. The resulting rule, FedStop , is never more than one point below tuned FedAvg in mean accuracy. Where its budgets vary, it raises worst-decile accuracy by 4–8 points over tuned FedAvg and an adaptive-interval baseline, and every one of these gains remains significant after a Holm correction. Against the client-side stopping rules ALT and FLASH, it has the highest CIFAR-10 worst decile on every seed and is level elsewhere. It stays stable where per-client early stopping collapses, and each stopping decision takes constant time. 

## 1 Introduction 

In federated learning (FL) with federated averaging (FedAvg), each round the server broadcasts the global model, a sample of clients train it locally for E epochs, and the server averages the returned models (McMa-han et al., 2017). The local epoch count E governs a basic trade-off. More local work reduces how often clients must communicate. Under statistical heterogeneity, however, it also lets each local model drift toward its own optimum and away from the global one, which degrades the aggregate (Li et al., 2020a; Karimireddy et al., 2020). In practice E is a tuned constant, and a fragile one. In a grid that also varies the learning rate, the best fixed configuration is two epochs on CIFAR-10 but one on CIFAR-100, where two epochs diverge (Section 8). More fundamentally, a tuned constant says nothing about why a budget works, how it should change as training proceeds, or whether it should differ across clients. We take a modelling view. Every local epoch is an investment: after it, a client observes what it bought, a reduction in its local loss, and what it paid, a further displacement from the round’s starting model. Returns diminish as the local loss flattens while the displacement keeps accruing, so deciding when to stop can be cast as a finite-horizon optimal stopping problem. A model of this kind is worth having only to the extent that it describes real local training, so we derive what it predicts and test those predictions against epoch-level logs. To our knowledge, this is the first formulation of a client’s choice of local epochs as an optimal stopping 1arXiv:2610.21832v1 [cs.LG] 15 Oct 2026 problem. The resulting rule, FedStop (Fed erated optimal Stop ping), runs locally on each client and ends its training as soon as another epoch is no longer worth its drift.

1. Model. Local-epoch selection as drift-priced optimal stopping, with a utility motivated by a one-round aggregation bound. 2. Theory. We derive five observable consequences of the model, such as diminishing returns per epoch and drift that adds up across epochs, and check each against epoch-level training logs. We compare FedStop with the two client-side stopping rules closest to it, ALT (Shenaj et al., 2025) and FLASH (Panchal et al., 2023), and with FedAvg (McMahan et al., 2017), FedNova (Wang et al., 2020), the adaptive interval of Wang et al. (2019), FedProx (Li et al., 2020a) and per-client early stopping. Random-swap and equal-split controls show where its gains come from. 

FedStop costs at most one point of mean accuracy against tuned FedAvg. Under strong label skew, it lifts the worst decile of clients by up to eight points over tuned FedAvg, a tail that fixed budgets match only with at least twice as many local epochs, if at all. Among the client-side rules, it has the highest CIFAR-10 worst decile, ahead of ALT and FLASH on every seed, and it is level with them on the naturally partitioned data. It remains stable where per-client early stopping fails. The decision itself is lightweight: constant time per epoch and a few hundred scalars of memory per client, independent of model size. 

## 2 Related Work 

Client drift. FedProx adds a proximal penalty to each local objective (Li et al., 2020a), SCAFFOLD corrects local directions with control variates (Karimireddy et al., 2020), and FedNova normalises updates by the number of local steps to remove objective inconsistency (Wang et al., 2020). These methods change the local objective or the aggregation rule and are complementary to choosing the training duration. FedStop 

uses FedNova-style normalisation directly. 

Local computation. The effect of the number of local steps on convergence is well studied for local SGD (Stich, 2019; Khaled et al., 2020; Woodworth et al., 2020), including step counts that vary across rounds (Qin et al., 2023) and the dependence of the surrogate objective on local work (Charles & Konečný, 2021; Patel et al., 2024). Wang et al. adapt a shared aggregation interval under a resource budget (Wang et al., 2019). These analyses bound or tune one budget per round. We instead model the budget as a client-side stopping decision and test the model’s assumptions directly. 

Client-side and global stopping. Two methods also set each client’s number of epochs, by different signals and for different ends. ALT stops a client once its representations diverge from the global model’s (Shenaj et al., 2025). FLASH addresses concept drift: it pairs a drift-aware server optimiser with a client rule that stops once validation loss stops falling (Panchal et al., 2023). Neither models the trade-off it decides. 

FedStop derives its threshold from an explicit stopping model with a priced drift cost, and Section 8.4 compares the three rules on CIFAR-10, FEMNIST and Shakespeare. At the server, stopping decisions take two forms: when to close a round given the client updates received so far, posed as optimal stopping (Jiang & Ying, 2020), and when to terminate training altogether (Mahmoudi et al., 2024; Niu et al., 2024). FedStop 

moves the stopping decision to the client, where it sets how long each client trains within a round. We report worst-client accuracy as an outcome, but unlike fairness-oriented methods (Li et al., 2020b; Mohri et al., 2019) we do not optimise for it. 23 Preliminaries

5.5 From theory to a deployable rule 

In deployment, the profile parameters are estimated online. Each participating client evaluates its full training loss at initialisation and after every executed epoch. These monitoring passes are counted as computation (Appendix). A single round rarely yields enough epochs to estimate a decay rate. Each client therefore estimates its profile shape (ˆ ρi or ˆ ai) from the per-epoch loss decreases of its current round and its last w participations, the rounds in which it was sampled. The loss scale ˆ¯ℓi changes as the global model improves, so it is fitted within the round. The shape is assumed stable over nearby participations. Because clients return different numbers of steps Ki = τiSi, the server aggregates with step normalisation (1). Algorithm 1 summarises the method. Once these estimates are formed, each stopping decision takes O(1) time. Refitting the shape scores G candidate values over its search range ( G = 200 here) and refines the best by golden-section search, at a cost of O(G(w + 1) Emax ) scalar operations. Each client stores O(( w + 1) Emax ) scalars. All of these costs are independent of model size. The dominant cost is evaluating the training loss, one forward pass over the local data, or a fixed subset of it, per epoch (Section 8.3). Figure 1 shows the rule at work on two client-rounds from the CIFAR-10 logs. Early in training, the predicted gain of the next epoch starts near 0 .5, falls geometrically, and drops below the priced drift after six epochs. Late in training, it starts more than an order of magnitude lower and drops below the priced drift after two. In both cases the client stops at the crossing, as Theorem 1 prescribes. The third panel gives the closed forms of Corollaries 1 and 2. Budgets shrink as the exchange ratio κ grows, logarithmically in 1 /κ for the geometric profile and polynomially for the power law.

Its proximal weight is selected from five values. On the same three settings we also run the client-side stopping rules ALT (Shenaj et al., 2025) and FLASH (Panchal et al., 2023), described in Section 8.4. 

FedStop ’s budgets vary both across rounds and across clients, which raises a question: which of the two drives its gains? A random-swap control answers it. It reruns training with the same clients in each round, but hands that round’s FedStop budgets to its clients in random order. For example, if FedStop gives clients A, B and C four, one and two epochs, the swap might give them one, two and four. Each round keeps the same budgets and the same total, but no client keeps its own. If matching budgets to clients matters, the swap should do worse than FedStop . If only the amount of training per round matters, the two should perform alike. An equal-split control goes further. It keeps each round’s total but gives every client in the round the same share, which removes the budgets’ heterogeneity altogether. Both controls replay the budgets of a FedStop run, so they test its schedule retrospectively rather than act online. Clients train without loss monitoring, and the server aggregates with (1). Because clients differ in size, both controls execute slightly more SGD steps than FedStop , about 5% more on CIFAR-10 and under 2% more on FEMNIST. We run the swap for the power-law profile in every setting and for the geometric profile wherever its budgets vary (CIFAR-10 at α = 0 .1 and FEMNIST). We run the equal split for the geometric profile on the same two settings. Finally, a round-wise validation oracle provides a reference point. It picks each round’s budget using global validation loss, which no real federation can observe. 

Protocol. Hyperparameters, including E⋆ and a separate drift price λ for each profile, are selected on seeds 1–5 by mean validation accuracy over the last five evaluations. Test data are never used for selection. We report seeds 6–10, paired across methods by partition, initialisation and client sampling. Runs that diverge are retained and scored at chance accuracy for the remainder of the horizon. The window w = 4 was fixed in advance to balance estimation noise against staleness, and spans about 40 rounds at 10% participation. Pooling over one or over all past participations does not do significantly better (Appendix). Each profile’s drift price is the best of a five-value grid spanning the range that a pilot round calibrates (Section 5.2). The selected prices range from 6 .7 × 10 −4 to 1 .27 × 10 4 (Appendix). The geometric shape is learned online. On CIFAR-10, however, it sits at its lower search bound in most decisions, so there the drift price effectively sets the budget. The power-law exponent mostly stays at its pilot value (2 .43 on CIFAR-10, 2 .48 on Shakespeare), because a rule that seldom trains past one epoch seldom accumulates the rounds a refit needs. In seven of the ten setting–profile pairs the selected price is the grid’s largest, which makes extra epochs expensive. In five of these pairs every budget is already one epoch, so a larger price could not change the rule. Only for the power-law rule on CIFAR-10 at α = 0 .1 and on Shakespeare, where budgets still vary, could the optimum lie beyond the grid. ## 7 Testing the Theory Against Local Training

7.1 Diminishing returns (P1) 

On held-out full-budget local trajectories, the geometric profile extrapolates better than the power-law profile for 75%, 73%, 79% and 63% of clients on the four CIFAR-10 partitions and 78% on FEMNIST. 7.2 Drift cost (P2) 

Fig. 2(a) follows drift epoch by epoch within the same client-rounds. The first epoch moves the model 3 .6(FEMNIST) to 4 .9 (CIFAR-10) times further than the second. On CIFAR-10, δ = 0 .240 against 0 .049, 0.043 and 0 .040 for epochs 2–4. On FEMNIST, δ = 0 .208 against 0 .058, falling to 0 .028 by epoch 6. A stationary cost over all epochs would therefore be misspecified, but the anomaly sits entirely in the compulsory epoch, which never enters the optimal stopping decision (Corollary 3). The deployed plug-in does include it (Section 5.5), pricing continuation at a median 16 (CIFAR-10) and 7 (FEMNIST) times the drift later epochs realise. This shortens budgets, the safe direction, though only 2 .1% and 5 .1% of client-rounds stop after one epoch. After the first epoch, drift is close to stationary on CIFAR-10, with epochs 2–4 within 20% of each other, whereas on FEMNIST it halves between epochs 2 and 6. Corollary 3 states when this matters. Fitting a geometric decay to epochs 2–6 gives σ ≈ 0.83 per epoch on FEMNIST (0 .90 on CIFAR-10, epochs 2–4). The threshold rule therefore remains optimal provided per-epoch progress decays at least as fast, ρ ≤ 0.83. The epoch logs confirm it: successive loss decreases over epochs 2–4 have median ratio 0 .34, and 86% of them lie at or below 0 .83. Drift is additive: per-epoch squared displacements sum to a median 0 .96 (CIFAR-10) and 0 .94 (FEMNIST) of the final squared drift, and δ is negative in only 2 .5% and 5 .1% of epochs. 

7.3 Determinants of client budgets (P3, P4) 

Budgets fall as training proceeds, as P3 predicts (Fig. 2(b)). The mean geometric budget drops from 3.8 epochs in the first hundred rounds to 2 .0 in the last hundred on CIFAR-10, and from 7 .4 to 4 .7 on FEMNIST. They fall faster than predicted, however. The fitted slope is 1 .6 (FEMNIST) to 4 (CIFAR-10) times steeper than the slope implied by separately estimated within-round decay and across-round decay of first-epoch progress. The model therefore captures the direction of annealing but not its rate. To separate training stage from client identity, we fit each seed’s budgets by least squares with additive round and client effects and average the explained variance over seeds. The round alone explains 44% of budget variance on CIFAR-10 and 18% on FEMNIST. Adding client identity raises this by only about ten points on both. The remaining within-round variation is therefore largely not a stable client property, but the round-to-round evolution of each client’s estimates. The stable client component that does exist has the sign P4 predicts: budgets correlate negatively with the number of local examples (Spearman −0.17 on CIFAR-10 and −0.09 on FEMNIST, averaged over seeds).

7.4 Nested decisions (P5) 

Successive epoch gains are negatively autocorrelated. For the power-law runs, lag-one correlations of the residual per-epoch loss decreases average −0.36 on CIFAR-10 and −0.50 on Shakespeare. For the geometric runs, they average −0.40 on CIFAR-10 and −0.32 on FEMNIST. On the geometric runs the margin of Corollary 5 fails in every client-round with at least four epochs. Under the fitted model, a stopping decision would reverse in 19% (CIFAR-10) and 27% (FEMNIST) of them. These are in-sample estimates on short, self-stopped trajectories, which fit each path’s own scale and omit unobserved continuations, so they are indicative only. Little is lost in practice: a rule that solves the autoregressive stopping problem exactly by dynamic programming matches the one-stage rule that uses the same loss-scale estimator (Table 3).

FedNova needs two to eight times as many epochs to reach the same tail, at E = 5, 10 or 20, and then loses 0 .9–4 .3 points of mean accuracy, every interval excluding zero (Appendix). Every FedAvg budget stays below it, and even the oracle reaches only 37 .8% with 200k epochs. The client-side rules ALT and FLASH use fewer epochs on CIFAR-10 but reach clearly lower tails. On FEMNIST, ALT is the only deployable method whose tail is as high as FedStop ’s, and it needs 3 .6 times as many epochs. FLASH and FedNova come within two points with fewer epochs, a difference within noise. FedStop comes close to the oracle’s tail there at about a quarter of its epochs. FedStop thus spends local epochs where they raise the accuracy of the worst-off clients. 

Robustness. FedStop is robust where a heuristic is not. Per-client early stopping ended at chance accuracy on all five CIFAR-10 seeds. It never learned on two of them and fell back after peaks of 27–43% on the other three (Fig. 4), and it collapsed at every patience value on some selection seed. On Shakespeare it trained 2 .2times as many epochs for 0 .75 points less accuracy on every seed (49 .4%, [0 .53 , 0.96]). On FEMNIST the two are level (80 .7% and a cross-fitted tail of 69 .6%, against FedStop in Tables 4 and 5). FedStop itself failed only at drift prices low enough to train two to five times longer than the selected one, and never at the two largest. An upward mis-set is therefore safe in practice as well as in principle. A drift price transferred from another setting also stays level with the locally tuned one on FEMNIST and Shakespeare. The one-stage rule is also more robust than the sequential alternatives we prespecified. A sequential probability ratio test and an upper-confidence-bound rule trained far longer and collapsed on five and four of the five CIFAR-10 seeds, while the exact autoregressive rule matched the one-stage rule (Appendix). 

Other mechanisms. FedProx, which penalises the drift that FedStop prices, collapsed on one CIFAR-10 seed (51 .6% over the five, tail 26 .8%). It reaches 82 .0% on FEMNIST (cross-fitted tail 68 .2%) and is the most accurate method on Shakespeare (50 .7%, tail 49 .2%, against FedStop in Tables 4 and 5). 8.4 Client-side stopping rules 

ALT and FLASH also set a budget for each client, so they test whether FedStop ’s model-based rule improves on simpler signals. We port each rule’s local stopping criterion into our training protocol, with the same learning rates, seeds and step-normalised aggregation. These are ports of the stopping rules, not reproductions of the authors’ complete systems. ALT stops a client once the cosine similarity between its local features and the global model’s features on the same batch falls below a threshold τ . The features are the first dense pre-activation for the CNNs and the final LSTM hidden state for Shakespeare. FLASH stops once an epoch reduces the mean validation cross-entropy by less than γ. A five-epoch FedAvg pilot sets each grid: the 10th to 90th percentiles of the pilot’s feature similarity for ALT, and 0.1 to 3 times its median first-epoch validation-loss drop for FLASH. Each threshold is then selected on seeds 1–5 like every other hyperparameter, with up to two outward extensions of the grid. Three selections, ALT on CIFAR-10 and Shakespeare and FLASH on Shakespeare, remain at the edge of the grid after both extensions. They are therefore bounded-search winners rather than interior optima. Table 7 reports the results, with bold marking the best method in each column. On CIFAR-10, geometric

## 9 Discussion 

Limitations. Three limitations matter most. First, optimality requires stopping decisions that do not reverse, and local progress is more strongly autocorrelated than the margin of Corollary 5 allows. An exact trajectory-aware rule gains nothing over the one-stage rule (Table 3), but the guarantee does not cover these trajectories. Second, the model describes a single round, so it predicts that budgets anneal but not how fast they do. Third, the drift price is motivated by an aggregation bound but chosen on validation data, because the curvature constants that would fix it are unavailable for neural networks. Beyond the theory, 17 the evidence comes from small vision and language models in simulated federations of 100 clients, so larger models and cross-device scale remain untested. 

Future directions. Three directions follow. One is optimal stopping under dependent progress: rules with regret guarantees for autocorrelated gains, between one-stage look-ahead and full dynamic programming. The other is a cross-round theory that couples each client’s stopping problem to the server’s trajectory, so that the annealing of local work, and with it the drift price, is derived rather than tuned. The third is stopping without monitoring passes, by correcting the losses recorded during training for within-epoch drift, which would remove most of the rule’s remaining overhead. 

## 10 Conclusion 

We recast the choice of local epochs in federated learning as optimal stopping with a priced drift cost. This view yields a provably optimal one-stage threshold, closed forms that make the rule’s behaviour interpretable, and an exact account of what estimation costs. Tested against epoch-level evidence, the model captures how local training actually behaves, and its central lesson is that the stage of training, not the identity of the client, determines how much local work pays. Acting on this, FedStop protects the worst-off clients at a cost of at most one point of mean accuracy, using a decision rule whose computation and memory are negligible beside training. It also degrades gracefully, rather than failing, when its single parameter is set too high. 

## Broader Impact Statement 

FedStop aims to make federated learning serve its worst-off clients better: under strong data heterogeneity it raises the accuracy of the least-served decile of clients at a cost of at most one point of mean accuracy. This can benefit users whose data differ most from the majority, who are often the ones a shared model serves poorly. The rule has three costs that deployments should weigh. First, where budgets vary, clients train for more local epochs than under tuned FedAvg, which consumes more energy and battery on client devices. Monitoring on a small fixed subset of examples reduces this overhead. Second, the gain for the worst-off clients can come with a slight loss for the best-served ones. Third, as in FedNova, each client reports its number of local steps, which reveals its local dataset size and how long it trained. FedStop exchanges no data or gradients beyond these standard updates, so it adds no other privacy exposure. All experiments use public benchmark datasets and involve no human subjects.

## F Training dynamics 

The main text’s training-curve figure follows top-1 and worst-decile accuracy over all 1000 rounds for geo-metric FedStop , tuned FedAvg, a random swap of FedStop ’s budgets and per-client early stopping. On FEMNIST the swap learns slightly faster in the first 200 rounds, consistent with its larger share of SGD steps, and the methods converge to similar top-1 accuracy after about round 400, while tuned FedAvg’s worst decile stays about four points below FedStop ’s. Per-client early stopping ends at chance accuracy on every CIFAR-10 seed. On two seeds it never learns, and on three it falls back after peaking at 27–43%, because returning its best iterate lets a client whose validation loss worsens hand back the unchanged broadcast model. Figure S1 shows where geometric FedStop spends its epochs, for every client and round of one reporting seed, with clients sorted by local data size. On CIFAR-10 budgets fall from left to right for almost every client, so the stage of training sets the budget (P3), and the smallest clients train somewhat longer early on, the weak size effect of P4. On FEMNIST budgets also start high everywhere but later vary more from round to round within each client, consistent with the round explaining less of the budget variance there.

## H Shape estimator and pilot priors 

The shape minimises J(θ) = nres log(SSE( θ)/n res ) + np(θ − θ0)2/s 20 over the current and last w = 4 par-ticipations, on 200 grid points refined by golden section, with np = 2 and search ranges ρ ∈ [0 .05 , 0.9995], 

a ∈ [0 .1, 4]. The prior ( θ0, s 0) and the drift prior δprior , the pilot median of δ(1), come from a pilot round per setting and profile. The prior is used alone until three informative rounds exist. Table S6 lists the values with the selected drift price and the share of decisions whose shape equals its prior. Each drift price is the best of a five-value grid spanning the range that the pilot round calibrates, selected on seeds 1–5 by validation accuracy. The selected prices range from 6 .7 × 10 −4 to 1 .27 × 10 4. Each grid spans many orders of magnitude, because the budget depends on the drift price only through log κ. On CIFAR-10, for instance, the geometric grid runs from 1 .27 × 10 4 down to 1 .3 × 10 −23 . The power-law exponent rarely leaves its prior, because a rule that seldom trains past one epoch seldom accumulates informative rounds.

## J Computational cost and subset monitoring 

Compute is counted in modeled client-epoch equivalents, including every monitoring pass and weighting a forward pass by each setting’s forward-to-training cost ratio. It is not measured elapsed time. In the reported runs each client evaluates its training loss on its full local dataset before training and after every epoch. Table S9 compares this with monitoring on a fixed subset of min(128 , n i) local examples, measured on the reporting seeds with every selected drift price unchanged. Work is expressed as a percentage of two-epoch FedAvg’s work on the same seeds, both to reach 95% of tuned FedAvg’s final mean accuracy and over the full horizon. The subset lowers the work in every setting. It brings the power-law rule on CIFAR-10 (89%) and the geometric rule on CIFAR-100 (91%) below two-epoch FedAvg’s work to reach the target, while geometric 

FedStop on CIFAR-10 and FEMNIST still needs more (149% and 203%). The saving is smaller than an accounting estimate that holds the decisions fixed would suggest, partly because the subset changes some decisions: on CIFAR-10, geometric clients executed 88 more epochs per run ([11 , 165]). No mean-accuracy difference excludes zero. The geometric CIFAR-10 tail fell by 1 .34 points ([0 .10 , 2.57]), which triggered a prespecified re-selection of the drift price. The re-selection chose the same price, so its repeat on the same seeds is a same-policy rerun rather than a tuning gain, and it is not an independent replication. In that repeat the tail differs from full monitoring by −0.20 ([ −2.51 , 2.12]). Figure S2 shows the measured work and, for reference, the share of monitoring passes that a subset of each size keeps.

## L Predicting client workload 

A client’s budget times its known steps per epoch is its local workload, so a server that can predict budgets can anticipate computation and stragglers. Predicting each client’s budget at its next participation from its previous one gives a mean absolute error of 0 .30 epochs on CIFAR-10 and 1 .44 on FEMNIST, against 0.47 and 2 .16 for the previous round’s cohort mean, and is exact for 76% and 48% of participations. Budget history is thus a useful scheduling signal on CIFAR-10 and a modest one on FEMNIST, consistent with budgets varying more across rounds than across clients.
