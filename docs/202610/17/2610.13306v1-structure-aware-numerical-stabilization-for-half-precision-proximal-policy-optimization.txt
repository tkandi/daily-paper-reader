# Structure-Aware Numerical Stabilization for Half-Precision Proximal Policy Optimization 

## Linxuan Wu

## Abstract 

Local online training on resource-constrained platforms requires low computational and training-state overhead. We study the nu-merical stability and computational efficiency of half-precision proximal policy optimization (PPO) on commodity GPUs. Directly converting PPO training to FP16 is unreliable because numerical errors feed back through policy updates and subsequent data col-lection, while PPO repeatedly compares a current policy with a fixed behavior policy. We propose PPO-FP16 with three structure-aware modules. Global scalar protection stabilizes statistics shared across a minibatch or parameter update. Policy-change residual protection extracts and preserves small differences between the current and behavior policies. Efficient persistent-update protection stabilizes optimizer state and parameter writeback while reducing state storage. These changes preserve the PPO objective, hyper-parameters, and training budget. Across six continuous-control tasks and five seeds, PPO-FP16 achieves the lowest mean return-AUC rank (1.90) among the four methods and ranks first in 17 of 30 environment–seed comparisons. Eight of nine performance configurations yield 1 .16–2 .03 × faster PPO updates. Relative to Full FP32 Adam, optimizer-state memory falls by 71 .7–74 .9%, and peak allocated GPU memory falls by up to 44 .5%. The results show that protecting a small set of structurally important quantities can deliver stable, efficient FP16 PPO without retuning.

## 1 Introduction 

Reinforcement learning (RL) agents adapt their policies through continued interaction with an environment. This ability is valuable in robotics, autonomous systems, and control. An agent trained in simulation may need further training after deployment to accom-modate differences in hardware, sensor noise, or task conditions. Local adaptation is especially useful when data cannot easily be uploaded, communication adds latency, or the device must operate independently for long periods. These resource-constrained set-tings motivate lower computational, memory, and energy costs for training [37]. Low-precision training offers a direct way to reduce these costs. FP16 parameters and states use half the storage of FP32 values, and compatible hardware can provide higher arithmetic through-put [20]. In continuous-control tasks, PPO often uses compact actor– critic networks, making it a promising candidate for deployment on resource-constrained edge devices. Yet half-precision PPO is diffi-cult to stabilize. Each policy update changes subsequent actions and the data used for later updates. PPO also performs several optimiza-tion epochs on each rollout and uses a probability ratio between the current and behavior policies in its clipped objective [24]. When the policy change is much smaller than either absolute log-probability, FP16 rounding can change the ratio and clipping decision. We study this numerical and computational problem on commodity GPUs as a step toward eventual use on resource-constrained platforms. SAC-FP16 showed that algorithm-specific numerical reformu-lation can stabilize half-precision SAC training [3]. SAC and PPO, however, have different objectives and data flows. SAC uses replay and a maximum-entropy objective, whereas PPO collects an on-policy rollout and performs multiple updates relative to the fixed behavior policy that generated it. In PPO, errors in batch-level ag-gregate statistics, such as means, variances, and gradient norms, can affect an entire minibatch, small differences between the current and behavior policies determine clipping decisions, and gradients must pass through Adam states and the discrete FP16 parameter grid before producing persistent policy changes. We therefore propose PPO-FP16. It preserves the PPO network architecture, objective, hyperparameters, and training budget while reorganizing three error-propagation paths. Module A, global scalar protection, stabilizes shared statistics through selective FP32 reductions and scale rearrangement. Module B, policy-change residual protection, extracts differences be-tween Gaussian policies before forming absolute log-probabilities and performs clipping in the log domain. Module C, efficient persistent-update protection, builds on hAdam’s root-second-moment representation. It introduces SHARP (Structured Half-precision Adam with Root-state Preconditioning), which shares second-moment state across parameter groups, and selectively compensates FP16 writeback of the log-standard-deviation. The residual-domain policy calculation and SHARP’s structured state sharing are our main new designs. Our contributions are: 

• We identify three sensitive error-propagation paths in FP16 PPO through stage-by-stage comparisons on fixed train-ing data and numerical stress tests: shared statistics, small policy changes, and persistent parameter updates. 

• We propose a structure-aware FP16 PPO design with three complementary protection modules while preserving the PPO objective and training configuration.

## 2 Background 2.1 Numerical Limits of Low-Precision Training 

IEEE FP32 has one sign bit, eight exponent bits, and 23 explicit fraction bits. FP16 has one sign bit, five exponent bits, and ten ex-plicit fraction bits [14]. Fewer exponent bits narrow the dynamic range, while fewer fraction bits widen the spacing between repre-sentable values. Training can consequently suffer from overflow, underflow, and loss of small terms during addition or subtraction. Early studies showed that successful limited-precision training de-pends on rounding, accumulation, scaling, and the placement of high-precision operations [2, 10, 31]. Recent methods control tensor scales and numerical dataflow more explicitly. Unit Scaling [4] targets unit variance in weights, activations, and gradients, while Jetfire [36] combines per-block quantization with an INT8 training dataflow. Recent low-precision studies have extended this direction to FP8 training, optimizer-state quantization, and long-horizon numerical stability [8, 22, 34, 35]. At lower precision, recent work studies how exponent–significand allocation affects training [27], develops FP4 training recipes [6, 32], and controls outliers or sensitive matrix multiplications through numerical and architectural transformations [1, 13]. These studies concern large language models rather than PPO, but reinforce the general principle that low-precision training depends on where and how numerical representations are applied.

We therefore examined whether SAC-FP16 components transfer to PPO. Our exploratory transfer study found limited, environment-dependent gains from these components. This motivated a closer analysis of PPO’s own numerical paths. Recent work has also studied low-precision reinforcement learn-ing at the scale of reasoning language models. QuRL [18] accelerates rollout generation with INT8 or FP8 actors and addresses mismatch and small quantized weight updates. QaRL [9] aligns training-side computation with quantized rollouts to reduce the mismatch be-tween a low-precision rollout policy and a full-precision learner. These methods focus on quantized LLM rollout generation, whereas our study concerns native half-precision PPO learner updates and persistent training state in continuous control.

## 3 Method: Structure-Aware Half-Precision PPO 

We propose a structure-aware design for half-precision PPO. Low-precision errors concentrate in three paths with broad or persistent effects. First, errors in means, variances, and gradient norms affect an entire minibatch or parameter update. Second, rounding and can-cellation obscure small changes between the current and behavior policies. Third, Adam state updates and FP16 parameter writeback can distort the second moment or discard nonzero updates. By structure-aware , we mean choosing numerical representa-tions and computations according to a quantity’s role in PPO and the reach of its errors. We distinguish global statistics shared across samples or parameters, policy differences that determine clipping, and optimizer and parameter states that persist across updates. We protect information at these propagation points. Figure 1 locates the three modules in the PPO update. Parameters, the main forward and backward passes, gradients, and major optimizer states use FP16. FP32 is reserved for a few points where errors propagate across samples, time, or updates. ## 3.1 Module A: Global Scalar Protection 

Our sensitivity analysis found that some local low-precision errors spread through global statistics. PPO includes several many-to-one computations: sums, means, variances, or norms reduce many sample or parameter values to a few statistics, which are then shared by a minibatch or parameter update. Advantage statistics, value-loss reduction, and the global gradient norm all have this form. The advantage mean and variance normalize a whole minibatch, the batch-mean value loss sets the scale of critic gradients, and the global gradient norm determines one clipping coefficient shared by all parameters. Errors in these quantities therefore propagate beyond the values that first produced them. ## 3.3 Module C: Efficient Persistent-Update Protection 

Adam [16] maintains persistent first- and second-moment states. The latter involves squared gradients. In FP16, a representable gradi-ent may have a square too small to represent, distorting the second moment and potentially destabilizing training. Recent convergence analysis likewise identifies weights and second moments as sensi-tive components when adaptive optimizers are quantized [28]. Building on hAdam from SAC-FP16 [3], we store 𝑤 𝑡 = √𝑣 𝑡 

rather than the underflow-prone second moment 𝑣 𝑡 and update it with a stable hypot operation. Our implementation retains Adam’s first moment, bias correction, and update semantics; it changes the numerical representation of the second moment. Standard Adam stores both moments for every parameter. For an 

𝑀 ×𝑁 weight matrix, each state has 𝑀𝑁 elements and must be read and written during updates. Low-precision optimizers compress these persistent states through 8-bit block-wise quantization [7], 4-bit moment quantization [17], or FP8 representations [8, 22, 35]. SHARP instead reduces their granularity by sharing a root-second-moment scale within parameter groups. Adafactor factorizes second moments [26]; Shampoo and KrADa-grad use structured matrix preconditioners [11, 19]; and 4-bit Sham-poo quantizes preconditioner state [33]. SHARP instead shares only the root-second-moment scale within PPO parameter groups. Building on hAdam, we introduce SHARP (Structured Half-precision Adam with Root-state Preconditioning), whose new con-tribution is structured state sharing for PPO parameters. SHARP maintains the first-moment state element-wise, while the root-second-moment state is shared within predefined parameter groups. For an 𝑀 × 𝑁 hidden-layer weight matrix, we use row-wise grouping, yielding 𝑀 shared root-second-moment states instead of 𝑀𝑁 element-wise second-moment states. Parameters within the same group therefore share only the preconditioning scale, while retaining independent first-moment states and parameter updates. Bias correction follows standard Adam, and the parameter

For small parameter groups and parameters that directly affect the policy output, we retain fine-grained states rather than enforcing sharing. For each row, SHARP updates the shared root-second-moment from a scale-safe gradient root-mean-square through the stable

Fewer second-moment values also reduce state reads and writes. FP16 writeback can also swallow small updates. Let 𝜃 𝑡 be a stored parameter and 𝑢 𝑡 its optimizer update at step 𝑡 . Module C thus protects both optimizer state and parameter write-back: SHARP reduces persistent second-moment storage and traffic, while selective residual writeback prevents repeated loss of small log-standard-deviation updates. 

## 3.4 Computational and Storage Complexity 

Let 𝐵 , 𝑅 , 𝐷 , 𝑃 , and 𝐺 denote minibatch size, rollout samples, action dimensions, parameters, and second-moment groups. All modules preserve PPO’s asymptotic update cost. Module A adds one 𝑂 (𝑃 )

maximum reduction; Module B remains 𝑂 (𝐵𝐷 ) and adds 2 𝑅 tem-porary bytes; Module C remains 𝑂 (𝑃 ) but reduces the root-second-moment state from 𝑃 to 𝐺 ≪ 𝑃 elements. For an 𝑀 ×𝑁 row-grouped layer, optimizer state changes from 4 𝑀𝑁 to 2 𝑀𝑁 + 2𝑀 bytes, ap-proaching reductions of 50% versus element-wise FP16 Adam and 75% versus Full FP32 Adam. In practice, eight of nine configura-tions achieve 1 .16–2 .03 × update speedups, while optimizer-state memory falls by 71 .7–74 .9% relative to Full FP32 Adam (Sec. 4.4). 

## 4 Experiments 

We evaluate numerical sensitivity, the contributions of Modules A–C, long-horizon learning quality, compute and memory costs, and the limits of lower-precision formats. 

Implementation details. All long-horizon methods use the same two-layer 2 × 64 tanh actor and critic, PPO objective, and hyper-parameters, with no environment- or precision-specific retuning. Runs use 2,048-step rollouts, 10 PPO epochs, 32 minibatches of 64 samples, and five seeds for 512,000 environment steps. The learning rate is 3 × 10 −4 with linear annealing, 𝛾 = 0.99, GAE 𝜆 = 0.95, the PPO clipping threshold is 0 .2, and the global gradient-clipping threshold is 0 .5. Adam uses (𝛽 1, 𝛽 2, 𝜖 ) = (0.9, 0.999 , 10 −5). Full FP32, AMP-FP16, AMP-BF16, and PPO-FP16 share these settings. PPO-FP16 keeps the main training state in FP16, with FP32 accounting for less than 0 .1% of persistent training-state elements. Experiments use PyTorch 2.3.0, CUDA 12.1, and an RTX 4060 Laptop GPU, which natively supports FP16 and BF16 computation. We use native Py-Torch dtypes and autocast rather than fake quantization or explicit quantize–dequantize emulation. The Native-BF16 study uses ac-tual BF16 tensors through a PyTorch reference implementation because the final SHARP CUDA kernel is FP16-specific. The nine-configuration performance benchmark uses one CUDA Graph for each complete PPO update. 

## 4.1 Sensitivity Analysis 

We first converted the parameters, gradients, and Adam states of standard PPO directly to FP16. We compared this naive path against FP32 stage by stage on the same training data. Naive FP16 did not immediately produce NaNs or Infs, but iteration KL and clip frac-tion soon diverged from FP32, as did learning curves across seeds. Here iteration KL is the approximate policy divergence accumu-lated within an update iteration, and clip fraction is the fraction of minibatch samples whose probability ratios leave the unclipped interval. Numerical error accumulated through the PPO update cycle. To locate the sources, we replayed the FP32 and FP16 paths on fixed rollouts and minibatches. We compared advantage statistics, losses, gradient norms, log-probabilities, log ratios, clipping deci-sions, Adam states, and actual parameter writeback. Stress tests covered extreme ranges rarely encountered in ordinary training. Holding the data fixed isolated differences in numerical representa-tion and computation. The first problem involved shared statistics. Errors in the ad-vantage mean and variance altered the scale of a whole minibatch. The global gradient norm had a similar reach. Direct FP16 squaring and summation produced nonfinite norms for all 24 large-gradient stress inputs; scaled-norm computation produced finite norms for all 24. Reduction error and overflow during squaring both mattered. The second problem involved the policy ratio. With Hopper states, actions, and old and new Gaussian parameters fixed, sub-tracting two absolute log-probabilities gave a mean absolute error (MAE) of 1 .07 × 10 −3 in the local policy change. Constructing the change directly from policy-parameter differences reduced MAE to 4.7 × 10 −5. The absolute-log-probability path obscured the small difference that determines the ratio and clipping decision. The third problem arose in optimizer state and writeback. Na-tive FP16 Adam rounded 64 .55% of second-moment states to zero, and the update-magnitude ratio relative to FP32 Adam reached 124 .07 ×. In the root-second-moment domain, the zero-state ratio fell to 0 .58%. The update-magnitude ratio fell to 1 .0722 ×. Later in 

> 5

training, many nonzero updates did not change FP16 parameters; accumulating them allowed eventual writeback. Thus both second-moment underflow and swallowed updates caused persistent error. Together, these tests locate three connected paths: shared reduc-tions spread local error, absolute probability calculations obscure small policy differences, and optimizer state and the FP16 grid re-tain error across updates. The three modules in Sec. 3 address these paths. 

## 4.2 Module Ablations 

We ablated the three modules in both subtractive and additive direc-tions on HalfCheetah-v5, with fixed networks and hyperparameters. Each configuration used three seeds. We marked a configuration Crash if it failed a 20,480-step stability gate; the others were reinitial-ized and trained for 151,552 steps. Full FP32 served as a reference. In the subtractive study, complete PPO-FP16 had the highest AUC and smallest endpoint variance. Removing A or B reduced AUC, while removing C caused a Crash. In the additive study, Naive FP16 crashed; adding C enabled training, and adding A and B raised AUC from 293.3 to 465.8 and 618.3. These results support complementary roles for the modules. 

## 4.3 Long-Horizon Learning Quality 

We trained for 512,000 steps in six Gymnasium MuJoCo-v5 continuous-control environments [29, 30]. We compared PPO-FP16 with Full FP32, AMP-FP16, and AMP-BF16. All methods used the same 2 × 64 tanh actor and critic, PPO objective, and hyperpa-rameters. Each method ran five seeds without environment- or precision-specific retuning. Full FP32 used FP32 parameters, activa-tions, gradients, and Adam states. AMP-FP16 kept FP32 parameters and Adam states while using FP16 autocast and GradScaler [20]. AMP-BF16 likewise kept FP32 parameters and Adam states while using BF16 autocast [15]. We define return AUC as the trapezoidal integral of deterministic evaluation return over environment steps, divided by the 512,000-step horizon. Figure 3 shows deterministic evaluation curves, and Table 3 reports return AUC. To avoid bias from different return scales, we ranked the four methods by return AUC within each environment and seed, then averaged over 30 environment–seed combinations. PPO-FP16 had the best mean rank, 1.90, versus 2.63 for each AMP method and 2.83 for Full FP32. It ranked first in 17 comparisons, versus 7, 5, and 1 for Full FP32, AMP-FP16, and AMP-BF16, respectively. PPO-FP16 had the highest mean AUC on Walker2d, Humanoid, and InvertedDoublePendulum, while maintaining stable learning on HalfCheetah, Hopper, and Swimmer. In paired comparisons, its AUC exceeded Full FP32, AMP-FP16, and AMP-BF16 in 20, 21, and 22 of the 30 environment–seed pairs, respectively. For cross-environment comparison, we normalized each envi-ronment’s AUC observations as e𝐴 = (𝐴 − 𝐴 min )/( 𝐴 max − 𝐴 min ),where 𝐴 min and 𝐴 max are the minimum and maximum over all four methods and five seeds in that environment. We then used an environment-stratified paired bootstrap to calculate 95% confidence intervals for method differences in e𝐴 . PPO-FP16’s normalized AUC difference from AMP-FP16 was 0.208, with interval [0.026 , 0.371 ].The confidence intervals against Full FP32 and AMP-BF16 include zero, while PPO-FP16 achieves the lowest overall return-AUC rank and maintains strong long-horizon learning quality across all six tasks without FP32 master parameters or element-wise FP32 Adam states. 

## 4.4 Compute Performance and Memory 

Performance tests covered nine network-width and batch-size com-binations on an RTX 4060 Laptop GPU. FP32 and PPO-FP16 per-formed the same complete PPO update. Each configuration used five independent processes, with 3,000 timed updates after warm-up. PPO-FP16 achieved 1 .16–2 .03 × faster updates in eight of nine configurations; the remaining one was near parity with FP32. SHARP reduced optimizer-state memory by 71 .7–74 .9% relative to Full FP32 Adam. Peak allocated GPU memory fell by up to 44 .5%. Recent low-precision systems also demonstrate substantial training gains on consumer GPUs [25]; our measurements address a different regime, namely native FP16 PPO updates with compact actor–critic networks. For a mixed-precision comparison, we tested widths 64, 512, and 2,048 at batch size 2,048 using Full FP32, AMP-FP16, AMP-BF16, and PPO-FP16. Each configuration used five paired processes, 500 warm-up iterations, and 1,000 timed updates. PPO-FP16 had the lowest update time at widths 64 and 512. At width 2,048 it was about 3–5% slower than AMP, but reduced optimizer-state memory from 64.70 to 16.23 MiB and peak allocated GPU memory from 181.89 to 101.01 MiB. Tables 1 and 2 use independent benchmark protocols; absolute latency should be compared only within each table. 

## 4.5 Precision-Boundary Study 

We study exponent range and significand precision separately. BF16 retains an FP32-like exponent range but has fewer significand bits [15]. Recent floating-point scaling studies also show that expo-nent allocation, significand length, and scaling granularity jointly determine low-precision training behavior [27]; FP4 methods there-fore require additional scaling or structural treatment [6, 32]. In a Native-BF16 variant , we replace PPO-FP16 parameters, gradi-ents, rollout data, first-moment state, and structured root-second-moment state with BF16 while retaining the three modules and their specified FP32 scalar boundaries. Because the final SHARP CUDA kernel targets FP16, we use an arithmetically equivalent PyTorch reference implementation. We compare learning quality, not BF16 speed, across the same six environments, five seeds, and 512,000 steps. Across the six environments, Native-BF16 retained only 10 .7– 79 .6% of PPO-FP16’s return AUC. A wider exponent range alone therefore did not preserve learning quality. The pattern is consistent with greater loss of small updates on BF16’s coarser parameter grid, but direct causal attribution requires update-level measurements. Our residual writeback covers only the log-standard-deviation, so it cannot compensate writeback of the other network parameters. We also held the exponent at five bits on HalfCheetah-v5 and shortened the main-tensor significand from E5M10 through E5M5. Each format ran two seeds for 100,352 steps. ## 5 Conclusion 

We presented PPO-FP16, a structure-aware approach that protects shared statistics, small policy changes, and persistent parameter updates while keeping parameters, gradients, and major optimizer states in FP16. Across six tasks and five seeds, it achieved the best mean return-AUC rank (1.90) and ranked first in 17 of 30 environment–seed comparisons. PPO updates were up to 2 .03 ×

faster, optimizer-state memory was 71 .7–74 .9% lower than with Full FP32 Adam, and peak allocated GPU memory was up to 44 .5% lower. BF16 and reduced-significand experiments delineated the remaining precision limits. These results establish the numerical and computational feasibility of FP16 PPO on commodity GPUs. Deployment on embedded accelerators, larger models, and more general low-precision methods remain future work.
