## Abstract 

Graph Neural Networks on large graphs commonly rely on in-memory graph storage with ad hoc data processing pipelines for both training and inference. Graph databases provide scalable storage and efficient traversal for graphs that can evolve over time, but their applicability in GNN training and inference are rarely studied. We show that graph DBs can be used in GNN training and inference directly, executing sampling, feature re-trieval, and even the full forward pass. We show different GNN training pipelines can perform on-the-fly sampling and retrieval in graph DBs using Cypher. We further provide optimisations that speed up the training process by ˜ 2.5x while maintaining the same accuracy. We illustrate that fully in-DB inference is possible, independent of how the GNNs are trained. Our inference setup can be >10x faster than cold loading the graph into RAM with no extra memory overhead on real-world graphs. Our work lays the foundation for in-graph-DB GNN training and inference. 

## Keywords 

Graph databases, Graph neural networks, Systems for graph ML. 

## 1 Introduction 

Graphs are ubiquitous, with domains ranging from social net-works to molecular structures. Predictive tasks on graphs include node classification and link prediction[28]. Graph Neural Net-works (GNNs) have emerged as the dominant architecture for these tasks[22, 28]. Most GNNs produce node embeddings by aggregating information from local neighborhood for each node. GNNs are particularly effective on data where relationships between entities carry important inductive bias. However, for a GNN of K layers, the computation graph of any node grows rapidly with K, a problem known as neighborhood explosion[8]. To address this issue, modern GNN training methods employ graph sampling that select subsets of full K-hop neighborhoods[2, 8, 31]. This introduces two additional stages into the training process, namely subgraph sampling on the topology and retrieval of feature vectors for every node in the sampled subgraph. Graph database is an established technology for storing and retrieving graph data[1]. Many real-world industry-scale graphs

Ad hoc storage and data processing pipelines are built for GNN training[19, 24, 33], with few systems-level improvements for inference. Moreoever, even though many of these referenced work are inspired by techniques from databases, none of them directly utilise existing graph DBs in the training or inference pipeline. We take an innovative, data-management perspective at GNN computation and formulate it as a database workload. Intuitively, most GNN forward equations are simply retrieving nodes ac-cording to some neighborhood (query) definition and perform aggregation. [14] touched upon this idea with a proof of con-cept. This inspires us to more tightly couple GNN training and inference process with graph DBs. Specifically, our contributions are: 

• We show that graph DBs can be used for forward com-putation in GNN training, including sampling and fea-ture retrieval. We note that the implementation can be done via either query (Cypher) or user-defined procedures (UDPs). We thoroughly benchmark different implementa-tion options and provide optimisation (pre-aggregation) that greatly speed up training for certain classes of graphs and GNNs. 

• We show that fully in-DB inference is possible, indepen-dent of how the GNNs are trained. It is particular useful for real-world graphs already stored in graph DBs that may evolve over time. To the best of our knowledge, this is the first work that uses graph DBs as a full GNN inference system.

Training. As graphs grow, distributed training is often used to remediate memory and throughput bottlenecks. The graph and features are partitioned across machines that synchronize weight updates. This improves throughput through parallelism, but at the cost of extra hardware, maintainence effort and graph partition time[27, 32]. Partitioned graphs additionally introduce extra memory (duplicate data) or slower querying[11, 25]. Some performs sampling on the partitioned graph directly in GPUs, but optimising GPU utilization and overall throughput remains challenging[13, 29]. 

Inference. Inference does not require the same repeated ran-dom access that makes training RAM-bound, since predictions are typically needed on-demand rather than continuously. Keep-ing the full graph resident in RAM between requests is therefore unnecessarily expensive when low latency inference isn’t crucial. Keeping the graph on disk between inference requests could sub-stantially lower the hardware cost, while providing acceptable inference latency. There are several algorithmic improvements that make infer-ence on large graphs more efficient, [16] incrementally updates embeddings after graph changes rather than recomputing full aggregations, [21] compresses graphs by reducing the number of nodes while minimising the approximation error. 

## 2.2 Graph Databases 

Graph databases provide native support for storing nodes and edges and their types and properties. Common graph databases include Neo4j[17](disk-based) and Kùzu[3](in-memory). On top of storage, there are query languages and query engines that efficiently traverse the graph. Cypher[5] is a common graph query language that follows the recent GQL standard[6]. The core concept of Cypher and GQL is graph pattern matching, where one can express patterns with filters on graphs in the DB, together with expressive transformations and aggregations. In addition to the declarative query languages, many graph DBs also support user-defined procedures (UDPs)[18] that allow developers to explicitly control traversal and retrieval through the DB kernel. 

## 3 System Design 

This section outlines our approaches for shifting parts of the GNN forward equations into graph DBs for both training and inference. 

## 3.1 Training Pipelines 

Our graph database-backed training pipeline stores the graph in a graph database while executing the learning algorithm in a separate training process. For each mini-batch, the database performs subgraph sampling directly on the graph on disk and returns the sampled subgraph and associated node features. Since only the sampled subgraph must reside in memory, this reduces the RAM requirement of the training process. The returned fea-tures are then used for the forward pass, loss calculation, and weight update, all executed in the training process (Figure 1).

UDP. Our second implementation replaces the Cypher queries with UDPs that are implemented in the DB. 

Aggregation in the graph database (Pre-aggregation) Our final variant reduces data transferred between the graph database and the training process by performing the parameter-free neigh-borhood aggregation of the first GNN layer inside the database. This eliminates the need to transfer the feature vectors of nodes that only contribute to the first aggregation operation, reducing the amount of data exchanged between the database and the training process. ## 3.2 Inference Pipelines 

Inference does not require backpropagation. This implies the full forward pass can be executed either inside the graph database or in a separate inference process. As a result, it introduces several possible inference architectures that further moves the forward pass into the graph database. We evaluate four such architectures to understand the trade-offs between latency and infrastructure cost. 

In-memory reference. This baseline has two versions, one (Cold) that loads the entire graph from disk to RAM, and one (Hot) that already has the graph in RAM upon the inference request. The full forward pass is executed in a inference process running on a CPU without the use of any graph database. 

Graph database with external inference process (Neigh-borhood sampling). This variant stores the graph in a graph database while executing the forward pass in a separate inference process. For each inference request, the inference process first executes sampling and feature retrieval followed by the forward pass executed in the inference process. 

In-database inference with UDP. In this variant, the trained GNN is deployed inside the graph database as a UDP. The data-base performs both neighborhood retrieval and the complete for-ward pass, returning only the final predictions. This minimizes data transfer between the database and an external process. 

In-database inference with Cypher. The full forward pass and sampling are executed entirely within the graph database as a single declarative query. We provide two implementations with slightly different Cypher queries. In the first, matrix mul-tiplicatoins are expressed with the general-purpose reduce() 

function, available in all Cypher versions. In the second, which we refer to as in_db_cypher_opt , we use Cypher 25’s native vector operations, which allow the aggregation to be expressed more directly. It is important to reiterate that to the best of our knowledge, there is no prior work that studies fully in-DB inference of GNN models, despite the simplicity of the idea and a basic implemen-tation (just some Cypher query). All prior work treat graph data-base simply as storage from which data is exported. 

## 4 Experiments 4.1 Setup 

All experiments were run on a single machine with 32 vCPUs and 256 GB of RAM, running Ubuntu 22.04 LTS on an Intel x86-64 platform. The Neo4j databases were hosted on four NVMe drives unified into a single RAID 0 partition, providing high sequential and random I/O throughput for disk-resident graph access. Python 3.12 with PyTorch 2.7.1 and PyTorch Geometric (PyG) 2.7 is used. UDPs are written in Java for Neo4j. For the ex-periments the following datasets were used: Cora [30], CoAuthor Physics [20], arXiv, ogbn-products and ogbn-papers100M [9]. More dataset statistics and model configuration are specified in Table 1. The hyperparameters were kept fixed throughout the experiments. For all training and inference pipelines that use Neo4j, we store the properties as byte arrays, which are more efficient during data transfer and decoding (Appendix A).

## 4.3 Inference 

We implement and benchmark the 5 inference strategies outlined in Section 3.2. For any GNN model, we can export a definition of it (for example in torch state_dict) and translate that to Cypher or UDP, with the model weights as parameters. This means the models can be trained anywhere with any library. We also include the time for each implementation. ## 5 Conclusion 

Limitations and Future Work. Exact time and memory re-source differences of various in-DB training and inference im-plementations will vary for other non-Neo4j disk-base graph DBs. However, the general trend and observations will be valid. We only included the well-known GraphSAGE (node-wise) and GraphSAINT (subgraph-based) sampling techniques. Other ap-proaches such as layewise samplings are not studied. Other dis-tributed training systems or GPUs are not included. They are orthogonal improvements that can be incorporated. In this work, we focused on the current lack of overlap between the field of GNNs and graph DB. We take an innovative data-management perspective to GNN training and in-ference, proposing that the majority of the computation (forward equations) can be shifted into graph DBs. We ask the question of whether an operational graph DB can be used as the execution engine of a GNN and confirm positively. We also noted that com-putations inside graph DBs can be achieved with queries (Cypher) or UDPs. We provided several implementations of training and inferences and benchmarked the runtime behaviour thoroughly. We find that training with on-the-fly retrieval in graph DBs can be achieved with Cypher, and that it can be significantly opti-mised with UDPs and pre-aggregation (under high-dimension embeddings). We also find that the most efficient inference can be achieved with in-DB UDP implementation of GNNs, even on graphs that can constantly change. This tight coupling of GNNs and graph DB at inference time is previously unstudied. Our work provides new training and inference systems for GNNs and use case scenarios for graph DBs.

## A Neo4j embedding storage format 

Casting embeddings into compute-ready tensors does not incur a large overhead when storing and transmitting them as byte[] 

properties, as shown in Figure 9. Conversely, if embeddings are stored in Neo4j as f64[] , significant overhead is introduced. This is due to the type conversion required because PyG expects f32 

elements, whereas Neo4j stores f64 , resulting in a doubling of the size of the TCP-payload. Even more critical is the behavior of the Bolt client driver, which casts every incoming f64 into a Python float , creating a substantial overhead. The Python Global Interpreter Lock (GIL) and object instantiation during a linear scan of a larger array incurs high cost compared to the bulk 

memcpy of a byte buffer that can be performed when using byte[] 

in transit. This optimization is used for all setups including the baseline and the effects on the Cora dataset is shown in Figure 9.
