# Learning to Sync at the Edge: Client-Side Adaptive Synchronization for Federated Learning 

Ebasa Temesgen, Ammar Ahmed, Nathan Lechamo †, Ali Anwar, Maria Gini 

Department of Computer Science & Engineering, University of Minnesota, Minneapolis, MN, USA

Abstract —The scalability and performance of edge federated learning are fundamentally constrained by synchronization bot-tlenecks. Clients operate under heterogeneous compute, time-varying communication conditions, and, in some settings, privacy constraints, yet synchronization is often still handled with fixed schedules. However, synchronization decisions are often made outside the client, even though the client directly observes local progress, resource state, and communication conditions. We present a client-side reinforcement learning synchroniza-tion framework in which each client decides during training whether to upload, download, synchronize bidirectionally, or defer communication. The framework places a lightweight sched-uler alongside the local model and formulates synchronization as a resource-aware control problem driven by local learning and system state. We study this framework on two represen-tative edge workloads, spatiotemporal traffic forecasting and image classification. Across both settings, adaptive client-side synchronization improves the accuracy–cost trade-off, reducing system cost in traffic forecasting while maintaining competitive cost and improving final accuracy in image classification. We also consider privacy-constrained settings, where synchronization must account for not only latency and energy but also privacy expenditure. Overall, our results show that synchronization in edge federated learning is better treated as an adaptive client-side control problem than only as a server-side coordination mechanism. 

Index Terms —federated learning, reinforcement learning, com-munication scheduling 

I. I NTRODUCTION 

Federated learning (FL) is appealing for edge computing because it keeps raw data on-device while allowing many resource-constrained clients to train a shared model collabora-tively [1]. This is especially valuable in edge settings such as traffic sensing, mobility analytics, and distributed perception, where data are generated continuously across many devices and may be costly, sensitive, or impractical to move to a central location. By keeping data local, FL reduces repeated raw-data transfer while still enabling clients to benefit from shared learning. In practice, however, the limiting factor is often not local optimization itself, but synchronization. Edge clients rarely operate under stable conditions: wireless bandwidth drifts over time, devices differ in compute capability and energy budget, and local data streams evolve. In privacy-sensitive settings such as traffic and mobility data, communication can also expose sensitive model updates and metadata [2], [3]. A client may have made little local progress, may be operating under a degraded channel, or may be close to exhausting a privacy budget. Synchronizing in these states can add latency, energy use, and privacy exposure without proportional learning benefit. This paper studies a central systems question in edge feder-ated learning: when should a client synchronize ? Rather than treating synchronization as only a server-side coordination decision, we treat it as a decision-event made at the client. Prior approaches primarily adapt communication from the server, for example through aggregation timing, buffering, or client selection [4]–[6]. In contrast, our framework (shown in Figure 1) moves synchronization control to the client, where the decision can respond directly to local utility and resources. Learning-based client selection has also been studied using multi-agent PPO for dynamic client selection in vehicular federated learning [7]. Our focus is the synchronization action available to a client: skip communication, upload, download, or synchronize bidirectionally. This motivates viewing syn-chronization as a client-side control problem rather than only a server-side scheduling or aggregation problem. The value of synchronizing depends jointly on local learning progress, staleness, channel state, energy cost, privacy budget, and the future consequences of acting now versus waiting. A decision that saves cost in the current round may increase staleness arXiv:2610.14268v1 [cs.DC] 9 Oct 2026 and reduce utility later, while immediate synchronization may improve coordination at the expense of time and energy. These delayed and coupled trade-offs are difficult to capture with a single hand-designed rule or fixed protocol, since simple threshold policies typically react to only one signal, such as model change, staleness, or bandwidth. We therefore introduce a lightweight reinforcement learning (RL) scheduler for this client-side decision process, as illus-trated in Figure 1, and evaluate it against learning-based client schedulers and server-side communication-control schedulers. At each decision event, the scheduler observes client-side signals that reflect both learning status and system conditions, including indicators of model utility, communication cost, and client resource state. Using compact server-broadcast context such as model update freshness, rank, and recent synchronization rates further improves performance, while the action remains selected by the client scheduler. The scheduler then selects whether to upload, download, synchronize bi-directionally, or defer communication. This allows synchro-nization to respond to learning progress, device heterogeneity, and time-varying network conditions without changing the underlying federated training pipeline. In this way, commu-nication becomes an adaptive systems-level mechanism rather than a static training parameter. We also consider settings in which synchronization cost is shaped not only by latency and energy, but also by privacy constraints. This motivates a privacy-aware formulation of client-side synchronization, in which communication decisions are made with awareness of both system cost and privacy con-siderations rather than treating privacy as a separate concern after the schedule has already been chosen. We examine our framework on two representative edge workloads: spatiotemporal traffic forecasting and image classi-fication. These workloads differ substantially in data character-istics, learning dynamics, and operating conditions, allowing us to evaluate whether the same client-side synchronization mechanism can perform across distinct edge learning settings. This paper makes the following contributions:  

> •

We present a client-side adaptive synchronization RL framework for federated learning at the edge, in which each client decides when to synchronize with the server.  

> •

We formulate synchronization as a resource-aware control problem that combines task utility with communication latency, energy cost, device heterogeneity, and privacy constraints.  

> •

We evaluate the same framework on two represen-tative edge workloads, traffic forecasting and image classification, against learning-based and server-side communication-control baselines.  

> •

Prior FL systems [2], [13] study secure aggregation and differentially private updates, while resource-aware methods such as FedCS [14] account for client compute and wireless conditions. S YSTEM OVERVIEW AND DESIGN 

We consider an edge federated learning system with a central server and heterogeneous clients. Each client stores private data, trains a local task model, and runs a lightweight synchronization scheduler. The server maintains the global model and aggregates received updates, while the scheduler decides when communication is worth its latency, energy, and privacy cost. Our design separates task learning from synchronization control. The task model performs prediction or classification, while the scheduler controls only communication with the server. This separation lets the same scheduling mechanism support different task backbones. We use STAEFormer [18] for traffic forecasting and ResNet-18 [19] for image classification, but the scheduler itself is model-agnostic. Figure 2 shows the architecture. At each decision event, the scheduler observes local learning and system state and chooses whether to upload, download, synchronize bidirectionally, or defer communication. For our method, the server update rule is fixed so that improvements come from client-side synchro-nization rather than changing the aggregation rule. 

A. Event-Driven Workflow 

The system is simulated as an event-driven process. Each client evolves through five runtime states: unavailable, available-idle, local-training, uploading, and downloading. A priority queue of timestamped events advances the simulation in event time, including availability changes, local-training completion, scheduler decisions, communication completion, and server updates. Local training is scheduled automatically when a client is available, idle, and has no pending communication. Scheduler decisions are generated only for available idle clients, which prevents the policy from interrupting training or in-flight communication. Thus, the scheduler controls synchronization rather than local training. At a decision event, client m maintains a local model Wm

trained on private data Dm. Upload transmits a protected model update, download refreshes the local model from the server, and the bidirectional action uploads, waits for the corresponding server update, and then downloads the updated global model.

B. Forecasting Backbone Validation 

The synchronization result is meaningful only if its task model is a credible federated forecasting backbone. We there-fore compare FedSTAE with FCGCN, FedProx, and Fed-GRU [33]. Figure 7 reports one-step (5-minute) and 12-step (60-minute) MAE on PeMS-08 and METR-LA [34], using a 60-minute input window. FedSTAE has the lowest MAE among the shown methods on both datasets and horizons. The gain is especially large at 12 steps on PeMS-08: 29.42 MAE versus 32.64 for FCGCN and 82.94 for FedGRU [17], [35]. This check rules out the interpretation that communication savings are measured around an intentionally weak traffic model. Table VI isolates model quality as the PeMS-08 partition count grows. FedSTAE remains substantially better than Fed-GRU from 2 to 16 clients. Its long-horizon RMSE varies only from 29.21 to 30.60, although MAE and MAPE gradually worsen as each client receives a smaller, more heterogeneous portion of the sensor graph. This task-level scaling result complements Table VIII(a), which measures scheduler latency rather than forecast error.

The experiments distinguish client-side synchronization from server-side aggregation. We leave Eq. (1) unchanged and obtain the reported trade-offs solely by changing whether a client uploads, downloads, does both, or waits. The matched gated and unguided runs show a modest latency–accuracy trade-off, with no reduction in aggregate-release privacy ex-penditure. Small server broadcasts provide context without moving the action back to the server. 

A. Why Separate Upload and Download? 

The action counts help explain the benefit of separating communication directions. In traffic forecasting, PPO uses 2,404 uploads and 2,506 downloads; it neither collapses to Always-sync nor treats every server refresh as requiring a local contribution. FedBuff performs only 749 downloads and 701 aggregations, but has 9.2% higher RMSE and nearly three times as many uploads as PPO. This comparison separates two forms of sparsity: reducing server aggregation alone does not ensure that clients send only useful updates, and reducing downloads too aggressively can leave local models behind the global state. The four-way action space lets the policy manage these effects independently. The image results expose a different operating regime. PPO’s upload count is close to the server-side baselines, but it uses far fewer downloads than Always-sync and reaches higher accuracy. Here, the value lies less in suppressing all communication than in choosing the direction and timing of synchronization. This difference across tasks is exactly why a fixed period is brittle: the traffic workload rewards a strongly cost-gated pattern, whereas the non-IID image workload benefits from a more accuracy-oriented schedule. 

B. Control Placement and Deployment 

The scheduler is client-executed but not client-isolated. A server may broadcast small coordination features with its model version, after which the client combines that context with signals unavailable at the server, such as current loss change, dirty progress, channel cost, and remaining privacy budget. The server does not issue the action. This division preserves local responsiveness while avoiding the assumption that a client can infer federation-wide freshness from local history alone. A deployment would first select normalized cost weights from service-level goals, for example an energy ceiling or a target privacy budget, and train the shared policy against representative availability and channel traces. At runtime, each client constructs the observation only when idle, applies the hard feasibility mask, and executes one policy forward pass. The server needs no policy-specific aggregation rule. If resource distributions drift outside the training range, the safe fallback is to retain masking and use a conservative schedule while the policy is revalidated; the current experiments do not claim zero-shot robustness to arbitrary drift. 

C. Scope of Generalization 

The traffic and image experiments reuse the synchronization abstraction, observation groups, legal-action logic, and policy architecture. They do not reuse one set of learned weights across tasks. Each policy is trained for its workload because the meaning and scale of task utility differ between forecasting error and classification loss. The evidence therefore supports 

mechanism-level portability, not cross-workload policy trans-fer. This is still operationally useful: adding a task model does not require designing a new synchronization protocol, but it does require training and validating the controller for that task’s dynamics. VIII. L IMITATIONS 

Our evaluation is an emulated edge-federated setting rather than a live deployment. First, communication, availability, and energy are evaluated in an event-driven environment with calibrated analytical costs, not a geographically distributed device deployment. Second, privacy claims are conditional on the fixed clipping, aggregate-noise mechanism, accounting scope, and budget above; PPO schedules contributions but does not strengthen the underlying privacy guarantee. Third, the scale study stops at 32 clients. Trace-driven tests on physical edge devices, more seeds, and larger federations are necessary before making production-level latency, energy, or robustness claims. IX. C ONCLUSION 

Client-side synchronization turns communication from a fixed protocol choice into a resource-aware decision. Across traffic forecasting and image classification, the shared masked-PPO scheduler improves the measured quality–cost trade-off while retaining a fixed aggregation rule.
