# PairSight: Characterizing and Predicting Operator-Level Interference in GPU Sharing for DNN Inference 

Xiao Liu 1,2 , Yujia Fu 1, Yue Yu 2, Dan Huang 1,* 

> 1

Sun Yat-sen University , Guangzhou, China 2Peng Cheng Laboratory , Shenzhen, China {liux276, fuyj25}@mail2.sysu.edu.cn, yuy@pcl.ac.cn, huangd79@mail.sysu.edu.cn 

Abstract —Serving DNN inference is costly, yet a single task seldom saturates both the compute units and the memory bandwidth of a GPU. GPU sharing raises utilization by co-locating several tasks on one GPU, at the cost of interference among them. Existing systems estimate this interference with binary compute–memory rules, standalone scaling curves, or model-level predictors. However, these estimates rest on a limited understanding of interference. They leave the software and hardware properties of co-located operators largely unexplored and give no slowdown prediction that is both interpretable and accurate. We conduct an operator-level study of interference under SM partitioning and use hardware counters to trace it to contention on the shared memory path, where the slowdown depends jointly on both co-located operators. Based on these findings, we propose PairSight, a hardware-aware interference predictor that learns how the hardware behaviors of co-located tasks interact and predicts the latency of each task before co-location. On six DNN models including LLMs, PairSight predicts latency under sharing with 4.3% mean error, against 19 .0% for a compute–memory intensity rule, and adapts to unseen models and inputs with a few samples. 

Index Terms —GPU sharing, multi-tenant inference, perfor-mance interference, hardware performance counters, learned performance prediction 

I. Introduction Serving DNN inference is expensive and occupies large numbers of GPUs, yet a single inference task seldom saturates both the compute units and the memory bandwidth of a GPU [1]–[3]. GPU sharing reclaims the unused resources by co-locating several tasks on one GPU and dividing its time or its streaming multiprocessors (SMs) among them [3]–[17], and whether it pays off depends on how much the co-located tasks slow each other down. Existing systems estimate this interference with a binary compute–memory complementarity rule [3], [16], standalone scaling curves [8]–[10], [13], or predictors built per model on identity [12], [18], [19]. However, these estimates reflect a limited understanding of interference and do not relate the GPU performance metrics of individual operators to their slowdown under co-location. They can neither identify which co-located operators interfere nor quantify how much each operator slows down. For example, the complementarity rule expects a memory-intensive operator to suffer little interfer-ence beside a compute-intensive one, yet memory-intensive

We study interference in the setting adopted by recent GPU sharing systems, where a latency-critical high-priority (HP) inference task shares a GPU with a low-priority (LP) best-effort task [3], [14], [15], [18], consistent with how production clusters co-locate latency-sensitive and best-effort work [20]. A scheduler in this setting co-locates segments of consecutive operators from the two tasks under an SM split. To keep the HP task within its latency budget, it must predict how long each segment will run beside the other before co-location. An underestimate violates the budget, and an overestimate rejects a feasible co-location and forgoes LP throughput. Predicting this latency is hard. First, SM partitions leave the L2 cache, the DRAM channels, and the interconnect shared [21], whereas MIG isolates them only across a few statically configured instances [22]. Second, the effect of the co-located task, the co-runner, depends on the target and is hidden in aggregate statistics. Third, the prediction must precede co-location, yet measuring each of the many candidate segments and SM splits on the GPU takes about 0 .2 s, and offline profiling of co-executions covers only short segments of known models. Our operator-level study shows that interference is an inter-action between the hardware behavior of two operators rather than a property of either task or of its intensity. The memory traffic of the co-runner delays the accesses of an operator to the shared L2 and DRAM path. The resulting slowdown is large for an operator with heavy memory traffic and poor latency hiding, and small otherwise. The memory traffic of both operators and the latency hiding of each can be measured when each task runs alone, and PairSight, as hardware-aware interference predictor, learns their interaction to predict latency before co-location. A. Shared Resources under SM Partitioning 

A DNN model executes as a sequence of operators, each usually one GPU kernel. We partition the SMs between two co-located tasks at the granularity of a Texture Processing Cluster (TPC), a pair of SMs, by attaching a TPC mask to every kernel launch through libsmctrl [23]. Partitioning isolates registers, shared memory, the L1 cache, and the warp schedulers, while the L2 cache, the DRAM channels, and the interconnect remain shared [21]. For a target operator, or a segment of consecutive operators, that runs on 𝑝 TPCs beside a co-runner on 𝑞 TPCs, we define its slowdown as 

𝛿 = 𝑇 share ( 𝑝, 𝑞 )/ 𝑇 solo ( 𝑝 ), (1) where 𝑇 solo ( 𝑝 ) is its standalone latency on the same 𝑝 TPCs. The denominator removes the effect of receiving fewer SMs, and 𝛿 retains only the cost of concurrent execution. 

B. Observations 

Directional interference. Interference is directional, and a memory-intensive operator slows down markedly more than a compute-intensive one beside co-runners of either intensity. Figs. 1a and 1b co-execute a compute-intensive Linear 

and a memory-intensive BatchNorm operator, each as HP, beside each of the two operators. In the complementary pair, Linear retains most of its standalone performance. BatchNorm loses less beside Linear than beside another BatchNorm, yet it remains far below its standalone performance. A com-plementarity rule that admits this pair as low-interference underestimates the slowdown of BatchNorm. 

Variation within the same intensity. The slowdown varies widely among operators of the same intensity. Fig. 1c extends the measurement to operator pairs from four real models. Compute-intensive HP operators stay near 𝛿 = 1 beside every co-runner, whereas memory-intensive HP operators slow down markedly beside both compute-intensive and memory-intensive co-runners. Even within the memory-intensive group, one operator remains almost unaffected, and the slowdown of a single HP operator ranges from negligible to more than 2 ×

across its co-runners. 

Dependence on the co-runner. The co-runner changes the slowdown of a target even when the SM allocation of the target is fixed. Fig. 1d fixes the HP model and its SM allocation and varies only the co-runner. With a small SM allocation, ResNet-50 runs up to 1 .31 × longer beside ResNet-101 than beside BERT, and the spread narrows as the allocation grows. This spread bounds the error of any estimator that ignores the co-runner. 

C. Where Interference Comes From 

Under SM partitioning, interference arises mainly on the shared memory path. The latency of a target operator can be approximated as 

𝑇 ≈ 𝑇 compute + 𝑁 miss (𝐿 base + 𝑄 shared ) , (2) where 𝑁 miss counts the accesses of the target to the shared L2 and DRAM path, 𝐿 base is their uncontended service latency, and 𝑄 shared is the queueing delay added by concurrent traffic. The co-runner can only raise 𝑄 shared , and the resulting cost scales with 𝑁 miss and with the fraction of the added latency that the target fails to hide. The model is consistent with the three observations. A compute-intensive operator hides most of its memory latency, whereas a memory-intensive operator pays the added queueing delay on its memory accesses, which yields the directional interference. Operators of the same intensity still differ in 𝑁 miss and in how much latency they hide. Finally, 𝑄 shared grows with the memory traffic of the co-runner, the overlap of the two operators, and the SMs allocated to each, which makes the slowdown depend on the co-runner even at a fixed SM allocation. A correlation analysis of hardware counters identifies the shared L2 and DRAM path as the main source of interference. PairSight: Hardware-Aware Interference Prediction PairSight predicts the co-execution latency of a candidate pair of segments under a given SM split (Fig. 3). Its core is a segment model of two XGBoost regressors trained on measured co-executions of segments with at most 16 operators per side. The representation holds one slot per operator, which limits the segment model to this length, and PairSight serves longer segments by dividing both segments into windows and composing the window predictions. Its inputs come from profiling each DNN model once with Nsight Compute [24] for per-operator counters and once per TPC count for per-operator standalone latency. B. Accuracy and Cost 

PairSight and PairSight-MLP are the most accurate predic-tors (Table I). PairSight errs by 4 .3% on the HP side and 2 .4% on the LP side. The two take the same inputs, and differ only in the learner, so the accuracy stems from this operator-level input of both tasks rather than from a particular nonlinear learner.

D. Generalization and Adaptation 

PairSight transfers to unseen DNN models and batch sizes better than the existing estimates and adapts to them with a few samples (Table II). Trained only on the four classic models at their default batch sizes and tested on the Llama pairs and on unseen batch sizes 16 and 64, PairSight and PairSight-MLP give the two lowest errors in every held-out group, whereas Abacus, which relies on model identity, errs by more than 100% on the LP side. Continued training on 100 new data samples lowers the HP error from 30 .2% to 13 .5% on Llama, with each update taking at most 5 s on CPU. Window composition lets PairSight predict segments longer than its 16-operator window without new samples. On 400 separate co-executions with segments of 24 to 96 operators and SM splits absent from training, PairSight errs by 5 .5% and 5 .4% on the HP and LP sides, against 9 .8% and 17 .0% for the intensity rule. On the 80 asymmetric co-executions, where the segment with fewer windows runs longer and the windows that Eq. (4) pairs by index do not coincide in time, it errs by 4 .9% and 5 .5%. V. Conclusion Interference among co-located DNN tasks under SM par-titioning arises mainly on the shared L2 and DRAM path and depends jointly on both operators. PairSight learns this interaction from the per-operator standalone profiles of both tasks and predicts the latency of each task with 4 .3% mean error, against 19 .0% for an intensity rule. Continued training on a few samples and window composition extend it to unseen models, batch sizes, and longer segments. Accurate interfer-ence prediction before co-location lets GPU sharing systems select co-locations that meet their performance requirements. References
