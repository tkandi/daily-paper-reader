# Spectral-MoE: Heterogeneous Per-Layer Expert Utilization in Dense-to-MoE Conversion

## Abstract 

Large language models impose substantial memory demands and arithmetic costs dur-ing inference. Dense-to-mixture-of-experts (MoE) conversion is a model optimization technique that aims to reduce these arithmetic costs. Dense-to-MoE conversion parti-tions pretrained feed-forward network (FFN) neurons into experts and selects a subset for each token. Leading methods fall into two categories: analytic methods produce a converted model quickly using heuristics, while learning methods produce a model much more slowly by optimizing via gradients. Both approaches, however, fix the same active expert count, or “utilization,” at every layer. We introduce Spectral-MoE, which learns to allocate a utilization budget heterogeneously across layers. Its name draws a metaphor between separating a dense FFN into experts and separating a light beam into its spectrum. Spectral-MoE consists of two distinct approaches: a static ap-proach that uses a single per-layer scalar which is shared across tokens, and a dynamic approach which emits a per-token schedule. We demonstrate that both allocators im-prove performance over uniform on both the analytic-based and learning-based SOTA dense-to-MoE methods. At 25% FFN utilization, dynamic allocation reduces WikiText-2 perplexity relative to uniform by 1.04 , 2.96 , and 2.71 points on the learned base for Llama-2-7B, Llama-3-8B, and Qwen2.5-7B, respectively. The corresponding reduc-tions on the analytic base are 2.45 , 6.26 , and 2.17 points. On the learned base, dynamic allocation improves six-task mean accuracy over uniform by an average of 1.5, 4.2,and 3.6 percentage points across the three models at FFN utilizations of 12 .5% , 25% ,and 50% , respectively. These results establish per-layer expert utilization as a novel optimization axis in dense-to-MoE conversion.

The scaling of large language models (LLMs) has produced remarkable capabilities in nat-ural language understanding and generation (Brown et al., 2020). Realizing these capa-bilities, however, requires substantial compu-tation and memory bandwidth, with the latter becoming increasingly problematic for perfor-mance (Gholami et al., 2024). Optimizing the transformer (Vaswani et al., 2023), the engine powering nearly all LLMs, has therefore be-come an extensive area of research. In this do-main there exist two primary axes along which compression research is performed: quantiza-tion (Frantar et al., 2023; Nagel et al., 2020; Xiao et al., 2024; Lin et al., 2026), and pruning (Frantar & Alistarh, 2023; Sun et al., 2024; Han et al., 2015). A third technique, orthogonal to both quantization and pruning, called dense-to-MoE conversion has also begun to emerge (Zhang et al., 2022). A dense FFN evaluates every neuron for every token. Dense-to-MoE conversion groups these neu-rons into experts and routes each token to a subset, retaining pretrained weights while reducing active computation. The fraction of experts active for a given token is the “utilization” and the assignment 1arXiv:2610.19232v1 [cs.LG] 13 Oct 2026 of neurons to expert groups is the neuron-to-expert “partition”. Existing methods differ in how they construct the partition and the token-to-expert router (Zhang et al., 2022; Qu et al., 2024; Pei et al., 2026; Bamba et al., 2026; Szatkowski et al., 2024), but all either use a uniform per-layer allocation or a floating utilization budget. However, research on layer-dependent sensitivity to compression in quantization and pruning work (Dong et al., 2019b;a; Yin et al., 2025) motivates investigating heterogeneous per-layer allocation as a potential optimization axis in dense-to-MoE conversion. To this end, we present Spectral-MoE, which learns per-layer allocation under a fixed utilization budget via an allocator agnostic to the base dense-to-MoE conversion method. Our contributions are as follows: 1. Heterogeneous per-layer allocation. We introduce the first formulation of learnable per-layer allocation under a fixed per-token budget for dense-to-MoE conversion. This adds a third opti-mization axis to dense-to-MoE conversion alongside expert partitioning and routing. 2. Static and dynamic allocator tiers. We develop a static allocator that learns one scalar per layer shared across tokens and a dynamic allocator that learns a per-token schedule. Both of these techniques are able to adhere to a user defined utilization budget. 3. Consistent performance gains. We evaluate both static and dynamic allocators on the two lead-ing approaches to dense-to-MoE conversion: analytic methods that convert the model quickly using heuristics (Pei et al., 2026), and learning methods that optimize the model through slower gradient-based training (Bamba et al., 2026). Using our approach, we achieve SOTA mean accuracy at 25% utilization and outperform uniform across budgets (Figure 1). 

## 2 Background and Related Work 

2.1 Heterogeneous Allocation Per-Layer Allocation. Prior work on per-layer allocation typically uses pretrained MoEs, with LTE (Zheng et al., 2024) as an exception (Section 2.2). Alloc-MoE’s Alloc-L uses dynamic pro-gramming over profiled layer sensitivities (Liu et al., 2026), while LExI uses evolutionary search over per-layer output-perturbation estimates (Chitty-Venkata et al., 2025). Both optimize surrogates that predict a schedule’s accuracy degradation as a sum of per-layer sensitivities. Spectral-MoE instead learns per-layer utilization directly through gradients. 

Per-Token Allocation. Prior work on per-token allocation uses confidence-based routing, null experts, or budget redistribution to vary utilization across tokens. Confidence-based routing sets utilization from the concentration of routing scores (Huang et al., 2024), while AdaMoE introduces null experts to vary utilization (Zeng et al., 2024). SeqTopK and Alloc-MoE’s Alloc-T redistribute a layer’s utilization budget across tokens (Wen et al., 2026; Liu et al., 2026). Spectral-MoE’s dynamic allocator maps hidden states to per-token schedules that conserve a utilization budget across layers. 

2.2 Dense-to-MoE Conversion Analytic Methods. Analytic methods quickly construct neuron-to-expert partitions from heuris-tics. MoEfication clusters neuron input weights (Zhang et al., 2022), while LLaMA-MoE-v2 uses activation and gradient importance (Qu et al., 2024). CMoE groups neurons by their activation pat-terns through balanced clustering, then uses a representative neuron from each cluster to construct the router (Pei et al., 2026). Spectral-MoE’s analytic base uses CMoE to construct the partition and router, recovers weights with LoRA as CMoE does, then trains the allocator with both frozen. 

Learning Methods. Learning methods use slower gradient-based optimization. LLaMA-MoE partitions FFN neurons into experts before continued pretraining (Zhu et al., 2024). LTE jointly trains the router and model weights with an efficiency penalty and heterogeneous utilization follows from thresholding router scores (Zheng et al., 2024). DOT-MoE jointly learns the neuron-to-expert partition and token-to-expert router through differentiable relaxations (Bamba et al., 2026). Spectral-MoE learns per-layer utilization in tandem with this joint optimization, enforcing the same total utilization budget for every token. 2Partition

## 3 Methods 

Spectral-MoE learns per-layer allocation under a budget derived from a user-specified target utiliza-tion. This requires straight-through estimators (STE) (Bengio et al., 2013) and budget-preserving allocation. We develop these components for static allocation, then extend them to a dynamic allo-cator that produces token-dependent schedules (Figure 2). Appendix A summarizes the notation.

## 4 Results 

4.1 Experimental Settings Implementation. We implement Spectral-MoE using PyTorch (Paszke et al., 2019) and Hugging Face Transformers (Wolf et al., 2020), with learned-base conversion run on four NVIDIA H100 GPUs and analytic-base conversion run on one. All converted models use 128 neurons per ex-pert, following Bamba et al. (2026), giving 86, 112, and 148 experts per layer for Llama-2-7B, Llama-3-8B, and Qwen2.5-7B, respectively. On the analytic base, we construct the partition and router following CMoE (Pei et al., 2026), recover weights with LoRA (Hu et al., 2021), then freeze the recovered weights, partition, and router while training only the allocator. On the learned base, we extend DOT-MoE’s joint partition and router optimization (Bamba et al., 2026) to train parti-tion affinities, router weights, and allocator parameters in tandem while keeping pretrained weights frozen. We optimize trainable parameters with AdamW (Loshchilov & Hutter, 2019). Appendix C details the full testing and training configurations. 6Models and Evaluation. We evaluate Llama-2-7B (Touvron et al., 2023), Llama-3-8B (Grattafiori et al., 2024), and Qwen2.5-7B (Qwen et al., 2025). We measure language-modeling performance using WikiText-2 test perplexity (Merity et al., 2016). Following the canonical benchmark selection of dense-to-MoE methods (Pei et al., 2026; Bamba et al., 2026), we evaluate downstream benchmark performance using the harness provided by Gao et al. (2024) on BoolQ (Clark et al., 2019), SciQ (Welbl et al., 2017), PIQA (Bisk et al., 2019), WinoGrande (Sakaguchi et al., 2019), ARC-Challenge (Clark et al., 2018), and HellaSwag (Zellers et al., 2019). 

Baselines. CMoE (Pei et al., 2026) and DOT-MoE (Bamba et al., 2026) represent the current SOTA in analytic and learned conversion, respectively, while LLaMA-MoE (Zhu et al., 2024) and LLaMA-MoE-v2 (Qu et al., 2024) are representative of earlier work. Given the substantial improve-ment of CMoE and DOT-MoE over previous work (Table 1), we build Spectral-MoE on these two methods and include the earlier methods as reference points. We were unable to locate a public implementation of DOT-MoE during our experiments. As a result, the reported uniform numbers are from our own implementation of DOT-MoE based on the details provided in the paper. Given our baseline implementation falls short of the published accuracy, we report both sets of results. Appendix C.4 describes our implementations of the conversion bases. 

4.2 Benchmark Performance Comparison with Previous Works. Table 1 compares Spectral-MoE with uniform allocation and published methods at 25% FFN utilization. On the analytic base, dynamic allocation reduces per-plexity by 2.17–6.26 points and improves six-task mean accuracy by 0.2–1.3 percentage points over uniform, exceeding published CMoE mean accuracy by 3.0 points on both Llama-2-7B and Llama-3-8B. On the learned base, dynamic allocation reduces perplexity by 1.04–2.96 points and improves mean accuracy by 3.0–5.8 percentage points over uniform. It exceeds published DOT-MoE mean accuracy by 1.3 and 2.5 points on Llama-2-7B and Llama-3-8B, respectively, and matches Qwen2.5-7B at 63.3% at the reported precision. Both allocators lower perplexity across all models and bases, demonstrating robust benefits across different partition and router construction techniques.

## 5 Conclusion 

Spectral-MoE learns per-layer expert utilization during dense-to-MoE conversion under a user-specified budget. Static allocation learns a schedule shared across tokens, while dynamic alloca-tion revises each token’s schedule and conserves the same total budget. Dynamic allocation lowers perplexity on both conversion bases across all three models at nominal 25% utilization, with learned-base perplexity and mean-accuracy gains extending to 12.5% and 50%. It also exceeds published DOT-MoE mean accuracy on Llama-2-7B and Llama-3-8B and matches it on Qwen2.5-7B. The learned schedules combine stable layer averages with context-dependent variation while preserving the same routed budget for every token. Future work should examine whether stabilizing expert assignments improves the joint adaptation of routers and allocators. 9AI Use Statement
