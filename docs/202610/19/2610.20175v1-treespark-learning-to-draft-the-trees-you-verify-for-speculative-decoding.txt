# ABSTRACT 

Parallel drafters such as DFlash and DSpark reduce proposal latency, but early mismatches limit how much of each block can be accepted. Retaining alternatives introduces parent states and candidate choices that reference-conditioned train-ing does not directly supervise. We introduce TreeSpark, a unified training and inference framework extending the semi-autoregressive DSpark architecture. It constructs parent-conditioned candidate trees from one backbone pass and replays their expansion, pruning, and budgeted packing during training. Survival negative log-likelihood weights retained edges by reference-derived path mass, while a capped coverage hinge promotes target-important alternatives near local selection boundaries. Both objectives reuse reference-chain target distributions without ad-ditional target forward passes. We evaluate greedy decoding with Qwen3-4B and Qwen3-8B on nine general benchmarks and six writing domains. Across targets and suites, TreeSparkimproves mean acceptance length over DSpark by 23.4% to 31.6% and mean single-request throughput by 15.1% to 25.0%. Batched Qwen3-4B throughput improves by 12.6% to 17.2%. At fixed attention capacity and infer-ence policy, the proposed supervision further improves mean acceptance length by approximately 6.4% and mean system throughput by 4.2% to 5.0% on Qwen3-4B. Our code is available at https://github.com/Robot2050/TreeSpark .

# 1 INTRODUCTION 

Speculative decoding reduces the sequential cost of language model inference by separating inex-pensive proposal generation from parallel target verification (Xia et al., 2023; Leviathan et al., 2023; Chen et al., 2023). Its efficiency depends on accepted length relative to drafting and verification costs (Xia et al., 2024). Prior work develops feature-level autoregressive drafting (Li et al., 2024a) and blockwise parallel prediction (Stern et al., 2018). DFlash combines target-feature conditioning with block diffusion to predict draft tokens in one backbone pass (Chen et al., 2026). DSpark re-tains parallel backbone computation and adds lightweight sequential correction for adjacent-token dependencies (Cheng et al., 2026). We study how coordinated training and inference can turn this inexpensive block computation into longer accepted continuations. Low drafting latency alone does not ensure efficient use of a predicted block. With a single pro-posed continuation, the first rejection discards the remaining suffix regardless of its local prediction quality (Leviathan et al., 2023; Chen et al., 2023). For a parallel drafter, computation has already been spent on every block position, but later predictions contribute only when the required prefix remains available. DSpark models adjacent-token dependencies, but selecting one continuation still excludes alternatives represented in its distributions. Its correction head is trained on reference par-ent tokens and encounters model-generated parents at deployment (Cheng et al., 2026). Accurate reference predictions therefore need not yield useful candidates under the deployed policy. Improv-ing acceptance requires considering both the available continuations and the supervision that shapes them.

Tree verifica-tion evaluates continuations with shared prefixes in one target pass (Miao et al., 2024; Cai et al., 2024), with candidate allocation constrained by verification cost (Chen et al., 2024). In the semi-autoregressive architecture, shared backbone outputs support lightweight parent-dependent correc-tions. This extension creates a specific learning requirement: each depth-specific prediction must support several proposed parents, and only a subset of their children receives verification positions. Reference-token losses do not directly supervise this allocation. Useful alternatives may fall below local selection boundaries or lose their prefixes during pruning. Training must therefore account for both local prediction quality and the selection decisions that determine complete-path availability. Draft-generated data and online distillation improve alignment with inference-time continuations and query distributions (Zhou et al., 2024; Liu et al., 2024). HASS and EAGLE-3 expose training to simulated inference states (Zhang et al., 2025; Li et al., 2025), while LK losses optimize speculative acceptance directly (Samarin et al., 2026). Our focus is the additional alignment required when a semi-autoregressive drafter retains multiple continuations. The correction head must learn on pro-posed parents, reinforce retained paths, and promote useful alternatives under the deployed budget. Sharing the backbone and reusing reference target distributions make this feasible without repeated transformer computation or new target evaluations on every branch. We introduce TreeSpark, which integrates parent-conditioned candidate generation with training under the deployed policy (Figure 1). Training replays expansion, pruning, and packing with the current drafter while retaining reference distillation. Survival weights retained-edge likelihood by reference-derived path mass; capped coverage promotes target-important alternatives near local selection boundaries, including omitted candidates. Both losses update the shared backbone and Markov head on model-generated parents, with discrete selection held fixed. Reference target dis-tributions are reused across branches. Deployment follows the same candidate policy with greedy verification and one draft backbone pass per round. The deployment policy defines the states and selections used for supervision. Learning can therefore improve the candidates presented to a fixed verifier without introducing a separate scoring model at inference. The main contributions are: • A unified extension of semi-autoregressive drafting that retains alternative continuations with one backbone pass and trains on model-generated parent states by replaying the de-ployed expansion, pruning, and packing policy. • Complementary survival and coverage objectives that weight retained-edge likelihood by reference-derived path mass and promote useful alternatives near local selection bound-aries, reusing reference target distributions without additional target forward passes. • Evaluations against DFlash and DSpark on two Qwen3 targets, with mean single-request throughput gains of 15.1% to 25.0% over DSpark. Controls at fixed attention capacity and inference policy isolate the additional benefit of supervision. 

# 2 RELATED WORK 

Parallel and semi-autoregressive drafting. Blockwise parallel decoding predicts several posi-tions before validating their longest usable prefix (Stern et al., 2018). EAGLE instead drafts target features autoregressively (Li et al., 2024a). DFlash combines target-feature conditioning with a lightweight block diffusion drafter, reducing proposal generation to one backbone pass (Chen et al., 2026). DSpark augments this structure with sequential token correction and confidence-based veri-fication scheduling (Cheng et al., 2026). These methods motivate our focus on increasing accepted length while retaining parallel backbone computation. TreeSpark builds on the DSpark Markov-head architecture and couples the generation of alternative continuations with supervision on the parent states and candidate selections used at deployment. 

Training and inference alignment. DistillSpec studies draft-generated data and decoding-specific distillation (Zhou et al., 2024), while Online Speculative Decoding updates the drafter from verification feedback (Liu et al., 2024). HASS aligns training with speculative rollout (Zhang et al., 2025), EAGLE-3 simulates inference during training (Li et al., 2025), and LK losses directly op-timize speculative acceptance (Samarin et al., 2026). # 3 ALIGNING PARALLEL DRAFTING WITH VERIFICATION 

TreeSpark extends the DSpark parallel backbone and lightweight correction head through coordi-nated training and inference (Figure 1). Inference retains alternative continuations in a bounded tree, and training replays the same candidate policy on model-generated parent states. Survival su-pervises retained edges, while coverage addresses local candidate admission before packing. The joint objective updates the backbone and Markov head with discrete selection held fixed. We first define candidate generation, then describe its replay and supervision. 3.1 PARENT -C ONDITIONED TREE DRAFTING 

Let x denote the committed prefix, whose final token is the root anchor ρ, and let P denote the frozen target model. We instantiate the semi-autoregressive drafter with the DSpark backbone and Markov head, applying the correction separately to each frontier parent. A single backbone pass produces vocabulary logits ℓd(x) for each draft depth d ∈ { 1, . . . , B }. For a parent u carrying token 3Preprint

Rr and projection Wθ ∈ R|V|× r are distinct from the frozen backbone input embeddings and output projection. All parents at a given depth share the backbone prediction but receive token-dependent corrections. Branching therefore requires additional lightweight head evaluations without repeating the backbone. Tree construction uses a local branching factor k, frontier width k, and budget N of non-root nodes. Starting from the anchor, each frontier parent proposes its top-k children. Every child enters a candi-date pool and receives the product of draft conditional probabilities along its path as its score. The k

highest-scoring children at that depth form the next frontier. After B depths, global packing retains up to N highest-scoring nodes from the pool, preferring shallower nodes when scores tie. Frontier pruning bounds expansion cost, whereas global packing determines the input to target verification. The resulting tree contains every ancestor of each selected node: a child has no larger path probabil-ity than its parent, and the tie rule places the parent first. Tree attention then lets each node attend to the committed prefix and its own ancestor path. Greedy verification follows children that match the target argmax and emits a target-selected bonus token when the matching path ends. Under identi-cal per-path target evaluations and argmax tie rules, this procedure preserves autoregressive greedy output for any such tree. Appendix A gives the construction and correctness arguments. 3.2 TRAINING UNDER THE DEPLOYMENT POLICY 

Reference training provides anchor contexts, continuations y1: B , and aligned target distributions 

¯pd = P (· | x, y <d ). We retain the baseline reference losses and replay the tree policy on a subset of training anchors with matching B, k, and N . The replay uses the current draft parameters, so each correction head is evaluated on model-selected parent tokens. The selected subtree consequently reflects both the model state and the verification budget. Replay separates discrete selection from differentiable prediction. Token identities, frontier choices, packing decisions, and supervision weights are detached. The conditional probabilities and logits used in the losses remain differentiable through the backbone and correction head. Each subsequent update reconstructs the candidate trees. This yields a direct supervised update conditional on the current tree selection. All parents at depth d reuse ¯pd, obtained by applying softmax to the aligned reference target logits. These labels are exact for the reference context and serve as proxies on alternative branches, avoiding additional target evaluations. Replay therefore matches the parent-conditioned expansion and budget policy while retaining reference-based target supervision. The complete objective augments reference distillation: 

L = Lbase + λsLsurv + λcLcov . (4) The baseline combines reference-token cross-entropy, distributional L1 matching, and the inherited confidence loss. These terms retain supervision beyond the currently selected branches. The confi-dence head is auxiliary to training and does not control the fixed tree budget. We also record mass coverage and reference argmax path survival on the packed tree to inspect how its candidates change during training; their definitions are provided in Appendix C.4. Our configuration uses B = 7 , k = 4 , and N = 32 , giving up to 100 candidates before packing and at most 33 target input positions including the anchor. Replay uses up to 128 anchors per sequence, with checkpointing to control activation memory. Inference batches frontier expansion and verifica-tion across requests, retaining one draft backbone pass followed by B lightweight correction stages. The tree objectives add no target evaluations during training or model components at inference. Tree replay requires a shared backbone prediction to support several parent-conditioned branch deci-sions. To increase the representational capacity available for learning these decisions, we widen the draft attention head dimension from 128 to 192 while retaining the layer count and tree policy. This expands the backbone attention projections; the Markov correction rank remains unchanged. Sec-tion 4.4 evaluates this capacity choice separately from the two objectives, and Appendix D specifies its computational implications.

# 5 CONCLUSION 

TreeSpark demonstrates how a deployment policy can guide the training of a parallel drafter. Train-ing on model-generated parent states under the deployed selection policy directs reference super-vision toward the continuations available for target verification. The controlled comparison yields longer mean accepted paths and higher mean throughput at fixed attention capacity and inference policy. This evidence supports treating candidate admission and prefix retention as integral to draft learning, alongside reference prediction accuracy. The resulting extension of DFlash and DSpark preserves shared backbone computation while improving how its predictions are used. These find-9Preprint ings motivate draft objectives that account for the states and selection constraints encountered during candidate generation and verification.

Proposition 1 (Prefix closure) . Suppose C contains every non-root ancestor of each of its nodes. Any initial segment of the score ordering above, together with ρ, is prefix closed. Proof. A conditional log probability is nonpositive, so Equation 5 implies s(v) ≤ s(pa( v)) . Astrictly higher parent score places the parent before its child. Equal scores also place the parent first because its depth is smaller. Applying this argument recursively shows that every ancestor precedes the node. Thus, selecting an initial segment cannot retain a node without its ancestors. 13 Preprint Prefix closure makes the global budget operationally useful: every selected node has a complete path available for target evaluation, and no extra ancestor positions need to be inserted after packing. The result depends on the ordering rule, not on the accuracy of draft probabilities. Appendix D.4 describes finite precision differences between the training and serving selection operators. A.2 ATTENTION CONTEXTS AND VERIFICATION 

Write the committed prefix as x = x<t xt, where xt is the anchor. The target retains the cache for x<t and processes the anchor together with the packed non-root nodes. The attention mask for node u exposes x<t and the root-to-u path, including u itself. Siblings and unrelated branches are masked. A node at depth d uses absolute position t + d, independently of its packed index. These choices give each node the causal context of its corresponding sequential continuation. Verification begins at the root. The output logits at the current node predict the next token. If the target argmax occurs among its children, that token is accepted and verification continues at the matching child. Otherwise, the target argmax is emitted as a bonus token and the round ends. A leaf likewise supplies the next target token as the bonus. End-of-sequence and output-length constraints can terminate the process earlier. 

Proposition 2 (Greedy output preservation) . Assume that tree-masked and sequential target eval-uations agree on identical token paths and use the same argmax tie rule. For any prefix-closed candidate tree, the verifier emits the same tokens as autoregressive greedy decoding with P .Proof. At the root, both procedures evaluate the same committed prefix and select the same next token. If the tree contains that token as a child, accepting it extends the prefix identically. The child attention context then agrees with sequential decoding on the extended prefix. Induction gives agreement at every accepted depth. At the first missing child, or at a leaf, the bonus token is selected from target logits on the same prefix and therefore also agrees. Applying the argument after every round establishes equality of the generated sequence. Target evaluation determines every committed token, while draft learning affects how many can be committed together. The guarantee therefore depends on per-path target evaluations and argmax tie rules, independently of the supervision used to train the drafter. In finite precision, different attention kernels can affect nearly tied logits, so the stated numerical assumption remains relevant. The evaluated procedure uses greedy decoding. A.3 ACCEPTANCE LENGTH CONVENTION 

Let Ag denote the number of accepted non-root draft nodes. An ordinary completed round commits 

Ag + 1 new tokens, including the target bonus. The implementation reports this committed length as acceptance length. A round stopped by an accepted end token or an output limit can omit the bonus. The anchor is already committed and is excluded. Training diagnostics at depth d describe draft-path survival and should therefore be distinguished from a committed-length metric that includes the bonus.

# D IMPLEMENTATION AND COMPUTATIONAL COST 

D.1 CONFIGURATION AND CAPACITY 

The reference configuration uses B = 7 , k = 4 , and N = 32 non-root nodes. A full seven-depth expansion produces 4 + 6 · 16 = 100 candidates before packing. A full target input contains the root anchor and 32 selected nodes, for 33 positions. The maximum ordinary committed length is eight tokens, comprising seven accepted draft tokens and one target bonus. This budget convention counts the anchor separately. The correction rank is r = 256 . Coverage uses J = 4 , margin δ = 1 , cap c = 4 , and depth-decay scale γ = 4 . Baseline coefficients are λCE = 0 .1, λ1 = 0 .9, and λconf = 1 ; the two tree coefficients are λs = λc = 0 .1. Up to 128 tree anchors are selected from 512 backbone anchor blocks per sequence. Training records its tree hyperparameters, and inference receives the corresponding branching and packing arguments explicitly. Deployment with the intended training policy requires matching those values. The complete systems for Qwen3-4B and Qwen3-8B use draft attention head dimension 192, com-pared with 128 in the inherited configuration. The motivation is to provide a larger attention repre-sentation for the shared contextual prediction that must support multiple parent-conditioned distribu-tions during tree replay. At fixed attention head counts, widening each head increases the dimensions of the query, key, and value projections and the input to the output projection. The residual hidden size, layer count, Markov rank r = 256 , and tree budget are unchanged. The capacity increase is therefore localized to the draft attention computation and is used during both training and inference. Attention normalization, rotary embeddings, and scaling follow the selected head dimension. The affected projection sizes and draft KV storage scale by 192 /128 = 1 .5; this factor does not describe the parameter count or memory footprint of the entire model. DSpark + Tree and each single-loss ablation retain dimension 192. The 128-dimensional variant retains both objectives and the candidate policy, allowing its throughput comparison to assess the capacity choice separately from the supervision. D.2 REPLAY COST AND ACTIVATION MEMORY 

The backbone outputs are shared across all parents at an anchor and depth. Following one backbone evaluation, replay computes at most k Markov corrections per anchor and depth. Their rank-r

vocabulary projection costs O(kr |V| ); it does not repeat transformer computation for each branch. Tree metadata requires O(Atree Bk 2) entries per sequence, while vocabulary logits are the dominant intermediate tensors. Anchor subsampling limits the number of tree states used in an update. Checkpointing reconstructs each depth of parent-conditioned logits during backward, reducing the need to retain vocabulary-18 Preprint sized activations. Target distributions are processed by depth. The detached pool stores token iden-tities, parent pointers, path scores, reference masses, and packing masks. These choices preserve the main source of parallel drafting efficiency: a shared backbone supplies the block, and a lightweight head supplies branch dependence. Tree supervision still adds draft computation and backward work. Its specific target-side advantage is that it reuses the teacher information already needed by reference distillation. D.3 BATCHED CONSTRUCTION AND VERIFICATION 

The first frontier has one parent per request and later frontiers have up to k. Compatible frontier shapes allow active requests to share each depth-wise correction and selection call. There remain 

B sequential head stages, but the implementation avoids a separate host expansion loop for every request. Local top-k identities can be selected from raw logits. Joint path comparison additionally requires the row normalization: 

log qi = zi − logsumexp j∈V zj . (18) Computing this value for selected tokens avoids materializing a normalized vocabulary tensor solely to retain a few entries. The normalizer itself is essential because candidates from different parents compete by joint probability. Parent pointers are converted on device into ancestor masks and path retrieval indices. The target verifies the packed trees in a batch, and accepted paths are retrieved per request. Cache management retains valid states associated with committed paths and invalidates rejected positions using per-request masks. Compaction is deferred until needed, allowing requests to commit different numbers of tokens without imposing a common accepted depth. D.4 NUMERICAL SELECTION CONVENTIONS 

Training and serving use the same expansion, frontier pruning, and node-budget rules. The serv-ing reference additionally prefers smaller depth on tied joint scores, as specified in Appendix A.1. Training uses floating point top-N without this depth preference. The packed sets therefore coin-cide for matching scores without boundary ties; exact agreement at ties requires a common tie rule. Proposition 1 assumes the stated depth-aware ordering. The optimized serving path performs local selection, subtraction, and exponentiation in the input precision, with float32 accumulation for the normalizer. Scores can consequently differ from full float32 log-softmax near a selection boundary. These operator-level differences concern the selected candidates. Greedy output preservation follows from the target verification conditions in Proposi-tion 2.

E.2 METRIC DEFINITIONS AND AGGREGATION 

Acceptance. For each dataset, acceptance length is the total number of newly committed tokens divided by the number of verification rounds. The anchor is excluded. The target bonus is included when emitted, while termination by an accepted end token or an output limit can shorten the final round. The eight-position block convention therefore corresponds to seven draft depths and at most eight newly committed tokens in an ordinary round. A tree budget of 32 counts non-root candidates; its target input has up to 33 positions including the anchor. The position-wise metric at draft position j divides the number of rounds that accept at least j + 1 

draft tokens by the number that propose that position. It is cumulative over accepted depth. The left panel of Figure 2 averages recorded rates at C = 12 across the nine general benchmarks; shading shows their range across tasks. These rates and aggregate acceptance lengths come from separate evaluation records. The right panel therefore uses the directly recorded lengths in Table 2, including their termination behavior. Figure 3 uses the same means of 4.23 for DSpark and 5.25 for TreeSpark, together with the suite-level DSpark + Tree aggregate of 4.93. Benchmark-level lengths and position-wise rates are not reported for this control. Acceptance length is measured in tokens per verification round, separately from throughput in tokens per second. 

Serving throughput. Single-request throughput divides total output tokens by accumulated de-coding time. The concurrency experiment uses a closed loop that admits a new request whenever a slot becomes free. System throughput divides all generated tokens by the elapsed workload time. For multiple cards, output counts are summed across workers and elapsed time is the maximum worker duration. At total concurrency 36, three workers each maintain up to 12 active requests. Each worker hosts its own model replica; the comparison measures aggregate serving throughput across these replicas. Per-request throughput is computed from each request after its first output token: subsequent output tokens are divided by the interval from first-token emission to completion, and the resulting rates are averaged across requests. It excludes time to first token and need not equal system through-put divided by nominal concurrency. Prefill, the filling and draining of active slots, and variable completion times affect the two measurements differently. 

Averages and relative gains. Within each target and workload group, Mean is the unweighted arithmetic mean of the benchmark metrics. In throughput tables, the Mean entry in a ∆ row averages the relative improvement on each benchmark. In Tables 2 and 6, the corresponding entry instead compares the two mean acceptance lengths. Figure 3 displays arithmetic mean absolute metrics. Gains between tree configurations compare these means: this convention applies to the 4.2% and 5.0% throughput gains over DSpark + Tree and the 2.0% system and 2.1% per-request gains from increasing head dimension. The approximate 6.4% acceptance gain uses the unrounded TreeSpark mean and the available DSpark + Tree aggregate of 4.93. Other aggregates are calculated from benchmark-level observations before rounding for presentation. Throughput values are averaged over three repeated measurements before benchmark aggregation.
