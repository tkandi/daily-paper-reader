ABSTRACT 

Sparse GNN training reduces computation, but deciding which edges to keep can be costly. Reusing one sparse graph is cheap but locks training to a fixed topol-ogy, while varying it across epochs can require repeated sampling or recompu-tation. We introduce EDiS (Edge-Di sjoint Subgraph sparsification framework), which separates one-time structural extraction from per-epoch graph composi-tion . EDiS decomposes the graph once into cacheable edge-disjoint subgraphs, then recombines them into graphs with edge-budget constraints across epochs and retention ratios without re-extracting structure. Our default construction uses feature-based scores and successive maximum-score covering forests, while the same composition mechanism also supports al-ternative edge-selection rules. We provide a combinatorial analysis of the per-epoch sampler, the composition step that draws a training graph from the cached decomposition. We show that, under the default covering-forest selector, the stored decomposition deterministically preserves high-score cut edges, and we derive a selector-agnostic conditional bound on high-score cut survival in com-posed training graphs. Across 19 homophilic, heterophilic, and large-scale node-classification benchmarks against 17 baselines under the same edge-budget, EDiS achieves the highest mean benchmark score (accuracy/ROC-AUC) and the lowest average rank and gap-to-best among ranked methods. Ablations show the clearest benefits of structural decomposition and epoch variation at tight edge-budgets. 

1 INTRODUCTION 

Graph neural networks (GNNs) propagate information along the edges of the graph (Kipf & Welling, 2017), which determines the amount of information computed, exchanged, and stored at each epoch during training of the GNN. Sparsifying the graph, by selectively removing edges, can therefore reduce the computational costs (Chen et al., 2021), but adds a different cost of deciding which edges to keep (or remove). In some sampling-based methods, this cost dominates training itself. For example, on Reddit, a graph with 233K nodes and 11.6M edges, the sampling-based method AS-GCN (Huang et al., 2018) spends 20.7 seconds per epoch for sampling neighbors, versus 3.5 seconds for the forward and backward pass (Zeng et al., 2020). Thus, a sparsified graph does not guarantee economical training if the graph must be rebuilt frequently. One option is to build a sparse graph once and reuse it every epoch to amortize the cost of sparsifi-cation. However, this also locks the GNN into the same set of edges for the entire training run (Chen et al., 2021; Seo et al., 2024). Another option is to change the sparse graph over time using different approaches such as repeated subgraph sampling in GraphSAINT (Zeng et al., 2020), incrementally grown spanning subgraph in SpanGNN (Gu et al., 2024), or computing a fresh random spanning tree at each epoch in GERN (Bonchi et al., 2025). While these methods show the value of varying sparse views during training, each couples graph variation to a particular sampling or structural-update mechanism at a fixed sparsity level. To the best of our knowledge, existing methods do not formulate the problem as a persistent, edge-disjoint structural library that is built once and efficiently recombined into different sparse graphs during training. This leads to an important question: Can we extract graph structure once and reuse it to cheaply generate many different training graphs with specific edge-budget constraints?

EDiS (Edge-Di sjoint Subgraph sparsification framework) addresses this question by turning edge-disjointness from a one-off computational step into a cacheable training paradigm . It decomposes the original graph into persistent and non-overlapping structural subgraphs only once. It then re-peatedly recombines them into training graphs with edge-budget constraints across epochs and re-tention ratios, without recomputing the decomposition. In our default setup, subgraphs are succes-sive connectivity-preserving sparse backbones, extracted from residual edges and cached before the GNN training begins. While GNN architecture, parameters, and training objectives remain the same, the sparse message-passing graph changes across epochs. For example, a 30% edge budget does not mean the model is stuck with the same 30% of edges for the entire run, but that it processes 30% of edges per epoch, while different epochs are exposed to different parts of the original graph (Figure 1). EDiS has three main components: edge scoring , structural extraction , and budgeted composition . While the first two are a one-time computation, budgeted composition is re-peated at every epoch with specific edge-budgets. Edge scores rank connections, a residual selector groups them into cacheable subgraphs, and composition selects a train-ing graph from those subgraphs. The subgraphs in extrac-tion are disjoint, but edges can be repeated over time in the composed graphs ( G(t) 

> B

). Our default algorithm pairs feature-based cosine scores with successive maximum-score covering forests. The first forest provides a high-score, connectivity-preserving backbone of the original graph, and once these edges are removed, later forests span the connected components of the residual graph using previ-ously unused edges. During training, the stored subgraphs are sampled with score-dependent priority and combined until the target budget is reached, trimming excess edges and filling any shortfall from the residual pool (detailed in Section 3). 

EDiS is not tied to one scoring or selection function: edge scoring and residual selection functions are modular, so any combination shares the same edge-budget-constrained procedure (Section 3). Separating extraction from composition also clarifies what we can guarantee: for maximum-score residual forests, stored prefixes preserve score-threshold cut certificates, and since a single epoch’s graph may omit or trim these forests, we separately characterize conditional edge retention and fixed-cut survival under composition for any residual selector. Matched-budget ablations provide empirical evidence that structural grouping and epoch resampling contribute to predictive perfor-mance (Section 4.3). We evaluate EDiS against 17 baselines on 19 datasets spanning homophilic, heterophilic, and large-scale graphs, separating sparse-graph inference ( EDiS -Lite) from full-graph inference ( EDiS -Full), while isolating structural decomposition, epoch resampling, selector choice, and edge scoring through matched-edge-budget ablations. At 30% retention, EDiS -Lite achieves the highest mean benchmark score among ranked methods (average rank 2.53 , gap 1.25 points), while EDiS -Full serves as a reference (Section 4). Cacheable subgraphs and graph variation give the largest gains at tight edge-budgets, while fixed graphs or simpler sampling remain competitive elsewhere. Our contributions are as follows. • A cacheable, edge-budget-agnostic graph decomposition: EDiS extracts structural subgraphs once, then recomposes them into training graphs with edge-budget constraints across epochs and sparsity levels, without rerunning extraction or changing the GNN architecture or loss. • A novel, selector-agnostic composition procedure: To the best of our knowledge, EDiS is the first method to separate extraction from composition: composition is independent of the extraction methods that build the subgraphs (e.g., maximum spanning forests or random selection). • A combinatorial analysis of the sampler: For maximum-score residual forests, we prove score-aware cut preservation for the stored decomposition; for any residual selector, we characterize conditional retention and high-score cut survival in the composed graph. • A controlled empirical study of structural reuse: Matched-edge-budget experiments across 17 baselines and 19 datasets test whether a cacheable structural decomposition and epoch variation justify their construction cost. 2EDiS 

2 RELATED WORK 

Existing methods either decide which part of the graph to use every step, or decide once and fix the graph beforehand via sparsification. EDiS sits between these two settings, deciding once but still varying the graph every epoch. It builds a cacheable edge-disjoint structural library once, then composes an exact-budget graph at each epoch. This section relates EDiS to four lines of work. 

Sampling-based GNN training. Neighbor sampling bounds neighbors per node (Hamilton et al., 2017). Layer-wise sampling instead scores whole node layers (Chen et al., 2018), and LADIES conditions each layer on the layer above it to stay connected (Zou et al., 2019). Subgraph methods sample subgraphs directly (Chiang et al., 2019) or correct the resulting bias (Zeng et al., 2020), and attribute-guided sampling prioritizes edges by similarity and diversity (Das et al., 2024). All of these resample the full topology at every step, rather than keeping an altered one. Other work avoids resampling by caching past embeddings (Fey et al., 2021), quantizing or compressing activations (Ding et al., 2021; Liu et al., 2022), repartitioning across workers (Wan et al., 2022), or compensating for missing cross-partition messages (Shi et al., 2023). EDiS instead organizes the topology once and reuses precomputed subgraphs under an explicit edge-budget, rather than resampling every step. 

Graph sparsification. A large family of methods target edge removal before training begins, which includes degree- and similarity-based heuristics (Hamann et al., 2016; Satuluri et al., 2011; Liu et al., 2023), spectral and distance-preserving sparsifiers (Spielman & Srivastava, 2008; Peleg & Schäffer, 1989), and cut sparsifiers built by repeatedly extracting a maximum spanning forest (Benczúr & Karger, 1996; Forster & de Vos, 2023). In contrast, some methods learn which edges to retain jointly with graph learning (Chen et al., 2021; Seo et al., 2024; Zhang et al., 2024; Das et al., 2026; Zhang et al., 2025). However, no single method effectively preserves multiple graph properties of interest well (Chen et al., 2023). EDiS employs similar sparsification principles, such as degree-, neighbor/similarity-, spanner-, and forest-based residual selection, and its composition step treats them identically regardless of the method used to build the library. 

Stochastic topology variation. DropEdge (Rong et al., 2020) and dropout-induced subgraphs (Luo et al., 2025) vary the topology by removing edges independently at random at each epoch. Other methods instead learn edge predictors (Chen et al., 2025) or stabilize message passing under a changing structure (Lee et al., 2025). These structures overlap substantially across epochs and carry no edge-budget or structural guarantee. By contrast, EDiS ’s epoch-to-epoch variation comes from a controlled, cacheable partition rather than independent per-edge noise. 

Cacheable edge-disjoint decomposition. A few methods train over a sequence of sparse struc-tures. For example, SpanGNN (Gu et al., 2024) trains on grown subgraphs and GERN (Bonchi et al., 2025) trains on random spanning trees, but neither is persistent. Prior edge-disjoint methods use disjoint structures as sampled or sequential objects, not as a persistent, budget-agnostic decom-position recomposed into exact-budget graphs across epochs and retention ratios. To the best of our knowledge, EDiS is the first approach to decouple one-time extraction from per-epoch composition, so it stays cached and reused throughout training. Appendix B (Table 4) compares these methods by budget, reusability, exposure, and disjointness. Appendix J adds quantitative comparisons against SpanGNN and GERN, and discusses our attempted AGS-GNN evaluation. 

3 ED IS: A S TRUCTURED SPARSIFICATION FRAMEWORK 

Sparse GNN training poses a tradeoff between computational cost and graph coverage. While reusing a sparse graph built once for the entire training is cheap, it significantly limits learning by fixing the graph structure for every epoch. Thus, changing the sparse graph over time exposes the GNN to more of the original topology. However, rebuilding from scratch at every epoch can make sparsification as expensive as the training it was meant to speed up. 

EDiS addresses this tradeoff using two main components: Decomposition , a one-time step that builds the cached structural library; and Composition , a per-epoch step that composes a graph from the library. We extract structural information once, organize it into cacheable edge-disjoint sub-graphs, and recombine them into an exact-budget graph each epoch. 5 CONCLUSION 

We introduced EDiS , a framework that separates one-time structural extraction from per-epoch graph composition. We extract structure once, then compose many sparse graphs from it for train-ing, avoiding both a fixed topology and repeated extraction. The same cached library supplies exact-budget graphs at every epoch and retention level without changing the GNN architecture or objec-tive. Our default MaxCF construction is just one instance of this framework, which stays modular across scores and selectors. Across 19 node-classification benchmarks under the same edge-budget, EDiS-Lite achieves the highest mean benchmark score among the ranked methods at both retention levels. EDiS-Full, reported as a reference using full-graph for inference, shows how much accuracy can be recovered when sparsification is restricted to training, and at a modest extra cost for inference. Structural decomposition and epoch-to-epoch variation matter most at tighter edge-budgets. More broadly, a cacheable structural representation that generates many sparse views can be more effective than searching for a single sparse graph to replace the original. Our future work will consider task-aware scores for decomposition, dynamic (temporal) composition, and extensions to graph- and link-level prediction. 10 EDiS

B EXTENDED RELATED WORK 

Sampling-based GNN training. Many GNN methods reduce training cost by processing only part of the graph at a time rather than constructing a new topology. Neighbor sampling selects a limited number of neighbors per node at every GNN layer (Hamilton et al., 2017). Layer-wise sampling instead selects a fixed number of nodes by importance (Chen et al., 2018). LADIES extends this idea by picking each layer’s nodes based on which nodes were kept in the layer above it, so the sampled nodes stay connected from one layer to the next (Zou et al., 2019). Other methods divide the graph into clusters and train on these smaller parts (Chiang et al., 2019), or sample training subgraphs and pass messages only within them (Zeng et al., 2020). AGS-GNN (Das et al., 2024) guides node and subgraph sampling by feature similarity and diversity, and reappears below as an edge-disjoint variant. Related approaches reuse historical node representations (Fey et al., 2021), quantize activations (Ding et al., 2021) or compress them further (Liu et al., 2022), or repartition the graph across workers (Wan et al., 2022) or compensate for missing cross-partition messages (Shi et al., 2023). These methods reduce how much of the graph is processed at once, but the resulting structures are repeatedly resampled from the original topology. EDiS instead organizes the topology itself and controls which precomputed subgraphs are used under a specified edge-retention budget. 

Graph sparsification and topology modification. A second group of methods changes which edges are used for message passing. Sparsification methods remove edges while preserving struc-tural or task-relevant information. Classical topology-based approaches include uniform random removal, Local Degree (Hamann et al., 2016), Rank Degree (Voudigari et al., 2016), Forest Fire (Leskovec & Faloutsos, 2006), and SCAN (Xu et al., 2007). Similarity-based approaches include L-Spar, G-Spar, and L-Sim, which retain edges using neighborhood similarity (Satuluri et al., 2011). Other structural approaches include spectral sparsification via effective resistance (Spielman & Sri-vastava, 2008), t-spanners (Peleg & Schäffer, 1989), and degree-based DSpar (Liu et al., 2023). A classical line of work builds cut sparsifiers by repeatedly extracting a maximum spanning forest from what is left of the graph, which gives provable guarantees on how well cut sizes are pre-served (Benczúr & Karger, 1996; Forster & de Vos, 2023). EDiS reuses this same repeated-forest-extraction idea, but keeps each extracted forest as a separate, cacheable training subgraph instead of merging them into one fixed sparsifier. A separate line learns or adapts which elements to retain. UGS (Chen et al., 2021), from the Uni-fied Lottery Ticket framework, prunes edges and model parameters together over repeated training rounds. TEDDY (Seo et al., 2024) instead removes edges in a single pass using a degree-based rule, avoiding UGS’s repeated pruning cycles. AdaGLT (Zhang et al., 2024) adds adaptive, automated sparsification. SGS-GNN (Das et al., 2026) learns which edges to keep using supervision and lets the user directly set the target sparsity. MoG (Zhang et al., 2025) combines sparsification experts with different pruning criteria and sparsity levels into task-adaptive graphs. Different sparsification methods preserve different graph properties, and no single method preserves all of them well (Chen et al., 2023). Removing edges therefore affects not only training cost but also how information propagates. Sparsification has also been used against oversmoothing and oversquashing, via spectrum-preserving pruning (Liang et al., 2025; Jamadandi et al., 2024) or a truss-based approach that removes edges outside dense subgraphs (Hossain et al., 2024). These methods generally focus on identifying or adapting a useful sparse topology for message passing, rather than on how that topology is exposed to the GNN across training steps. A related line changes the topology stochastically during training. DropEdge removes a random subset of edges at each iteration (Rong et al., 2020). Even feature dropout, which does not explic-itly remove edges, induces different stochastic subgraphs across feature dimensions. A theoretical analysis attributes part of dropout’s ability to mitigate oversmoothing in GCNs to this variation (Luo et al., 2025). Other methods replace random removal with learned edge predictors (Chen et al., 2025), or add parameters to stabilize message passing under changing structures (Lee et al., 2025). Structures generated by DropEdge or dropout-induced subgraphs at different training steps can sub-stantially overlap in the edges they use. EDiS instead decomposes the graph once and reuses the resulting non-overlapping subgraphs, composing them under an explicit edge-retention budget that lets the message-passing topology vary while directly controlling the number of retained edges. 18 EDiS 

Cacheable edge-disjoint decomposition. A smaller body of work trains GNNs using multiple graph structures rather than one sparse topology. The AGS-GNN sampling framework (Das et al., 2024) introduced above also includes AGS-GS-Disjoint, which sequentially constructs sparse sub-graphs with different heuristics, removing selected edges before constructing the next. Our objective still differs, and so does the intent behind the construction itself. AGS-GS-Disjoint’s edge-disjoint subgraphs feed a dual-channel sampler that uses submodular function maximization to select similar and diverse neighborhoods, adapting to homophily and heterophily. Its per-epoch sample draws a fixed number of these cached subgraphs padded with random residual edges, with no exact-edge-count guarantee or attached structural theory. EDiS instead studies edge-disjoint decomposition as a general sparsification primitive. Any residual selector composes into an exact-

B-edge training graph, with a proven cut-preservation guarantee for the stored decomposition and a retention guarantee for the composed graph that holds regardless of selector. This lets us directly compare organizing a fixed number of retained edges into non-overlapping subgraphs against alter-native ways of using the same budget, rather than optimizing for informative neighborhoods. SpanGNN (Gu et al., 2024) trains using spanning subgraphs to reduce memory, and MoG (Zhang et al., 2025) combines sparsification experts to produce adaptive sparse graphs. GERN (Bonchi et al., 2025) also exposes a GNN to multiple sparse structures, progressively training over a sequence of random spanning trees transformed into path graphs. Unlike EDiS , it does not build a persistent edge-disjoint repository, progressing instead through independently generated sparse path structures toward efficient training rather than budget-controlled composition of a fixed repository. To our knowledge, none of them partitions the original edge set into a persistent, mutually edge-disjoint collection that is later recombined under a retained-edge constraint. These approaches confirm that exposing a GNN to multiple sparse structures can help, though each still addresses a different question from EDiS , namely how to allocate a fixed retained-edge budget across a cacheable collection of non-overlapping subgraphs. The graph is decomposed once, unused edges remain explicitly accounted for in a residual subgraph, and the subgraphs are recomposed under the same edge-budget constraint at every epoch, so different training-graph constructions can be compared under the same retained-edge count. A cut’s crossing edges are the only channel through which one layer of message passing can move information between its two sides, so the results below characterize threshold-qualified crossings in stored maximum-score forest prefixes and their conditional retention under composition. F.1 FAMILY OF RESIDUAL SELECTORS 

Each of the seven selectors below implements the same residual-selector interface F from a residual graph H and its fixed edge scores s, so any one of them can be dropped into EDiS without changing anything else in the pipeline. What differs is only how each one decides which edges to keep, and consequently its round cost Ti(n, m i), explained selector by selector below and summarized in Table 5 at the end of this appendix. 

Maximum/minimum covering forest ( maxst /minst , MaxCF/MinCF in Section 4). The im-plementation names maxst /minst reflect that each round’s forest spans every component of the current residual graph. We call it a covering forest instead because that spanning property holds only within one round, not for the budget-limited graph actually composed for training (Section 3.4). This selector puts every remaining edge in order by score, either strongest first or weakest first, and adds edges one at a time down that list, skipping any edge that would close a loop back to nodes already connected. This is Kruskal’s algorithm (Kruskal, 1956; Edmonds, 1971) run on our scores instead of raw edge weights, with ties kept in their original order. Because it only adds loop-free edges, it ends up touching every part of the residual graph. maxst and minst differ only in which direction we sort. Each round sorts the remaining edges and tracks which nodes are already connected, costing a little more than one plain scan. 

k-nearest neighbor ( knn ). This selector lets every node claim its k best-scoring remaining edges, or all of them if fewer than k are left, and keeps the union of every node’s claims. Asking for a fixed total M instead of a fixed k trims or tops up that union by score until it hits M exactly, which can take away an edge a node had already claimed, so no node is guaranteed to keep its own top choices. This touches each remaining edge from both ends. 

Graph spanner ( spanner ). This selector works on each disconnected piece of the residual graph on its own, building a smaller subgraph using edge lengths that shrink as the score grows, so the best-scoring edges look shortest. The result must keep every pair of nodes within a stretch factor α of their true distance in these lengths (Peleg & Schäffer, 1989), a bound on path length rather than hop count. Construction is randomized, and its cost depends on the spanner routine used. 

Degree-normalized ( degree_norm ). This selector draws a set number M of edges at random without repeats, weighting each edge uv by wuv = 1 /d H (u) + 1 /d H (v) so edges touching low-degree nodes are more likely to be picked, recomputing degrees every round. It ignores the score entirely and guarantees neither connectivity nor a minimum degree. One pass over the edges is enough to compute the weights. 

Random ( random ). This selector shuffles the remaining edges uniformly at random and keeps the first M . Ignoring both score and structure, it serves as the score-free baseline the others are compared against, and needs only one pass over the edges. 

Scheduled (Hybrid). A schedule chains several selectors together, using a different one each round on whatever edges remain, and repeating the last one once the list runs out. Any of the five selectors above can appear, in any order, and can repeat, so this is a general way to combine selectors rather than one fixed recipe. The schedule used in our experiments runs maxst , then knn ,then degree_norm for every later round, first extracting a connectivity-preserving backbone, then giving each node its own best local edges, then spreading coverage to whichever nodes are still thin. It stops under the same conditions as the other selectors. 

Selectors outside this family. Effective-resistance sampling (Spielman & Srivastava, 2008) needs spectral information recomputed as the residual changes. Forest-fire sampling (Leskovec & Falout-sos, 2006), SCAN (Xu et al., 2007), and rank-degree sampling (Voudigari et al., 2016) likewise need traversal, clustering, or ranking steps beyond a residual degree pass. None of these are implemented here as candidate selectors inside EDiS ’s own construction, though several are compared against

G.2 BASELINE SETTINGS 

We compare EDiS against baselines drawn from three families, grouped by the structural signal each relies on rather than by implementation detail. Anchors bound the comparison without adapting to the graph, and comprise a feature-only MLP (No Graph), a Spanning Tree, and Full Graph with the same tuned GNN configuration (Luo et al., 2024). Semantic baselines learn which edges to keep jointly with the GNN parameters, and comprise Unified-LTH (Chen et al., 2021), AdaGLT (Zhang et al., 2024), and MoG (Zhang et al., 2025). Topological baselines score edges directly from the graph, independent of the downstream task, and comprise Random, Local Degree (Hamann et al., 2016), Rank Degree (Voudigari et al., 2016), Forest Fire (Leskovec & Faloutsos, 2006), SCAN (Xu et al., 2007), L-Spar and G-Spar (Satuluri et al., 2011), L-Sim (Chen et al., 2023), Spectral (ER) (Spielman & Srivastava, 2008), t-Spanner (Peleg & Schäffer, 1989), and DSpar (Liu et al., 2023). 

Sampling-based related methods. Neighborhood- and subgraph-sampling methods such as GraphSAGE (Hamilton et al., 2017), GraphSAINT (Zeng et al., 2020), and Cluster-GCN (Chiang et al., 2019) control a different resource, the minibatch or neighborhood size used for scalability, rather than a global edge-retention budget. They are therefore not part of the matched-edge-budget comparison, and GraphSAGE appears only as the alternate backbone in RQ7. G.3 PROPAGATION AND EVALUATION SETTINGS

40 EDiS I.5 EXTENDED BACKBONE COMPARISON 

EDiS is a framework that works with any backbone, not just GCN or GraphSAGE, and neither backbone dominates across this panel. How well EDiS performs on a given dataset depends more on how expressive that backbone is for the dataset, a property of the backbone’s own architecture, than on the sparsification procedure itself. EDiS simply plugs into whichever backbone a task calls for and inherits its expressivity, rather than being tied to one. The largest gap between backbones appears on Squirrel, a few points at most, while most of the panel differs by a point or less, so the two backbones are close substitutes almost everywhere despite that occasional larger split. The stronger backbone does not track homophily in any simple way. GCN wins on some of the most heterophilic datasets in the panel (Squirrel, Chameleon) and some of the most homophilic (Cora, Coauthor-Physics) alike, so backbone choice reflects the architecture-dataset match itself rather than a graph statistic we could read off in advance. J.1 COMPARISON WITH SPAN GNN AND GERN Table 4 (Appendix B) compares EDiS against AGS-GNN (Das et al., 2024), SpanGNN (Gu et al., 2024), and GERN (Bonchi et al., 2025) qualitatively, by budget control, reusability, multi-structure exposure, and edge-disjointness. Here we compare SpanGNN and GERN quantitatively and discuss our attempted AGS-GNN evaluation. 

AGS-GNN (Das et al., 2024) samples edges by a combination of attribute similarity and diversity, selecting diverse yet locally similar neighborhoods through greedy submodular function maximiza-tion. Its sampling objective differs from EDiS ’s score-based composition, and its published version provides no analysis of edge-disjoint or reusable structural properties. We attempted to include AGS-GNN under the same matched-budget protocol as our other 17 baselines, but its submodular diversity step was extremely slow and failed to complete on our evaluation hardware. AGS-GNN’s own paper notes that this precomputation is embarrassingly parallel and relies on scaling up the number of worker threads to run in practice, a requirement outside the modest budget we allocate uniformly across all 19 datasets and three retention ratios. We omit AGS-GNN from Table 26. 

SpanGNN (Gu et al., 2024) instead grows a single spanning subgraph incrementally across epochs to reduce memory during training, rather than composing a fresh sparse graph from reusable struc-ture each epoch. We adapt SpanGNN to our training framework and compare it against EDiS under matched training conditions. 

GERN (Bonchi et al., 2025) instead trains a GNN over a sequence of random spanning trees, each converted into a path graph and used as that epoch’s sparse training structure, so it exposes the model to many cheap, independently resampled sparse views rather than a persistent, budget-controlled decomposition. We adapt GERN to our training framework, running it with the same tuned GCN backbone, optimizer, training protocol, and dataset splits as EDiS , isolating the sparsification and composition mechanism from incidental training differences. Both methods are evaluated under full-graph inference , so this table reads against EDiS-Full ,not EDiS-Lite, on the five datasets with SpanGNN and GERN results. Both reuse a sequence of sparse structures across epochs, as EDiS does, but neither separates one-time decomposition from per-epoch composition (Section 2).

Our experiments focus on node classification, so the benefits of reusable edge-disjoint decomposi-tions for graph- and link-level prediction remain to be established. Our default edge score, residual selector, and extraction-depth cap all come from an empirical sweep rather than a principled per-dataset selection rule, and no single choice dominates across datasets, so automatic tuning of any of the three remains open. The composed graph is not guaranteed to remain connected at every fixed edge budget, and our default trimming rule favors total cached weight over expected retained score, a choice we did not test against that alternative empirically. EDiS also assumes the full graph is available before extraction begins, so inductive or streaming settings where edges arrive afterward are not addressed. Finally, our empirical study focuses on GCN and GraphSAGE backbones, and broader architectural validation remains future work. 46
