Abstract 

Training-free pruning removes weights from pretrained large language models to reduce their deployment cost. However, existing approaches typically consider only the total number of retained weights. Consequently, their pruning decisions do not account for how target hardware stores and processes those weights. These methods select a sparsity structure in advance and apply it uniformly to all projections, rather than accounting for how each structure trades pruning error against latency and memory across sublayers. We propose Het-erogeneous Structured Pruning (HSP), a hardware-aware pruning framework that combines two components: (i) a heterogeneous search space of dense, unstructured, 2:4, block-sparse, and head / channel (H/C) pruning options and (ii) structure-regularising constraints based on latency and memory models. Our contribution generalises existing importance-based methods: a global solver selects one option per sublayer to minimise proxy pruning loss under a resource budget, thereby assigning each projection a structure and density. We evaluate HSP on OPT and LLaMA models for an NVIDIA Jetson Orin Nano Super edge device, where it improves the quality–resource frontiers over training-free baselines. A project page and updated links for the related code is maintained at: cameronbarker.me/publications/hsp.

Rapid growth in the size of large language models (LLMs) has created a severe deployment bottleneck for resource-constrained edge devices. Earlier work on efficient neural networks explored pruning, quantisation, and depthwise-separable convolutions, including methods for deploying convolutional neural networks (CNNs) on mobile platforms (LeCun, Denker, and Solla 1989; Han et al. 2015; Wu et al. 2016; Han, Mao, and Dally 2016; Chollet 2017). LLMs, however, pose a different pruning problem from CNNs (Zhu et al. 2024). CNNs reuse weights across spatial dimensions and are often compute-bound, whereas LLMs can be compute-bound or memory-bandwidth-bound depending on the inference phase. These differences call for revisiting pruning structures chosen for CNNs and considering finer-grained sparsity within LLM projections. Post-training pruning takes a trained model and removes weights to reduce the model’s footprint while attempting to preserve accuracy. One class of post-training pruning methods, which we refer to as retraining-free pruning, avoids the need for expensive recovery training and instead relies on lightweight heuristics to select weights for removal (M. Sun et al. 2024; An et al. 2024). These methods typically use density (the proportion of remaining weights) as a proxy for model footprint, and they only consider a single sparsity structure across the entire model. While unstructured pruning can achieve strong performance under a density budget, in this setting density is a poor proxy for deployment costs such as latency and memory footprint. This has led to the development of structured pruning methods, which remove weights in a way that is more amenable to hardware acceleration. However, these methods pick a single, coarse-grained sparsity structure a priori, which may degrade accuracy more than necessary if the target device supports a more fine-grained structure or the network contains critical layers that are sensitive to the chosen structure. To address this gap, we propose Heterogeneous Structured Pruning (HSP), a training-free framework that jointly optimises per-sublayer sparsity structures and densities under resource constraints. Rather than relying on hardware-agnostic proxies such as parameter count, HSP uses profiled latency or analytically modelled memory to guide the pruning process. We show that tailoring sparsity structures on a per-sublayer basis improves the trade-off between model performance and deployment efficiency. Figure 1 summarises the framework. Our main contributions are: • Joint structure and density selection: We formulate pruning as a bilevel problem: the inner problem selects masks, while the outer problem allocates structures and densities under a resource budget as a multiple-choice knapsack problem (MCKP). • Proxy-loss failure mode and density floors: We identify a failure mode in which the importance-based proxy favours excessive pruning of a few projections or sub-layers, and use structure-specific density floors to prevent these choices. • Sparse kernels and on-device evaluation: We develop sparse kernels for the Jetson GPU and CPU and evaluate pruned models on both processors, measuring end-to-end latency and peak memory alongside model quality. 32 Related Work 

Post-Training Pruning: Pruning may be integrated into training or applied to an already pretrained model. Although post-training pruning (PTP) avoids pretraining a smaller model from scratch, some PTP methods still use distillation or fine-tuning to recover quality after components are removed (Muralidharan et al. 2024). Training-free methods differ in how they preserve quality without recovery training. Wanda (M. Sun et al. 2024) leaves retained weights unchanged, FLAP (An et al. 2024) adds bias compensation, and SparseGPT (Frantar and Alistarh 2023) updates retained weights through one-shot reconstruction. 

Pruning Granularity: Pruning structures span a hierarchy of granularities. At the coarsest level, methods such as ShortGPT and SLEB remove redundant transformer blocks (Men et al. 2025; Song et al. 2024). Width pruning removes coupled channels or attention heads. LLM-Pruner combines such removal with lightweight recovery tuning (Ma, Fang, and Wang 2023), whereas SliceGPT reduces the embedding dimension by deleting rows and columns after orthogonal transformations (Ashkboos et al. 2024). Finer-grained approaches remove weights individually, in constrained 𝑁 :𝑀 groups (Mishra et al. 2021), or in block-sparse groups (D’Alberto et al. 2024). These impose different restrictions on the admissible masks and have varying consequences for storage and execution. 

Multi-Granularity Pruning: Recent methods combine several pruning granularities rather than committing to one throughout the model. Sheared LLaMA jointly prunes layers, heads, and channels to a target architecture, followed by continued pretraining (Xia et al. 2024). Cascaded Multi-Granularity Pruning removes layers, heads, and feed-forward channels in stages, using low-rank recovery between stages (Wang et al. 2026). Both combine coarse-grained pruning with further training but do not consider finer-grained sparsity within projection matrices. 

Hardware-Aware Pruning: Hardware-aware methods optimise directly against deploy-ment cost rather than parameter count or FLOPs. HALP uses a single channel-based structure in a latency-constrained global knapsack formulation for CNNs (Shen et al. 2022). Spatial downsampling, such as pooling, gives CNN layers different computation per weight which is exploited by HALP. ZipLM targets runtime speedup by iteratively removing structured components with the least favourable loss-runtime trade-off, and supports both one-shot and gradual compression (Kurtić, Frantar, and Alistarh 2023). MDP jointly prunes channels, heads, and embeddings for vision workloads through a mixed-integer nonlinear program constrained by predicted latency (X. Sun et al. 2025). Related hardware-aware neural architecture search (HW-NAS) methods compare candidate architectures using measured or estimated device costs (Li et al. 2021). HSP jointly selects pruning structures and densities across LLM sublayers (attention or MLP) without further training, considering both coarse-grained structures and finer-grained sparsity within projection matrices. Its bilevel formulation separates mask construction by existing pruning procedures from global structure and density selection. HSP uses profiled latency or analytically modelled memory rather than a density proxy. To our knowledge, HSP is the first to combine these granularities in training-free LLM pruning under a modelled latency or memory budget. 43 Pruning with Structured Sparsity 

Removing weights reduces cost only when the target hardware, software kernel, and storage format can exploit the resulting sparsity. Supported structures differ across devices: coarse pruning maps naturally to smaller dense operations, whereas fine-grained sparsity may require specialised hardware primitives (e.g., NVIDIA’s 2:4 sparsity) and otherwise fall back to slower general-purpose paths. Software is often the limiting factor, since hardware support provides speed-up only when exposed through efficient kernels for the relevant matrix shapes. Weight memory is similarly structure-dependent: irregular formats require more positional metadata than block-sparse formats or the compact dense matrices produced by width pruning, so equal retained density need not imply equal memory footprint (Figure 2).

Heterogeneous Structured Pruning (HSP) is a training-free framework for selecting sparsity structures and densities under a deployment-resource budget. Its bilevel formulation separates two decisions: the inner problem constructs a pruned mask for each candidate option, and the outer problem chooses one option per sublayer. HSP reuses the importance scores and masks selected by standard pruning methods for each candidate option, then compares these candidates globally by quality and hardware cost (Figure 1).

## 4.4 From Solution to Pruned Model 

The selected options determine the masks to apply, with no further search. Options are implemented using the same memory format and related kernel that the profiler picked for their structure and density. Head and channel pruning is implemented by converting layers into narrower dense projections and thus use the same kernels as the original model. Shared activations or attention heads and associated cached key–value pairs that are pruned are thus removed without needing specialised kernels. Following FLAP (An et al. 2024), we compensate for removed input contributions by adjusting the consumer bias for the coarser options for models that already have biases. Section B gives the algorithm and realisation details. 

# 5 Experiments 

We evaluate two variants of our method: HSP-Lat, which selects pruning configurations under a profiled-latency budget, and HSP-Mem, which selects them under an analytical-memory budget. # 6 Conclusion 

In this work, we propose Heterogeneous Structured Pruning (HSP), a training-free pruning framework in which a global solver selects one option for every attention and MLP sublayer under a deployment budget, thereby assigning a sparsity structure and density to each projection. Instead of optimising a hardware-agnostic density ratio, HSP assigns every candidate structure and density an explicit deployment cost: latency obtained from reusable kernel profiles on the target device or memory computed analytically from the storage formats and the workload-dependent key–value cache. The model-wide allocation is then solved as a multiple-choice knapsack problem (MCKP). Density floors exclude extreme candidates for which the importance proxy becomes unreliable. On OPT-2.7B and an NVIDIA Jetson Orin Nano Super, HSP-Lat improves the quality–latency frontier on both the CPU and GPU, while HSP-Mem improves the GPU quality–memory frontier. These frontiers use modelled resource costs, with end-to-end latency and peak memory evaluated separately on device. The selected configurations show that both the target hardware and the resource objective govern which sparsity structures are effective. By combining a heterogeneous search space with hardware-aware resource constraints, HSP enables pruning to be tailored to the target device and its deployment bottleneck. 16 Acknowledgements

## A.3 Budgets, Metrics, and Randomness 

Resource Budgets Latency- and memory-constrained searches use a fixed budget 𝐵 in units of time or bytes, respectively. The solver allocates 𝐵 − 𝐶 0 across sublayers. Reported total costs include 𝐶 0. For memory, the sublayer costs include projection weights and biases, sparse metadata, and peak key–value cache size. For latency, they include the profiled projection and attention operations. Density-constrained baselines instead target prunable projection weights, following the convention in Section 3. They do not impose a latency or encoded-memory budget. 

Evaluation Metrics We report perplexity on the WikiText-2 test split (Merity et al. 2017). Its text rows, including empty rows, are joined with two newlines and tokenised once with the evaluated model’s tokenizer. We then use 1024-token windows with stride 1024. Due to runtime constraints, the on-device CPU perplexity results in the main paper use only the first 32 windows. All other perplexity results use the full test split. We also report the unweighted mean zero-shot accuracy across BoolQ, PIQA, HellaSwag, WinoGrande, ARC-e, ARC-c, and OpenBookQA (C. Clark et al. 2019; Bisk et al. 2020; Zellers et al. 2019; Sakaguchi et al. 2020; P. Clark et al. 2018; Mihaylov et al. 2018). Following the 22 harness, we use plain accuracy for BoolQ and WinoGrande and length-normalised accuracy for the other five tasks. The resource metrics are retained parameter density, profile-backed latency, and analytical memory including the workload-dependent key–value cache. In figures, we normalise each to the corresponding dense model unless absolute units are stated. 

Randomness Given the calibration statistics in Table A.2, mask construction, dominance filtering, and MCKP selection are deterministic. Perplexity evaluation is deterministic. Kernel timing and cache control follow the measurement protocol in Section C.4.

## B.2 Independent Option Mask Construction 

For each independent option, the sparsity structure defines the comparison groups in the inner problem of Equation 4. Weights or blocks are ranked by importance within each group, and the requested density determines how many are retained. Unstructured masks keep the highest-scoring weights in each output row. For 2:4, we keep the two highest-scoring weights in each group of four consecutive input columns within a row. For rectangular blocks, we sum the importance scores within each block, then keep the highest-scoring blocks in each row of the block grid. For unstructured and block masks, the requested density determines how many weights or blocks to retain per row, using the rounding rule in Section A. With these counts fixed, selecting the highest-scoring weights or blocks minimises the total importance removed.

## B.5 Coupled-Structure Extensions 

The coupled masks extend to gated MLPs and grouped-query attention (GQA). 

Gated MLPs A gated MLP adds a gate projection 𝐺 alongside the up projection 𝑈 . We apply the same retained-channel row mask to 𝑈 and 𝐺 and the corresponding column mask to the down projection 𝐷 , so M(𝐺 ) = M(𝑈 ) = (M(𝐷 ) )⊤. The inner loss remains the consumer-projection loss on 𝐷 , while the option cost includes 𝑈 , 𝐺 , and 𝐷 .

Grouped-Query Attention In GQA, several query heads share one key–value head. Removing a query head removes its 𝑑 ℎ rows of 𝑄 and the matching 𝑑 ℎ columns of 𝑂 . We keep a key–value head, and its 𝑑 ℎ rows of 𝐾 and 𝑉 , if and only if we keep at least one of the query heads that share it. The 𝑄 mask is therefore still the transpose of the 𝑂 mask, but the 𝐾 and 𝑉 masks are not. A GQA option keeps 𝑑 𝑄 𝐻 𝑄 of the 𝐻 𝑄 query heads, chosen so that they share exactly 𝑑 KV 𝐻 KV of the 𝐻 KV key–value heads. Fixing both head counts fixes the option’s cost, including the key–value cache, before the inner problem chooses which heads to keep. 25 B.6 Bias Compensation 

Consider a projection y = Wx + b with mask M. Let ¯ x be the mean of the rows of the input-activation matrix X(𝜋 ) over the calibration tokens. We use the compensated bias 

eb = b + ( W − W ⊙ M) ¯x, (B.4) which matches the original projection output at the calibration mean. For HSP, we apply Equation B.4 to the consumer projection ( 𝑂 or 𝐷 ) of each coupled option and to every independently block-sparse projection, using that projection’s input mean. We do not compensate coupled producer projections or independent unstructured and 2:4 masks. All reported OPT HSP runs enable this correction and modify their existing biases, so no additional storage or latency is introduced. The bias-free LLaMA-7B HSP runs do not use bias compensation.

## C.4 Measurement Protocol 

All profiles and on-device evaluations use the fixed power, fan, and thermal controls in Section A. For each kernel configuration, we perform two warm-ups followed by ten timed repetitions and use the median latency. We wait for the device temperature to fall below 55 ◦C and flush the L2 cache before each iteration. GPU timings use CUDA events and CPU timings use high-precision timers. For whole-model results, we perform one warm-up followed by five timed GPU executions or three timed CPU executions and report the median. We measure peak memory separately from latency. For GPU runs, we report the maximum memory allocated through the GPU allocator. For CPU runs, we report the maximum physical RAM used by the process.
