Abstract 

Federated vision–language models (FedVLMs) represent one of the emerging frontiers of machine learning, aiming to integrate federated learning within the fine-tuning pipelines of vision–language models (VLMs). In this work, we intro-duce BLEND, a new framework designed to jointly enhance the personalization and generalization capabilities of FedVLMs. BLEND proposes a selective VLM parameter fine-tuning and aggregation strategy that consists of (i) global vision and text adapters shared across clients, and (ii) a personalized vision projection adapter tailored to each client. BLEND further introduces a novel loss func-tion that blends the outputs of the global and personalized adapters to achieve personalization, while simultaneously promoting generalization through a princi-pled anchor design implemented via the projection heads of the zero-shot VLM. Extensive experiments demonstrate that BLEND consistently outperforms state-of-the-art baselines, highlighting its ability to effectively balance personaliza-tion and generalization across diverse system settings. 1 Introduction 

Vision–language models (VLMs) such as contrastive language-image pretraining (CLIP) [1] have rapidly become a major part of modern intelligence [2, 3]. In many applications, the data that fuel VLMs are inherently distributed across numerous entities, which necessitates developing frameworks that enable the collaborative fine-tuning of VLMs across distributed clients . This has given rise to a rapidly emerging research area, termed federated vision–language models (FedVLMs) [4–9]. In this work, we focus on balancing model generalization vs. personalization in FedVLMs , a highly recent field with only a few relevant studies [5, 10]. Our approach is built upon revisiting a powerful observation made in [11], a work dedicated to centralized fine-tuning of VLMs. In particular, revisiting Fig. 1 in [11] , we obtain two intuitions, which point to an effective approach for balancing model generalization vs. personalization in the distributed fine-tuning setting of FedVLMs: 

(Intuition 1) The vision branch of CLIP yields lower dataset-level recognition performance compared to the text branch, indicating that it captures more generalizable representations. However, such generality lacks client-specific adaptation, necessitating personalization to client data in FedVLMs. 

(Intuition 2) Compared to the vision branch, the text branch of CLIP exhibits a higher degree of dataset-level recognition, and thus further personalization may result in overfitting in FedVLMs. Based on the above intuitions (concretized in Sec. 3.2.2), we formalize our research question below: 

> Preprint. arXiv:2610.12907v1 [cs.CV] 9 Oct 2026

How can we design adaptation mechanisms for the vision/text branches of CLIP in FedVLM settings so as to preserve the generalizable representations of the vision branch, avoid over-personalization in the text branch, and jointly enable effective client-specific personalization? 

To address this, we systematically (i) determine where to apply model personalization, (ii) decide which components should be aggregated to capture global knowledge and which should remain local to capture client-specific adaptations, and (iii) obtain a carefully crafted loss function that can leverage such local-global model fine-tuning decomposition in FedVLMs to balance model generalization vs. personalization. In doing so, we modify the CLIP architecture to produce three complementary sources of representations . In the vision branch, we generate a generalized vision representation 

and a personalized vision representation , while in the text branch, we maintain a generalized text representation . To effectively combine these representations, our introduced dual-objective training entails a fusion loss and an anchor loss . This design enables the model to blend the generalized and personalized vision representations into a fusion representation that captures both general and client-specific knowledge, while simultaneously preserving the generalized knowledge of CLIP encoded in its text branch. Compared to a few comparable works [5, 10] and other FedVLM works that are mostly focused on sole model personalization [4, 6, 2, 3] and use techniques such as optimal transport and singular value decomposition, an advantage of our method is its simplicity : it offers a systematic global-local decomposition of CLIP fine-tuning elements combined with a modified loss function that effectively utilizes this decomposition and outperforms all baseline methods. Our contributions are as follows: • We introduce BLEND, a systematically designed framework to balance personalization and general-ization in FedVLMs. BLEND is a fine-tuning strategy that augments CLIP with trainable modules: global vision and text adapters, and a personalized vision projection adapter. • BLEND establishes a selective aggregation scheme, where only the global adapters are aggregated across clients while the personalized projection adapter remains local. It further introduces a novel dual loss function that is designed to leverage the output of these adapters to ensure a balanced model personalization vs. generalization during distributed model fine-tuning in FedVLMs. • Extensive experiments confirm that BLEND consistently outperforms state-of-the-art FedVLM baselines, achieving superior balance between model personalization and generalization. 

⋆ Following the comparable works [3, 2, 6, 5, 4, 12], we adopt CLIP as the deployed VLM. 

2 Related Work 

2.1 Centralized VLM Fine-Tuning Techniques 

Works on the fine-tuning of VLMs can be broadly categorized into (i) prompt-based learning techniques and (ii) adapter-based learning techniques . On the former front, early works such as CoOp [13] introduced learnable text prompts, while subsequent studies, such as ProVP [14], MetaPrompt [15], TCP [16], MoP [17], and DPC [18] focused on improving prompt optimization. Also, researchers have explored Bayesian regularization [19], margin-based tuning [20], test-time adaptation [21], integrating LLMs for dynamic prompt routing [22], self-distillation for zero-shot generalization [23], and semantic-guided prompt optimization [24, 25]. Overall, while prompt-learning serves as a highly lightweight adaptation technique for VLMs, it often suffers from limited performance compared to more expressive adaptation approaches such as adapter-based learning. Adapter-based learning introduces lightweight trainable modules into transformer architectures to capture task-specific information while keeping most parameters frozen. Early works such as Tip-Adapter [26] and CLIP-Adapter [27] proposed leveraging cached feature representations and multi-layer perceptron adapters within the CLIP backbone for few-shot classification. Recently, MMA [11] developed multi-modal adapters for CLIP that jointly refine vision–language alignment, which as a byproduct helps balance generalization and personalization in centralized VLMs. Compared to the aforementioned studies that focus on centralized fine-tuning strategies, BLEND targets an underexplored research direction: the joint design of parameter-efficient adapters, architec-tural adjustments to CLIP, and a novel loss function that explicitly balances model personalization and generalization in distributed/federated fine-tuning settings for VLMs. 

2.2 Federated Vision-Language Models (FedVLMs) 

Recent works on FedVLMs such as PromptFL [6] and FedOTP [4] introduced federated prompt optimization for VLMs, which was followed by PromptFolio [28], FedPGP [5], FedPHA [3], and 2pFedMoAP [2]. In parallel, works such as FedDat [29] and TriplePlay [30] introduced adapter-based fine-tuning of VLMs to distributed clients with non-independent and identically distributed (non-IID) data, which was advanced by pFedMMA [10]. Further, PromptFolio [28] proposed fusing global and local text prompts for FedVLMs. Among these studies, FedPGP, pFedMMA, and PromptFolio are among the very few that aim to improve both personalization and generalization in FedVLMs. Compared to the above works, BLEND provides a systematic design explicitly tailored to balance generalization and personalization in FedVLMs. This objective is directly encoded in its architectural modifications to CLIP, its local training and aggregation procedures, and its loss formulation. Exten-sive experiments demonstrate that BLEND consistently outperforms all existing relevant baselines, establishing a new state-of-the-art in the FedVLM literature.

We consider a FedVLM system, comprising a set of clients N = {1, . . . , N }, where |N | = N . Each client n ∈ N possesses a local dataset Dn, and maintains a local copy of the CLIP model. The goal of our proposed method, i.e., BLEND, is to achieve two potentially competing objectives: (i) enhanced 

personalization , by adapting the local CLIP model to each client’s data through fine-tuning, and (ii) enhanced generalization , by improving the local CLIP model’s ability to recognize previously unseen classes/labels (i.e., those that are not part of clients’ data) that may be encountered during deployment. A schematic of BLEND is depicted in Fig. 1 and its operations are summarized below. 

3.2.1 BLEND in a Nutshell 

BLEND establishes a federated fine-tuning methodology for CLIP based on Intuitions 1&2 

presented in Sec. 1. Specifically, BLEND employs two learnable components, each im-plementing a distinct but complementary mechanism in the learning process to achieve the aforementioned two competing objectives. The first component is a set of adapters (called 

global vision/text adapters ), aggregated across clients, that are designed to learn generaliz-able representations through collaborative learning across clients, while the second compo-nent is an adapter (called personalized projection adapter ), kept local at each client, that is crafted to learn client-specific personalized representations . These two components are then systematically used in a novel loss function to balance personalization and generalization. The realization of these two complementary components stems from a two-step design process. First, we inject the global vision and text adapters into the transformer blocks of both the vision and text encoders of CLIP. These adapters are integrated into the transformer pipelines, pro-ducing a vision output that is passed through the pre-trained (frozen) CLIP vision projection head, referred to as the G-head, and a text output that is processed by the pre-trained (frozen) CLIP text projection head. These pro-jection heads are intentionally kept frozen to preserve the generalizability of the pre-trained CLIP model while ac-companying the learnable global adapters. We then enable the local training of the global adapters on each client, followed by periodic aggregation at the server during federated training. As shown in Fig. 2 (blue square), compared to the pre-trained CLIP model, incorporating the global adapters improves gener-alization to unseen classes by capturing cross-device knowledge. However, while these adapters also yield gains in personalization, we hypothesize that there remains room for improvement, since global adapters alone cannot fully preserve client-specific representations due to their periodic aggregation. To address this limitation and enhance personalization, BLEND injects a personalized projection adapter into the vision projection head of the pre-trained CLIP, referred to as the P-head. This adapter module is learnable but is not transmitted to the server and remains on the client to learn personalized 4representations. As shown in Fig. 2 (orange circle), when this module is used in conjunction with the global adapters, it can significantly improve model personalization. Nevertheless, the results show that relying solely on this module leads to a significant degradation in the model’s generalization. Specifically, given the individual generalization and personalization gains achieved using the global adapters with the G-head and the P-head, respectively, the remaining challenge is as follows: how to integrate all of these components into a unified model architecture to take advantage of their complementary benefits? BLEND achieves this goal through fusing the outputs of G-head and P-head to construct a combined representation, referred to as the projected representation fusion ,which is used as the final visual embedding. Afterwards, by defining a novel loss function, called 

representation fusion loss , BLEND enables the model to fuse these two representations in an optimal manner to balance personalization and generalization of the model. As shown in Fig. 2, BLEND achieves the highest level of personalization while simultaneously maintaining superior generalization, jointly captured by the reported harmonic mean (H-Mean) value. Below, we provide a theoretical motivation for BLEND’s asymmetric personalization of the vision and text branches of CLIP. 

3.2.2 Theoretical Motivations 

BLEND’s asymmetric personalization of encoder branches of CLIP is rooted in the mutual in-formation between the input scene and the visual and textual observations as concretized below: 

Proposition 3.1. Let S denote the latent semantic information of a scene, V its visual observation, and T a textual description generated from V . The information flow satisfies the Markov chain 

S → V → T, and therefore the mutual information I(·; ·) of (S; V ) and (S; T ) holds in 

I(S; T ) ≤ I(S; V ).

Proof. The proof follows from Theorem 2.8.1 (Data-Processing Inequality) in [31]. The inequality I(S; T ) ≤ I(S; V ) establishes that a textual description generated from an image 

cannot contain more information about the underlying scene than the visual observation from which it is derived. For instance, an image may depict a brown dog running on grass, facing left, with a red ball nearby, while its textual description may simply state “an image of a dog.” Thus, the visual observation can preserve multiple semantic attributes that are omitted from the textual description. The above information-theoretic result can be further complemented with two empirical observations from prior work on centralized fine-tuning of VLMs. First, Schrodi et al. [32] identified an information imbalance in CLIP-like models, showing that textual captions often provide sparse descriptions while images contain richer information. Second, Yang et al. [11] observed that textual representations can exhibit stronger dataset-specific behavior compared to vision representations. Together, these observations further justify Intuitions 1&2 presented in Sec. 1, and subsequently motivate BLEND’s asymmetric personalization of CLIP’s encoder branches, with stronger client-specific adaptation applied to visual representations (through introducing P-head).

4 Experiments 

We next study the performance of BLEND (code: https://github.com/payamsiabd/BLEND.git). Experiments were run on a server with 3×L40S GPUs, 1TB of RAM, and 2×Intel 8562Y CPUs. 

System Setup: Following [3] and baselines listed below, we consider a system comprising 10 clients (higher numbers of clients are studied in Ablation Study 2 ). We inject the global adapters to the vision and text transformer layers from ℓS = 1 to ℓE = 12 (see Ablation Study 6 for other injection-layer choices), consider the ranks rx = rw = 16 and rp = 256 , and set the balancing coefficients as 

γ = 0 .09 , λ = 0 .2. Each client performs one epoch of local training (with mini-batch size of 16 and 

η = 0 .0015 ) before participating in global aggregation. 

Baselines: We compare BLEND with several baselines, including zero-shot CLIP [1], FedOTP [4], PromptFolio [28], FedPGP [5], PromptFL [6], pFedMMA [10], pFedMoAP [2], FedPHA [3], and MMA + FedAvg [11]. Here, MMA + FedAvg is our federated extension of a state-of-the-art cen-tralized CLIP fine-tuning, i.e., MMA [11], where clients perform local MMA-based fine-tuning and models are aggregated via averaging as in (25). For all baselines, we use the optimal hyperparameters reported in their papers. 

Datasets: We consider 10 datasets: ImageNet [33], Caltech [34], OxfordPets [35], StanfordCars [36], Flowers [37], Food101 [38], DTD [39], EuroSAT [40], FGVC Aircraft [41], and UCF101 [42]. 

Data Distribution and Evaluation Method: Each dataset is evenly divided into base and novel 

subsets, where only the base portion is used for model training. To simulate non-IID data across clients, we adopt the Dirichlet distribution Dir (αDir ), where αDir denotes the concentration parameter controlling the degree of data heterogeneity [3]. Following [3], we sample 16 shots per class from the base subset for training across clients (the effect of varying this few-shot configuration is analyzed in 

Ablation Study 3 ). Following [11, 5], we consider two performance metrics: 1. Personalization (Per.) Accuracy: measures the accuracy of the final model of each client on its local test data from base classes ( 10% of local dataset is used as test data), thereby capturing the model’s personalization capability .2. Generalization (Gen.) Accuracy: measures the accuracy of the final model of each client tested on the novel classes, thereby capturing the generalization capability .We report the harmonic mean (H-Mean) of the above metrics as a measure of the balance between personalization and generalization [11, 5]. Since the H-Mean emphasizes the smaller of the two values, it is sensitive to imbalances and reflects how well both metrics are simultaneously achieved.

5 Limitations 

BLEND is built on CLIP and focuses on text–image modalities. Extending it to broader multi-modal settings (e.g., audio or video), retrieval and generative tasks, and multi-task learning remains an open direction (see Appendix R). Also, BLEND’s performance depends on hyperparameters (e.g., γ and 

λ) that govern the personalization–generalization trade-off, and their adaptive selection in new envi-ronments remains an open problem. Moreover, extension of BLEND to fully-decentralized/serverless FedVLMs remains open. Finally, the integration of privacy-preserving parameter aggregation mecha-nisms, such as differential privacy, within BLEND is worth investigating. 

6 Conclusion 

We introduced BLEND, a FedVLM framework that integrates globally aggregated adapters into the vision and text encoders of CLIP, together with a locally optimized vision projection head. Through a novel loss formulation, BLEND leverages the output of these components to enhance the balance of personalization vs. generalization. Extensive experiments validated each of the introduced model components and showed that BLEND consistently outperforms state-of-the-art baselines.

5C Varying the Number of Clients 

To extend our experiments regarding the number of clients that collectively train the global model, we repeated the experiments as in Table 1 with larger numbers of clients N ∈ { 20 , 30 }, the results of which we include in Tables 10 and 11, respectively. Inspecting the results in these Tables along with Table 1 in the main text, we can observe that the H-Mean performance of BLEND is consistently better than all the baselines in all the datasets. More importantly, the performance margin between BLEND and the strongest baseline increases as the number of clients grows: the average gain rises from 4.81% for N = 10 (Table 1), to 5.64% for 

N = 20 (Table 10), and further to 6.11% for N = 30 (Table 11). This trend can be attributed to the fact that increasing the number of clients typically amplifies local data bias and heterogeneity: by jointly leveraging globally aggregated adapters and locally personalized projection heads, BLEND is better able to mitigate this increased heterogeneity, resulting in improved robustness and scalability as the number of clients increases.

6D Large-Scale Setting with Real-World Data Distribution 

We further evaluate BLEND under realistic, large-scale client data heterogeneity to better reflect practical deployment scenarios. To this end, we use the real-world iNaturalist-User-120k dataset [43], and conduct experiments with N = 100 and N = 200 sampled clients, where each client follows naturally occurring data distributions reported by the dataset. We use the same network and learning parameters (e.g., λ and γ) as the simulations presented in the main text. The results are summarized in Table 12 and Table 13. For N = 100 clients (Table 12), BLEND achieves the highest H-Mean of 63 .37 , outperforming all baselines by a clear margin. This im-provement stems from simultaneously maintaining strong personalization (Base = 86 .96 ) and better generalization to unseen classes (New = 49 .84 ). In contrast, while some methods such as pFedMOAP achieve high personalization, they suffer from significantly lower generalization, leading to inferior H-Mean. As the number of clients increases to N = 200 (Table 13), all methods experience a drop in performance due to increased data heterogeneity and reduced per-client data. Nevertheless, BLEND achieve the best performance, with an H-Mean of 57 .39 , maintaining a consistent gap over baselines. Overall, these results demonstrate that BLEND scales effectively to large client populations and remains robust under realistic, highly non-IID data distributions, consistently achieving a superior balance between personalization and generalization compared to existing methods.

E Different Few-shot Settings 

To further explore the effect of the number of shots seen from each class during the model training across the clients, we performed an ablation study sweeping the number of shots on the values 

{2, 4, 8, 16 , 32 }. We have performed this experiment for all of the datasets mentioned in the paper and included the results in Figs. 16-25. Also, Fig. 15 depicts the average performance across all the datasets. Intuitively, as the number of shots increase, we would expect each participating client’s local model to become more adapted to the local data distribution on that specific client, hence increasing personalization (this is reflected in Fig. 15-left and in most of the datasets shown in Figs. 16-25). Simultaneously, increasing the number of shots can result in the local models being more biased to their corresponding client’s data distribution, hence damaging the generalization performance. This hypothesis is also confirmed in the generalization performance subplot of Fig. 15-middle and in most of the datasets shown in Figs. 16-25. One key takeaway is that BLEND’s average performance across the datasets is better than all other methods when H-Mean performance is considered (Fig. 15-right). Further, across all individual datasets, BLEND’s H-Mean performance is the best when the number of shots is less than or equal to 16, while being marginally outperformed in two datasets when the number of shots is 32 . 11 G Different Bottleneck Sizes for Personalized Projection Adapter 

To study the effect of the personalized projection adapter’s bottleneck size, we have con-ducted a separate set of experiments, sweeping the bottleneck rank rp over the range rp ∈{4, 8, 16 , 32 , 64 , 128 , 256 , Full-rank }. The results for individual datasets are presented in Tables 26-35, while the average performance across all datasets is reported in Table 25. Given the utility of the personalized projection adapter in our method (discussed in Sec. 3.2), it is expected that a more sophisticated structure for this adapter will result in better personalization performance. This hypothesis is verified as in 7 out of 10 cases in our experiments (Tables 26, 27, 29, 32, 33, 34, 35) the best personalization performance occurs under full-rank settings. On the other hand, the generalization performance often peaks under the two shallowest ranks rp ∈ { 4, 8}

(Tables 26, 27, 30, 31, 32), showing how dedication of relatively larger number of parameters to personalization in the trained global model can negatively affect the generalization performance. Focusing on Table 25 that contains the average personalization and generalization performances alongside their H-Mean for all of the tested rp values across all datasets, we observe that the best personalization performance is achieved under the Full-rank setting and the best balance between generalization and personalization (i.e., the H-Mean) is realized under one of the larger bottleneck ranks ( rp = 256 ). We can thus conclude that sophisticated personalized projection adapters, even though harmful to the generalization performance in some cases, have proven to be significantly beneficial in improving personalization and consequently the balance between personalization and generalization.

12 H Adapter Injection at Different Layers

{1 − 1, 1 − 2, 1 − 3, · · · , 1 − 11 , 1 − 12 , 2 − 12 , 3 − 12 , · · · , 11 − 12 , 12 − 12 }. Our results for these experiments regarding the personalization, generalization, and H-Mean of these two are included in Figs. 27-35, and the average performance across all the datasets is reported in Fig. 26. The H-Mean curves (i.e., the right sub-plots) in Figs. 27–35 indicate that the optimal placement of the adapters depends on dataset-specific characteristics. Nevertheless, when performance is averaged across all datasets (Fig. 26-right), the peak H-Mean is achieved when adapters are distributed across a broader portion of the backbone (layers 1 to 12), highlighting the advantage of multi-layer adaptation. 15 I Effect of γ on Performance

21 K Effect of Aggregation on Various Components 

We devised a set of experiments to localize the effect of aggregating each trainable component in the model. We consider the following scenarios: • Our proposed method (BLEND) where the vision and text adapters are aggregated while the personalized projection adapter is preserved locally. • Aggregation of all the trainable components (corresponding to “Full" in the tables) • No aggregation for any component where all the trained components are kept local (corre-sponding to “NoAgg" in the tables) • Only vision adapters are aggregated (corresponding to “Only Vision" in the tables) • Only text adapters are aggregated (corresponding to “Only Text" in the tables) Our results indicate that for 8 out of 10 scenarios (Tables 37-39, 41, 43-46), aggregating only the text adapters results in the best personalization performance. This comes naturally, as the information about the classes is primarily encoded into the text modality. Hence, aggregating only the text encoders, given the use of pretrained CLIP models for the vision backbones, can enable the models to swiftly adapt to the local data distributions, making personalization less effortful. Intuitively, since the (Full) scenario aggregates all components, we expect it to result in better generalization performance. Our results verify this hypothesis as 9 out of 10 experiments (Tables 37-40, 42-46) have their best generalization test accuracy under the full aggregation scenario. However, in all datasets (Tables 37-46) along with the case that the average performance across the datasets is considered (Table 36), considering the balance between the generalization and personalization (i.e., the H-Mean performance), BLEND results in the best performance.

22 L Effect of Trainable Text Projection Head 

To examine the effect of frozen vs. trainable text projection head, we experiment with different text projection head adapter ranks for the values {∅ , 4, 8, 16 , 32 , 64 , 128 , 256 , Full-rank }, where ∅

corresponds to the case where no adapter is used (i.e., the case where the text projection head is frozen). We include the results for all datasets in Tables 48-57 with the average performance across the datasets included in Table 47. The best personalization result in 6 out of 10 scenarios (Tables 49, 50, 53, 55-57) is achieved without adapting the text projection head to the new data (i.e., by keeping it frozen). The same situation holds for the generalization performance in 5 out of 10 scenarios (Tables 51-55). Given that the best average H-Mean across all datasets is also observed in the absence of the text projection head training (Table 47, right column), we conclude that fine-tuning the text projection head over new sets of data can be redundant on average as it introduces training overhead with no performance gains in terms of H-Mean.

24 N Effect of the Running Anchor Loss 

To further investigate the effectiveness of BLEND’s running anchor loss, we compare it against two alternative zero-shot CLIP regularization strategies: (i) Zero-Shot G-Head , which uses the frozen zero-shot CLIP vision representation for the G-head, and (ii) Zero-Shot Distilled G-Head , which employs KL divergence to distill zero-shot knowledge from frozen CLIP’s vision projection into the G-head. All experiments follow the experimental setting of Table I in the main paper. As shown in Table 59, our proposed running anchor loss achieves the highest H-mean of 82.15%, outperforming the zero-shot G-head and zero-shot distilled G-head by 2.20 and 1.61 percentage points, respectively. These results demonstrate the effectiveness of our running anchor loss in balancing personalization and generalization.

26 P Effect of Explicit Personalization 

To investigate the effect of explicitly supervising the personalized branch (i.e., P-head), we introduce an additional cross-entropy (CE) loss that directly optimizes the personalized representation, rather than relying solely on its contribution through the fusion CE loss. We refer to this variant as BLEND (Sep-Per). As shown in Table 61, introducing explicit personalization increases personalization accuracy from 92.91% to 93.56%, but decreases generalization accuracy from 74.62% to 70.96%. Consequently, the H-mean decreases from 82.15% to 79.78%. These results suggest that directly optimizing the personalized branch can improve client-specific performance at the expense of gener-alization, supporting BLEND’s original formulation, which optimizes the personalized representation through the fusion objective.

R Limitations and Generalization Beyond CLIP 

Extension to Other Multimodal Architectures. BLEND currently focuses on CLIP-based image– text models, consistent with prior work on personalized federated learning with vision-language models. Extending BLEND to other multimodal architectures primarily requires reconsidering where global and personalized modules are inserted into the model pipeline. For instance, in Qwen2.5-VL, one possible extension is to maintain a global vision merger while introducing a separate personalized vision merger. Their resulting visual representations could then be fused and passed to the language model as visual tokens for generation. 

Extension to Other Downstream Tasks. While BLEND currently focuses on classification, ex-tending it to image–text retrieval would require adapting its fusion mechanism and training objectives. Unlike classification, retrieval involves ranking image–text pairs rather than selecting from a fixed set of classes. Therefore, the fusion of global and personalized visual representations must pre-serve cross-modal alignment while balancing generalization and client-specific personalization. For generative tasks such as visual question answering (VQA), BLEND’s global–local decomposition could be retained, while its fusion mechanism and anchor objectives would need to be adapted to autoregressive generation. 

Extension to Multi-Task and Multi-Modal Settings. Extending BLEND to more complex multi-task and multi-modal settings introduces two important challenges. First, different tasks may rely on different modalities to varying degrees, making the information imbalance across modalities task-dependent. This introduces an additional form of heterogeneity, which we refer to as heterogeneity in information imbalance across tasks . Addressing this challenge may require task-dependent placement of personalized modules and adaptive fusion weights. Second, globally shared and aggregatable components must account for task similarity, modality relevance, and potential negative cross-task transfer under heterogeneous client data distributions. Investigating these challenges represents an important direction for extending BLEND to broader multimodal federated learning scenarios. 28
