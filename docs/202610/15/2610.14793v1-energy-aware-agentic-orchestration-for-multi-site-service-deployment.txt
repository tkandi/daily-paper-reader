Abstract —Energy-aware orchestration across geographically distributed infrastructures is increasingly important as data centers absorb growing Artificial Intelligence (AI) workloads. However, existing approaches often exclude the energy cost of automated decision-making from system-level sustainability assessments. This paper presents an energy-aware agentic or-chestration framework that treats Large Language Model (LLM) reasoning as a measurable component of the control loop. We compare single-shot tool calling, ReAct, and plan-and-execute strategies on a multi-site Kubernetes testbed spanning two European data centers and instrumented at layers ranging from power distribution units to per-pod energy attribution. Using ten natural-language orchestration intents with varying decision complexity, we show that reasoning strategy and intent com-plexity strongly influence graphics processing unit (GPU) energy consumption during orchestration decisions. ReAct and plan-and-execute generated executable tool calls across all evaluated intents but required multiple LLM inferences, whereas the single-shot configuration was less costly but non-actionable. Prefix caching reduced mean ReAct GPU energy consumption by 29.4%. For the evaluated lightweight workload, the infrastructure-energy changes observed within the one-second measurement window were insufficient to offset the measured GPU decision energy. These findings demonstrate the importance of accounting for reasoning overhead when assessing the sustainability of AI-native orchestration and motivate energy-budgeted selection of agent strategies. 

Keywords —Green networking, Energy-aware orchestration, Agentic AI, Large language models, Kubernetes, GPU energy

According to the International Energy Agency (IEA), data centers already account for close to 1.5% of global electricity demand and are projected to more than double their consumption by 2030, a trajectory amplified by the rapid uptake of accelerator-bound artificial intelligence workloads. Deciding when, where, and how to deploy containerized ser-vices across geographically distributed infrastructures without compromising sustainability has thus emerged as a central, unresolved operational challenge [1], [2], [3], [4]. Two decades of work in cluster scheduling, autoscaling, and energy-aware placement have produced rich heuristic, op-timization, and learning-based controllers, and modern Kuber-netes ecosystems already expose layered telemetry from power distribution units down to per-pod attribution [5], [6], [7]. More recently, the operations community has begun to investigate Large Language Model (LLM) agents as a new orchestration interface, translating natural-language intents into sequences of monitoring and actuation calls through patterns such as single-shot tool calling, ReAct, and plan-and-execute. The promise is substantial: operators could request the most energy-efficient site for a new workload in plain language, get causal explana-tions for sudden power spikes, or trigger graceful migrations during congestion events, thereby indirectly benefiting end users with greener, more reliable services. However, existing studies evaluate these agents primarily by task accuracy and operational effectiveness, while generally treating the energy consumed by the decision-making process as negligible relative to the managed infrastructure. This assumption may not hold when iterative reasoning executes repeatedly on accelerator-based platforms. In energy-aware orchestration, excluding this control-plane overhead can over-estimate the sustainability benefits attributed to an automated decision. Determining whether, and under which conditions, the operational value of LLM-driven orchestration justifies its additional energy cost therefore remains an important open question [8], [9]. To address this gap, this paper proposes an energy-aware arXiv:2610.14793v1 [cs.NI] 10 Oct 2026 agentic framework for orchestrating containerized services across geographically distributed Kubernetes clusters, in which the energy consumed by the agent itself is treated as a first-class, measurable quantity alongside the energy consumed by the infrastructure it influences. The framework couples a reasoning agent with a typed tool interface that exposes fine-grained Power Distribution Unit (PDU), Running Average Power Limit (RAPL), and per-pod telemetry as well as life-cycle actuation across sites, and instantiates the same pipeline under three reasoning strategies evaluated on a benchmark of ten natural-language intents stratified by decision complexity. We hypothesize that the sustainability of agentic orchestration depends on the interaction between intent complexity, reason-ing strategy, and workload magnitude: more adaptive strate-gies improve actionability at the cost of additional inference energy, while lightweight workloads may fail to amortize this reasoning cost. Our key contributions are: (i) an energy-aware agentic orchestration framework incorporating LLM decision energy into multi-site infrastructure assessment; (ii) the Net Energy Benefit (NEB) metric comparing short-horizon infrastructure savings against accelerator decision energy; (iii) a real two-site Kubernetes testbed instrumented from PDUs down to per-pod attribution and GPU accelerators; and (iv) an empirical evalua-tion quantifying how intent complexity, reasoning strategy, and prefix caching impact decision cost, latency, and actionability. Sections II, III, IV, and V present the related work, method, testbed, and results, respectively; Section VI concludes the paper. II. RELATED WORK

Fig. 1: End-to-end pipeline of the proposed method. The method comprises four coupled components. The first is an intent interpretation layer that receives the operator’s natural-language request and routes it to a reasoning agent. The second is a telemetry access layer, which exposes fine-grained energy measurements of the infrastructure as callable tools. The third is an actuation layer that exposes lifecycle operations on target deployments as callable tools. The fourth is the reasoning agent itself, whose strategy determines how it orchestrates these tools to reach a decision. We describe each component and then explain how they combine into the end-to-end workflow shown in Figure 1. Each intent is a free-form request that refers to one or more sites and expresses a goal related to energy, performance, or placement. Requests range from simple observations, such as querying a site’s current power consumption, to complex optimization goals involving multiple sites and constraints. We categorize intents by complexity level based on the number and nature of operations required to satisfy them. Level 1 intents are satisfiable by a single query or a single actuation. Level 2 intents require a sequence of reads and writes with an explicit dependency between them. Level 3 intents require causal reasoning, hypothetical projection, or multi-constraint optimization across sites. This taxonomy is not a property of the natural language itself but of the decision process that the intent demands. It lets us analyze how different reasoning strategies behave as decision complexity grows. The agent interacts with the infrastructure exclusively through a typed tool interface. Two families of tools are exposed. The first family grants read access to the infras-tructure’s energy state. It includes tools to retrieve aggregate site power, per-pod energy-consumption ranked by magnitude, host-level power measured at the power distribution unit, energy time series for trend analysis, and a cross-site com-parison that enables evidence-based placement decisions. The second family grants the orchestrator write access. It includes tools to query deployment status, scale deployments, migrate workloads between sites, and operate a deployment in both sites simultaneously. Each tool is described to the agent using a natural-language signature that specifies its purpose and parameters. The agent chooses which tools to call, in what order, and with what arguments. This separation is important for the analysis that follows. The telemetry tools are side-effect-free and can be called repeatedly at low infrastructural cost. Actuation tools modify the cluster’s live state and can only be evaluated by executing them. The first is a single-shot tool-use baseline, in which the model receives the intent together with the tool catalog and is expected to emit the required tool calls in a single generation. The second is an iterative strategy based on the ReAct paradigm, in which the agent alternates between reasoning steps and tool invocations. After each tool call, the agent observes the result and decides whether to continue reasoning, call another tool, or terminate with a final answer. This strategy generates multiple inference requests per intent, and the num-ber of requests increases with decision complexity. It offers explicit self-correction at the cost of additional inferences. The third is a plan-and-execute strategy, in which the agent first emits a complete sequence of tool calls based solely on the intent and the tool catalog, and then executes the sequence without further reasoning. This strategy separates planning from execution. In our implementation, it may require additional model calls for plan normalization or final response synthesis, but it does not perform the observation-reasoning loop used by ReAct. These strategies occupy distinct positions on the space de-fined by reasoning overhead and adaptivity. The experimental evaluation in Section V measures where each strategy lands in practice. Figure 2 details the internal loop that the agent executes when processing an intent. The agent first combines the intent with a system prompt that describes the available tools and the orchestration context. The resulting prompt is submitted to the language model, which produces either a textual response, one or more tool calls, or both. When the model emits tool calls, it dispatches each call to the corresponding telemetry or actuation tool. The tool result is appended to the conversation and, if the strategy permits, fed back to the model for the next reasoning step. The loop terminates when the model emits a final textual answer, when a strategy-specific step budget is exhausted, or when a tool returns a terminal condition. Because the method treats reasoning cost as a measurable quantity, we introduce an explicit attribution scheme that separates three contributions to the total energy associated with an intent. We quantify energy consumption across three layers. We measure inference energy at the compute acceler-ator hosting the language model. Managed workload energy is measured at the power distribution unit and attributed at each site’s software layer. We estimate the energy impact of each agent action from the change in infrastructure energy consumption observed before and after the intervention over a fixed measurement window. For action intents, the observed short-horizon infrastructure-energy difference is computed as 

Esaved = ( Pbefore − Pafter )∆ t, where Pbefore and Pafter are the mean cluster powers measured over one-second windows immediately before and after the action, respectively, and 

∆t = 1 second. We then define the net energy benefit as 

N EB = Esaved − EGPU , where EGPU is the accelerator energy consumed during the complete decision window. A positive NEB indicates that the observed infrastructure-energy reduction exceeded the decision energy within the evaluated observation window. A negative value indicates that the decision energy was not recovered within that window; it does not establish whether the action could be energetically amortized over a longer operational period. The infrastructure consists of a single Kubernetes 1.28.15 cluster split across two sites connected by a Virtual Private Network (VPN) tunnel: the primary site at ITAV (Aveiro, Portugal) hosting 3 control plane and 3 worker nodes, and a secondary compute site at UPV (Valencia, Spain) with 3 worker nodes. Control plane responsibilities reside at Aveiro, while Valencia acts as a compute pool. ITAV nodes run Ubuntu 24.04 on kernel 6.8 with contain-erd as the container runtime. UPV nodes run Ubuntu 22.04 on kernel 5.15 with the same runtime. The cluster uses Flannel for pod networking over VXLAN. The sites are reachable via pri-vate addresses: 10.255.40.0/24 for Aveiro and 172.21.32.0/24 for Valencia, and traffic between them traverses the VPN tunnel that connects the two networks. The workload under orchestration is a lightweight dis-tributed web application deployed as a Kubernetes Deployment with no explicit resource requests or limits. The agent scales, migrates, and duplicates replicas of this deployment across the two sites. Because the sites host no competing workloads during the experiments, the application’s contribution to each worker’s power envelope is small relative to the host’s baseline consumption. This property is relevant to the interpretation of the NEB reported in Section V. Each worker node is instrumented to measure energy at three levels, which together provide a layered view of con-sumption from the physical outlet down to the process. At the physical layer, a PDU measures the bare-metal power drawn by each host. At the operating system layer, Intel RAPL exposes per-socket energy counters for the CPU package and its memory controller. At the container orchestration layer, Kepler runs as a per-node DaemonSet in a dedicated namespace and attributes energy to individual pods and containers by combin-ing RAPL counters with cgroup-level resource accounting. All three data sources are published to a single InfluxDB instance at one-second granularity, and the agent consumes them via the Holosense platform, which exposes the underlying store as a query API. The instrumentation covers both Aveiro and Valencia workers symmetrically, enabling the agent to compare the two sites on an equivalent basis. The power draw of the Graphics Processing Unit (GPU) hosting the language model is sampled at 1 Hz through the NVIDIA Management Library, and the resulting power trace is integrated using the trapezoidal rule to estimate the GPU energy consumed per intent. The integration spans the full decision window, including LLM inference, tool execution, and communication delays, and therefore includes the acceler-ator’s power draw during these activities. It does not include the energy consumed by the agent host’s CPU, memory, or network interface; consequently, EGPU represents accelerator-side decision energy rather than the total control-plane energy of orchestration. The evaluation comprises two phases that differ only in their prefix-cache configuration. Phase 1 uses the FP16 model with vLLM’s prefix cache enabled, whereas Phase 2 uses the same model and precision with the prefix cache disabled. In each phase, all 10 benchmark intents are evaluated under each of the three reasoning strategies, with five repetitions per intent–strategy combination executed in randomized order. Before every action intent, the cluster is automatically restored to a canonical baseline in which Aveiro hosts one replica and Valencia hosts none, thereby providing a comparable initial placement state across runs. A 5-second cooldown is inserted between consecutive runs to reduce short-term thermal carryover on the GPU. Every run produces a record that combines four sources of evidence. The GPU energy trace is produced locally on the agent host. The inference-level telemetry, which includes prompt and generation token counts, as well as the prefix cache hit rate, is obtained from vLLM’s Prometheus endpoint and differenced over the run window. The cluster energy state, sampled at both the PDU and Kepler layers, is retrieved from Holosense immediately before and after any action intent. The agent’s orchestration loop records the reasoning trace, which captures the sequence of model inferences and tool invoca-tions. The four sources are aligned on a common timeline and persisted in a single CSV file per phase for subsequent statistical analysis. V. RESULTS AND DISCUSSION

Short-Horizon Infrastructure Impact and NEB . Fig-ure 9 reports the NEB for the five action-oriented benchmark intents. Consistent with the definition in Section III, N EB =

Esaved − EGPU , where Esaved is the infrastructure-energy difference observed across the one-second pre- and post-action windows and EGPU is the accelerator energy consumed during the decision. The resulting values therefore characterize the short-horizon energy balance of each evaluated configuration, rather than the total savings that an action might accumulate over its operational lifetime. Table VI reports a negative NEB for 14 of the 15 strategy– intent combinations under the evaluated one-second attribution horizon. The sole positive value, obtained for Tool Calling on L1_02, cannot be attributed to orchestration because this configuration produced no tool calls and executed no infras-tructure action. Instead, it reflects short-term variation between the two one-second cluster-power measurement windows used to compute Esaved . The Tool Calling entries should therefore be interpreted as a non-action baseline that illustrates the sensitivity of short-window NEB estimates to background power fluctuations. Figure 10 shows no consistent reduction in total cluster power between the one-second pre- and post-action observa-tion windows. Because the lightweight web-service workload contributes only a small fraction of each host’s overall power draw, any action-induced change is difficult to distinguish from short-term variation in the infrastructure baseline. Con-sequently, the observed infrastructure-energy differences were generally insufficient to offset the GPU energy consumed by LLM reasoning within the evaluated observation window. Kubernetes execution records confirmed that the actionable strategies completed the intended operations. ReAct scaled down replicas for L2_04 and migrated the workload for L2_05, while Plan & Execute completed the migration for L2_05. VI. CONCLUDING REMARKS 

Energy-aware orchestration across geographically dis-tributed infrastructures requires accounting for both the en-ergy consumed by managed workloads and the GPU energy cost of automated decision-making. This paper presented an agentic framework that measures these components jointly, introduced the NEB to compare short-horizon infrastructure-energy changes with GPU decision energy, and evaluated three reasoning configurations on a real multi-site Kuber-netes testbed. For the evaluated lightweight workload and one-second attribution horizon, the observed infrastructure-energy differences were generally insufficient to offset the energy consumed by LLM reasoning. ReAct and Plan & Exe-cute produced actionable tool calls, whereas the single-shot Tool Calling configuration did not; prefix caching reduced mean ReAct GPU energy by 29.4%. These findings show that the sustainability of agentic orchestration depends jointly on actionability, reasoning strategy, workload magnitude, and the period over which operational savings are evaluated. Fu-ture work will investigate larger and more energy-intensive workloads, additional model families, adaptive strategies that match reasoning depth to intent complexity, longer post-action observation periods, and carbon-aware placement based on renewable-energy availability.
