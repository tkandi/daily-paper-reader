## Abstract 

Fault diagnosis in asset-intensive industries requires multiclass models trained on noisy, sen-sitive data collected across geographically distributed client sites. Federated learning enables collaborative training without centralizing data, but most approaches do not provide formal privacy guarantees for individual records stored at client sites and can degrade under label noise. To address these limitations, we develop a differentially private, distributionally ro-bust framework for federated multiclass classification that provides sample-level privacy for records stored at each client. We build on a federated Wasserstein distributionally robust multiclass objective with the Crammer–Singer loss, solved using an Alternating Direction Method of Multipliers (ADMM)–based algorithm. To protect the released client updates, our method injects calibrated Gaussian noise into the proximal linearized client updates of both the local model and the local distributionally robust variable before aggregation at the central server, and we quantify the cumulative privacy loss using the moments accountant. We provide per-round sample-level ( ϵ, δ )-differential privacy guarantees and an overall com-posed privacy guarantee across all training rounds, together with convergence results and an explicit privacy–utility tradeoff. Experiments on simulated industrial fault data and UCI benchmark datasets show that our method outperforms differentially private federated em-pirical risk minimization and stochastic gradient descent baselines in most tested settings, with the largest gains under strong privacy-induced noise. 

## 1 Introduction 

In asset-intensive industries such as mining, energy, and aerospace, firms increasingly rely on long-term service agreements with Original Equipment Manufacturers (OEMs) to ensure the reliability of critical components over extended periods of time (Velmurugan & Dhingra, 2021). These agreements are particularly attractive for customers because they shift the risk of equipment failure and maintenance to OEMs, who can reduce maintenance costs throughout their customer base and pass on savings (Bollapragada et al., 2007). As part of the service, the OEM is responsible for diagnosing and distinguishing among multiple fault types that can lead to equipment failure. To meet the performance guarantees specified in these agreements, OEMs must rely on advanced fault diagnostic tools capable of detecting faults early and accurately. In recent years, data-driven machine learning (ML) classifiers have become central to enabling such capabilities. These 1arXiv:2610.13631v1 [cs.LG] 9 Oct 2026 models have been successfully applied to diagnose faults in various systems, including gas turbines, lithium-ion batteries, and aircraft fuel systems (Li et al., 2023; Samanta et al., 2021; Sun et al., 2021). Despite their promise, the implementation of ML-based fault diagnostic systems in real-world industrial settings presents several key challenges. First, training robust ML models typically requires access to large volumes of operational data generated across geographically distributed client sites. However, sharing such data raises privacy concerns and risks disclosing proprietary or sensitive operational information, often restricted by contractual, regulatory, or competitive constraints. As a result, centralized data collection is frequently infeasible. Second, transmitting high-dimensional sensor data to a centralized cloud system for processing can saturate bandwidth and introduce latency, delaying fault detection (Jouini et al., 2024). Such delays increase the risk of missing early signs of failure, which undermines the effectiveness of predictive maintenance strategies. Third, harsh industrial environments often introduce noise into sensor readings, and inconsistencies in fault labeling across sites or operators further amplify data uncertainty. These issues collectively degrade model performance and complicate accurate fault classification. Federated learning (FL) (Syed et al., 2023) has emerged as a promising alternative to centralized training when data pooling is restricted or impractical because of privacy, ownership, or bandwidth constraints. In FL, clients keep their data locally, train models independently, and send model updates to a central server for aggregation. This decentralized approach has been successfully applied to fault diagnosis tasks (Du et al., 2023; Zhang et al., 2021; Yu et al., 2023), making it well suited to our problem setting. However, privacy concerns persist even in federated settings. As shown by Dayal et al. (2023) and Geng et al. (2023), shared model updates can leak sensitive information through the iterative learning process. In industrial fault diagnosis, such leakage may reveal operating conditions or fault severity, thereby risking a client’s competitive advantage. Therefore, while FL reduces the need for centralized data sharing, it does not by itself guarantee privacy, which motivates the incorporation of stronger privacy-preserving mechanisms. In addition to privacy concerns, industrial fault diagnosis data often exhibit significant uncertainty due to noisy labels and features. Distributionally Robust Optimization (DRO) (Kuhn et al., 2019; Ndong & Smyrnakis, 2018; Van Parys et al., 2021; Mohajerin Esfahani & Kuhn, 2018; Delage & Ye, 2010) provides a principled way to address such uncertainty by constructing a set of plausible distributions, called an ambiguity set, around a nominal distribution, most commonly the empirical distribution, and minimizing the worst-case expected loss over this set. We defer the formal definition to Section 2.2. Recent work has also explored DRO in federated learning (Ibrahim et al., 2024b), enabling decentralized training that is more robust to noise. However, most existing approaches focus on binary classification or, when extended to multiple classes, rely on one-vs-one (OvO) or one-vs-all (OvA) strategies for multiclass classification (Zgarni & Braham, 2018; Liu et al., 2023; Islam & Kim, 2019; Mandal & Kale, 2020). Such decompositions can be limiting in practice because they either incur high computational cost or fail to capture relationships among classes. These limitations point to the need for a federated DRO framework that can directly handle multiclass fault classification while remaining robust to uncertainty. While federated learning and DRO together address decentralization and uncertainty, they do not by them-selves eliminate the risk of privacy leakage through shared model updates. To provide strong privacy guar-antees, a widely used mechanism is Differential Privacy (DP) (Dwork, 2006; Dwork & Roth, 2014), which ensures that the distribution of an algorithm’s output changes only slightly when any single data point is added, removed, or modified. In learning settings, this is commonly enforced by adding calibrated noise to the model update before it is released (Chaudhuri & Monteleoni, 2008; Chaudhuri et al., 2011). Although effective for privacy protection, such perturbations can interfere with optimization and degrade model perfor-mance. Furthermore, many existing DP methods rely on assumptions such as strong convexity or smoothness of the objective function, which may not hold for a broad class of loss functions. As a result, incorporating DP into our federated DRO multiclass setting is nontrivial and requires adapting the privacy analysis to this framework. Our work connects three lines of research that have largely been developed in isolation. Prior federated DRO methods based on mixtures of Wasserstein balls have been developed for binary classification with-out privacy guarantees (Ibrahim et al., 2024b), while existing multiclass DRO formulations have primarily been studied in centralized, non-private settings (Ibrahim et al., 2024a). Separately, differentially private 2Alternating Direction Method of Multipliers (ADMM) methods have been developed for empirical risk min-imization (ERM), including settings without smoothness or strong convexity assumptions (Huang et al., 2019), but these methods do not address the federated DRO structure considered here. The one setting in which all three meet uses DRO for a different purpose, namely to counteract the uncertainty introduced by perturbing the local training data for privacy (Shi et al., 2022). Our work instead combines a federated Wasserstein DRO formulation, a native multiclass loss, and sample-level differential privacy for the released client updates. We build on the mixture-of-Wasserstein-balls formulation of Ibrahim et al. (2024b), which considers binary classification and releases exact client updates; we address the multiclass setting directly through the Crammer–Singer loss and release perturbed client updates that satisfy sample-level differential privacy. 

Contributions. The objective of this work is to develop a federated distributionally robust multiclass classification framework for fault diagnosis with formal differential privacy guarantees. The proposed model enables collaborative training across G clients, accounts for uncertainty in both features and labels, and protects sensitive information through privacy-preserving client updates. To the best of our knowledge, this is among the first works to integrate federated learning, Wasserstein DRO, multiclass classification, and differential privacy into a unified framework for fault diagnosis. The main contributions of this paper are as follows: 1. We formulate a federated distributionally robust multiclass classification problem for fault diagnosis that captures uncertainty in both features and labels. We further show that the resulting client update admits a tractable convex optimization formulation, enabling efficient decentralized training. 2. We propose a differentially private ADMM-based algorithm for the resulting multiclass federated DRO problem, which perturbs both the local model and the local DRO variable with calibrated Gaussian noise. The proposed methodology allows privacy-preserving training under weaker as-sumptions than those commonly required in existing DP methods, such as strong convexity and smoothness. 3. We derive sample-level differential privacy guarantees for the noisy client updates and use the mo-ments accountant to compose the resulting privacy loss across training rounds. 4. We analyze the convergence behavior of the proposed noisy training algorithm and characterize the resulting privacy–utility tradeoff. 5. We evaluate the proposed method on simulated industrial fault diagnosis data and several UCI benchmark datasets through a comprehensive benchmarking study. The results show that the pro-posed approach remains competitive under privacy constraints, with additional experiments assessing scalability with respect to the number of clients and classes. The remainder of this paper is organized as follows. Section 2 introduces the problem setup, the distri-butionally robust learning framework, and the differential privacy preliminaries. Section 3 develops the federated multiclass DRO formulation and the non-private training algorithm. Section 4 presents the pro-posed differentially private algorithm. Section 5 establishes its privacy guarantees, and Section 6 analyzes its convergence and the resulting privacy–utility tradeoff. Section 7 reports the experimental evaluation, and Section 8 concludes the paper.

2.3 Differential Privacy 

In federated learning, clients share model updates with a central server, which in our setting is the OEM, rather than sharing raw data. However, these updates may still reveal information about the underlying local datasets through inversion attacks (Fredrikson et al., 2015) or membership attacks (Shokri et al., 2017). 4In our setting, such leakage could reveal whether a client site operates under a sensitive regime or exhibits unusually high failure rates. Therefore, our goal is to protect individual records within each client’s local dataset, rather than to hide whether a client participates in training. We consider two possible adversaries: an honest-but-curious server that follows the protocol but may use client updates to infer information about local records, and an external eavesdropper who has access only to the communicated model updates. We assume that the adversary cannot directly access a client’s local dataset. To address this issue, we adopt differential privacy (DP), a rigorous mathematical framework that provides formal privacy guarantees for individual data records. DP ensures that replacing a single data sample in a dataset does not significantly alter the output distribution of a randomized algorithm, thereby limiting the amount of information that can be inferred about that sample (Dwork, 2006). 

Definition 1 (( ϵ, δ )-Differential Privacy (Dwork & Roth, 2014)) . A randomized mechanism M satisfies 

(ϵ, δ )-differential privacy if, for all adjacent datasets D and D′ differing in one element, and for all measurable sets S,

P[M(D) ∈ S] ≤ eϵ P[M(D′) ∈ S] + δ. 

Here, ϵ denotes the privacy budget, with smaller values corresponding to stronger privacy guarantees, while 

δ denotes a small failure probability under which the guarantee may not hold exactly. In this work, we employ the Gaussian mechanism, which adds noise sampled from a Gaussian distribution to each model update before it is sent to the server.

The design of Algorithm 2 is guided by three main considerations. First, the approximate augmented Lagrangian enables us to handle a nonsmooth objective by replacing it with a first-order approximation. This makes the local subproblem easier to solve, leads to a closed-form update, and allows faster computation. More importantly, it enables us to derive upper bounds on the sensitivity of the local updates without requiring the original objective to be smooth or strongly convex. Second, the proximal terms ∥Mg − ˜M t−1 

> g

Since the sensitivities of the local updates scale with the step sizes, the step size directly determines the amount of injected noise, which allows the two to be tuned jointly through a single parameter. 

## 5 Differential Privacy Guarantees 

In this section, we establish the differential privacy guarantees of the proposed algorithm. ## 7 Experimental Evaluation 

In this section, we evaluate the performance, sensitivity, and scalability of the proposed differentially private federated multiclass DRO algorithm. We first study the sensitivity of the algorithm to the privacy parameters and its convergence behavior on simulated industrial fault diagnosis data. We next benchmark the method against private and non-private baselines on five UCI multiclass datasets. Finally, we examine scalability with respect to the number of clients and the number of classes. Unless otherwise stated, each experiment 12 is run for T = 150 training rounds and repeated over 10 random seeds, each redrawing the data partition and all injected noise. We report the mean performance across seeds, and all error bars represent one standard deviation. We use uniform client weights, that is, αg = 1 /G for all g = 1 , . . . , G , and all clients share the same local hyperparameter settings. Across all experiments, we compare the proposed method against several relevant baselines. These include a differentially private federated ERM model, denoted DP-Fed ERM, based on Huang et al. (2019) and instantiated with the Crammer–Singer multiclass hinge loss and the Frobenius regularizer R(M ) = 12 ∥M ∥2 

> F

, as well as a federated adaptation of differentially private stochastic gradient descent (Abadi et al., 2016), denoted DP-Fed SGD. We also include the non-private federated multiclass DRO model described in Algorithm 1 in the sensitivity analysis. In the benchmarking experiments, we further compare against an OvA federated DRO formulation, which trains independent binary DRO classifiers using the binary hinge loss, and a centralized non-private benchmark.

7.2 UCI Data Experiment 

In this benchmarking experiment, we use the Macro-F1 score as the primary evaluation metric, since it weights all classes equally and is therefore better suited to the class-imbalanced client splits used in this experiment. 

Datasets. We use five popular multiclass benchmark datasets: Dermatology with 6 classes, and Iris, Wine, Palmer Penguins, and Seeds with 3 classes each. For all datasets, we use an 80%/20% train-test split and set G = 4 clients. Features are min-max scaled using statistics computed on the training split, and each sample is normalized to have ℓ2-norm at most 1. The training samples are distributed across clients using a controlled class-imbalanced split, where each class is unevenly allocated across clients while ensuring that all clients retain samples from every class. To account for label uncertainty, 10% of the training labels are flipped at random, with the test labels left clean; all results in Table 1 are reported in this setting. 

Hyperparameters. We fix the differential privacy parameters to ϵ = 0 .5 and δ = 10 −6 across all datasets, with the per-round budget split as in Section 7.1, and train for T = 50 rounds, which corresponds to a cumulative budget of ¯ ϵ ≈ 3.7. 7.3 Scalability Experiments 

This experiment evaluates the scalability of the proposed algorithm with respect to both the number of clients and the number of classes. For client scalability, we consider two regimes. In the first, the number of clients increases while the total sample size is fixed, so that each client has fewer local samples as the federation grows. In the second, the number of clients and the total sample size increase together, so that the number of samples per client remains fixed. These two settings allow us to distinguish the effect of adding more clients from the effect of reducing the number of samples assigned to each client. To compare all models under a common stopping rule, we measure scalability using the runtime required to attain the peak mCCR within a prescribed tolerance. 

Dataset. To control the data generation process, we use the make_classification function from the Scikit-learn Python package (Pedregosa et al., 2011). Across all scalability experiments, we use an 80/20 train-test split. In the client scalability experiments, we fix K = 4 classes and P = 10 features, and vary the number of clients as G ∈ { 5, 10 , 15 , 20 , 25 , 30 }. The fixed-total-sample setting uses N = 3000 samples in total, while the fixed-local-sample setting uses 100 samples per client, in both cases before the train-test split. In the class scalability experiment, we fix G = 4 clients and P = 10 features, and vary the number of classes as K ∈ { 3, 5, 7, 9}. Each class is generated from a Gaussian distribution with standard deviation 1, with class means located at the vertices of a P -dimensional hypercube with side length 5. We apply the same min-max and ℓ2 normalization as in Section 7.1. The samples are balanced across classes, and no label flipping is introduced. 

Hyperparameters. Across all scalability experiments, we train for T = 120 rounds and fix the privacy pa-rameters to ϵ = 0 .2 and δ = 10 −8 per round, with the per-round budget split as in Section 7.1, corresponding 15 to a cumulative budget of ¯ ϵ ≈ 2.2. The ADMM-based federated models use a global consensus parameter of 

ρ = 0 .5. The DP-Fed DRO client problems use the label transportation cost κ = 0 .5, the Wasserstein ambi-guity radius r = 0 .02, and the penalty parameter γ = 0 .1, while the non-private DRO models use κ = 0 .25 and r = 10 −5. The DP-Fed ERM baseline uses the regularization parameter λ = 10 −4, with cw set on each configuration to the Frobenius norm of the non-private ERM solution, while DP-Fed SGD uses the decaying learning rate ηk = 0 .1/√k with the same regularization parameter. We use the ℓ∞-norm throughout. The step-size constants are set to cM = 1 and cλ,g = 1 for all clients. The remaining constants are set to C = 0 .4, 

V = 1, and L = 1, so that S = max {r, κ − r} = 0 .48. 

Results. The results are presented in Figure 3. In the fixed-total-sample setting, the runtime required to reach peak mCCR remains relatively stable for the private models as the number of clients increases. Thus, adding clients does not substantially increase the runtime when the total dataset size is fixed. For the Non-Private Fed DRO baseline, the runtime decreases initially and then stabilizes, which is consistent with the fact that each client receives fewer local samples as G increases and the local DRO subproblems become smaller. Across all values of G, Non-Private Fed DRO remains the most expensive method by at least an order of magnitude, because each round requires solving local DRO optimization subproblems, whereas the private models rely on simpler local updates. Among the private models, DP-Fed DRO is slower than DP-Fed ERM and DP-Fed SGD by a roughly constant factor, reflecting the additional terms introduced by the DRO formulation. In the fixed-samples-per-client setting, the runtime generally increases with the number of clients. Unlike the fixed-total-sample setting, increasing G also increases the total sample size, so the models must process more data while coordinating across more local clients. The same relative ordering is observed: Non-Private Fed DRO has the largest runtime, while the three private models remain substantially faster and within an order of magnitude of one another. These results indicate that the proposed DP-Fed DRO method scales similarly to the private ERM and SGD baselines with respect to the number of clients, while maintaining the robustness benefits of the DRO formulation. For scalability with respect to the number of classes, the runtime increases with K for all methods, especially for the Non-Private Fed DRO baseline. This behavior is expected because, when the feature dimension is fixed, increasing the number of classes makes the multiclass problem harder by requiring the models to separate more groups in the same feature space. The private models show a much milder increase. DP-Fed DRO remains slower than DP-Fed ERM and DP-Fed SGD by a roughly constant factor across the tested values of K, so its relative overhead does not grow with the number of classes, and it stays orders of magnitude below the non-private model. This suggests that the additional cost of the private DRO formulation remains manageable as the number of classes grows. 

## 8 Conclusion 

In this paper, we address the problem of federated multiclass classification under feature and label uncer-tainty, with formal privacy guarantees for the local data. To this end, we develop a differentially private federated distributionally robust multiclass classification framework that combines Wasserstein-based robust-ness with sample-level differential privacy across all training rounds. We further derive a training algorithm that privatizes both the local model and the local DRO variable, and establish its convergence at an O(1 /√T )rate. On simulated industrial fault diagnosis data with distribution shift, DP-Fed DRO is the strongest pri-vate method at moderate privacy budgets, outperforms DP-Fed ERM at every budget where the private models are informative, and is the most robust to the choice of δ. On the benchmark datasets, DP-Fed DRO achieves the best private performance on most datasets, while the runtime experiments show that it scales with the number of clients and classes similarly to the private baselines. Comparisons with non-private na-tive multiclass and OvA DRO show complementary behavior on real datasets, while the controlled synthetic study identifies correlated features and overlapping fault classes as the setting in which the native multiclass formulation provides its clearest advantage. Overall, these results show that robustness and privacy can be incorporated simultaneously in federated multiclass learning. Future work could investigate how to jointly optimize each client’s communication schedule and its privacy budget allocation across training rounds, so 16 that clients communicate only in selected rounds and spend their budget at different rates over training, under a fixed per-client privacy budget.

B.2 Datasets Utilized B.2.1 Simulated Industrial Fault Diagnosis Dataset 

The simulated pump dataset was generated using the pump fault diagnosis model in MATLAB/Simulink (MathWorks, 2026). We considered four operating conditions: healthy operation, cylinder leak, blocked inlet, and increased bearing friction. Only single fault conditions were considered, so that at most one fault was active in each simulation run. The simulator parameterizes each fault on a grid of 10 severity levels, the first of which corresponds to nominal operation. For each fault type, we sampled from a subset of the upper severity levels (levels 5 and 6 for the cylinder leak, levels 3 and 4 for the blocked inlet, and levels 7 to 9 for bearing friction), so that the three faults differ in difficulty while remaining distinguishable from healthy operation. The dataset contains 2,000 simulation runs, with 750 healthy samples and 418, 418, and 414 samples for the cylinder leak, blocked inlet, and bearing friction classes, respectively. The simulated time series data were then processed to extract 13 tabular features, which were subsequently used as inputs for the classification models.

B.4 Controlled Study on Correlated Fault Classes 

To identify the conditions under which a native multiclass formulation is most beneficial relative to OvA, we consider a controlled synthetic setting with correlated features and varying overlap between fault classes. Such conditions may arise in physical fault-diagnosis systems when faults in different components influence the same subsystem measurements, producing correlated sensor responses and similar fault signatures. This creates a setting in which the relationships among fault classes may be important for classification, making it well suited for comparing the non-private federated multiclass DRO model (Non-Private Fed DRO), which learns all class margins jointly, with its one-vs-all counterpart (Non-Private Fed OvA DRO), which trains independent binary classifiers. We generate a four-class synthetic dataset consisting of Normal, Fault A, Fault B, and Fault C, with Ntrain =860 training samples and Ntest = 216 test samples. The training set is imbalanced, with the Normal class overrepresented relative to the fault classes, whereas the test set is balanced. For each class, feature vectors are sampled from a multivariate normal distribution in an eight-dimensional feature space. The class-specific mean vector determines the class center, while a covariance matrix shared across all classes controls the feature spread and correlation structure. The covariance matrix is constructed so that two subsets of features are internally correlated, while the remaining features exhibit a weaker correlation structure. The class centers are chosen so that Fault A and Fault B form a related fault pair, while Fault C represents a more distinct fault mode. To vary the degree of overlap, we change the center of Fault B relative to Fault A, producing strong-, moderate-, and low-overlap regimes while keeping the covariance structure fixed. We introduce 10% random label noise only in the training set, while the test labels remain clean. Each experiment is repeated over 10 random seeds with G = 4 clients and T = 50 communication rounds. We select the Wasserstein radius r ∈ { 10 −4, 10 −3, 10 −2} and label transportation cost κ ∈ { 0.25 , 0.5, 0.75 } by 3-fold cross-validation, while fixing ρ = 0 .5 and using the ℓ∞-norm. 39 Table 4: Performance of native multiclass DRO and OvA DRO across Fault A/Fault B overlap regimes.
