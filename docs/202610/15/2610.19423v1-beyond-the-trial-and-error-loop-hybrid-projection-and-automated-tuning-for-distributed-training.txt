# Beyond the Trial-and-Error Loop: Hybrid Projection and Automated Tuning for Distributed Training 

Anshu Raina Peyman Razaghi Yuankai Chen Cheng Yao Yao Fu Vidushi Goyal Devang Patel Wen Xie Zhenyu Gu Emad Barsoum 

Advanced Micro Devices, Inc. Correspondence: anshu.raina@amd.com 

## Abstract 

Configuring distributed LLM training across hundreds of GPUs requires jointly optimizing tensor, pipeline, expert, context, and data parallelism alongside micro-batch size, recomputation, sharding, and overlap, a combinatorial space where the boundary between memory-legal and OOM can hinge on a single byte of per-element activation accounting. Today, teams navigate it through expensive multi-node trial and error, burning GPU-hours on crashes and suboptimal recipes. We present Primus Projection , which closes this loop with two coupled components. First, a hybrid projection engine that measures what it can and simulates what it cannot: GPU microbenchmarks on as few as a single device capture compute kernels and any in-scope communication; a sub-node downscale–measure–upscale workflow analytically restores out-of-scope collectives, FSDP overlap, and the full pipeline schedule at the multi-node target. A pure simulation mode runs entirely on CPU, enabling pre-silicon planning without any GPU. Second, the Primus Tuning Agent treats the projection engine as a fast scoring oracle (seconds per candidate instead of tens of minutes) and uses it to drive an intelligent search over the configuration space. A deterministic seed planner sweeps single axes; an LLM investigation loop proposes cross-axis combinations the planner cannot reach, with an analytic memory pre-filter that rejects infeasible candidates before any GPU time is spent. Validated on Llama 3.1 (dense) and Mixtral 8 ×22B (MoE) across two GPU generations, all projections stay within ∼10% of measured multi-node throughput, and fidelity holds at production scale: 4.7% error on DeepSeek V3 671B at 1,024 GPUs and 1.9% on Qwen3-235B-A22B at 256 GPUs. In a Mixtral 8 ×22B case study, the Primus Tuning Agent discovers a configuration delivering +27% throughput over the published 4-node BF16 reference at the same scale, in under 30 minutes of single-node exploration, with no hand-written configs and no full-cluster profiling pass. 

## 1 Introduction 

The rapid growth in large language model (LLM) scale, from billions to trillions of parameters, has made distributed training configuration a first-class engineering challenge. A single 100B+ run jointly selects five interacting parallelism dimensions (TP, PP, EP, CP, DP) plus micro-batch size, sequence length, gradient accumulation, recomputation, FSDP sharding, and overlap flags; each combination trades memory, compute utilization, and communication overhead differently, and the combinatorial space of valid configurations grows rapidly with model size and cluster scale, making exhaustive trial-and-error infeasible. In practice, teams launch full multi-node runs, observe OOMs or suboptimal throughput, adjust, and repeat—each iteration consuming hours of expensive cluster time and delaying model development. Existing tools fall short of closing this loop. Analytical cost models (e.g. Calculon [10], Gal-vatron [18]) predict training time from hardware specifications or single-device layer profiles with nominal-bandwidth communication, but miss GPU frequency throttling under sustained

Full-system simulators (e.g. ASTRA-sim [25], Echo/Moye [7]) achieve higher fidelity but require target-hardware traces or extensive calibration, and do not scale to the configuration sweeps needed for planning. We present Primus Projection : compute kernels are benchmarked on as few as a single GPU; out-of-scope communication, FSDP overlap, and the full pipeline schedule are reconstructed analytically at the target scale. A pure simulation mode runs entirely on CPU for pre-silicon planning. The Primus Tuning Agent treats the projection oracle as a fast surrogate to search the full configuration space end-to-end—parallelism, schedule, recomputation, MoE backend, and precision—replacing days of manual profiling with minutes of single-node exploration. We validate Primus Projection against published multi-node training results [2] on AMD Instinct MI325X and MI355X GPUs across dense (Llama 3.1 [9]) and MoE (Mixtral 8 ×22B [12]) mod-els, keeping all projections within ∼10% of measured throughput, and at production scale on DeepSeek V3 671B [4] (1,024 GPUs, 4.7% error) and Qwen3-235B-A22B [33] (256 GPUs, 1.9%). Projection also preserves configuration rankings (Spearman ρ ≥ 0.93 ) on Mixtral, Qwen3-235B-A22B, and GPT-OSS-120B [21], always selecting the measured-best configuration. In a Mixtral tuning case study, the agent delivers +27% throughput over AMD’s published 4-node BF16 reference at the same scale in under 30 minutes. 

## 2 Background and Motivation 

A distributed LLM training run is defined by a configuration vector (TP, PP, VPP, EP, CP, DP) 

along with choices for micro-batch size, sequence length, gradient accumulation, recomputation, FSDP sharding, and communication overlap. Each combination determines a unique point in a high-dimensional space of memory consumption, compute utilization, communication overhead, and pipeline bubble fraction. For a Mixtral 8 ×22B run on 64 GPUs, the partition constraint TP × PP ×

EP × CP ≤ 64 alone admits roughly 160 ordered factorizations; layered with MBS ∈ { 1, . . . , 8},recomputation level ∈ { none, sel, full }, MoE backend ∈ { A2A, DeepEP }, and pipeline algorithm 

∈ { 1F1B, Interleaved [20], ZB [24], ZBV, ILP }, the search space grows to O(10 4) topologically valid points, of which only a small fraction is simultaneously memory-legal and high-throughput— and the boundary between feasible and infeasible is razor-thin: a 4-byte miscount of activation memory per element across 56 MoE layers is enough to push a configuration from legal to OOM ,voiding hours of queued cluster time. Manual exploration in this space is impractical. Pure analytical performance modeling has inherent pitfalls: GPU clock throttling under load creates a 44–70% gap between peak and Maximum Achievable FLOPs [26]; ROCm stack updates (hipBLASLt, Composable Kernel, Triton) shift per-operation throughput unpredictably; MoE grouped GEMMs depend on wave quantization and routing distributions that resist closed-form treatment [8]; and opti-mizer, allocator, and gradient-accumulation overhead is invisible to roofline models. Benchmarking actual kernels on the deployed stack captures all four automatically. Primus Projection pairs such a benchmark—measured on as few as a single GPU—with analytical models for collective communi-cation, pipeline scheduling, and FSDP overlap, composing the two so the projection transfers to any multi-node target without launching a multi-node run. 

## 3 Methodology 

Primus Projection is a modular pipeline of five components: memory projection (analytical), layer profiling (benchmark or simulation), communication modeling, pipeline schedule simulation, and multi-node scaling. Hardware profiles for AMD Instinct MI300X, MI325X, and MI355X are bundled and custom YAML profiles can be added (Appendix A). The two top-level capabilities— Modeling 

and the Primus Tuning Agent that builds on it—compose into a single projection oracle (Figure 1). 

3.1 Modeling Memory: A hierarchical profiler tree mirroring the model’s module structure (embeddings, dense / MoE transformer layers, output, loss) returns sharded per-GPU parameter, gradient, and optimizer-state counts together with per-microbatch activation memory, accounting for TP weight splits, PP layer assignment, EP expert sharding, CP replication, and FSDP/ZeRO sharding across DP. The projector treats precision as two independent axes—a parameter/optimizer regime (BF16 vs. FP8 mixed precision; Adam / AdamW / distributed optimizer) and a saved-activation regime (BF16 by default, FP8 when the framework casts saved activations to FP8 for memory savings)—each contributing a per-element byte cost. Pipeline-schedule interleaving, gradient accumulation, and recomputation (full or selective) compose with the activation regime; full recomputation reduces the per-layer activation footprint to a single checkpointed input, again at the selected activation precision. 2Figure 1: Primus Projection architecture. Modeling components consume model card, hardware profile, and cluster spec; the projection oracle returns a memory and throughput estimate that drives both multi-node projection and the Primus Tuning Agent’s iterative search. 

Activation memory dominates for large MoE workloads (a single MoE layer can outweigh attention activations by an order of magnitude), so accurate per-layer accounting is critical for memory-legal configuration discovery. Per-component equations and byte costs are in Appendix B. 

Performance: measure what you can, simulate what you can’t. Primus Projection’s default mode combines both: when a parallelism dimension fits inside the available benchmark GPUs—TP AllReduce within a node, EP All-to-All within the benchmark configuration, intra-node DP gradient AllReduce—the corresponding collective is captured directly by the kernel-level benchmark alongside compute. Only communication that falls outside the single-node benchmark scope, primarily inter-node traffic and the deltas introduced when a parallelism dimension is reduced for benchmarking, is reconstructed analytically. In the limit of a single-GPU benchmark no collective is directly measurable, since collectives are inherently multi-GPU; in that case every communication term is reconstructed from the analytic model below, and only compute time is measured. Pipeline scheduling follows deterministic dependency rules and is simulated exactly from the per-stage compute times produced by the benchmark. Framework overhead (allocator pauses, gradient-accumulation glue) is absorbed implicitly into the measured kernel sequence; the optimizer step runs once per iteration and is measured directly on the benchmark (falling back to an analytic HBM-bandwidth model). Measure. The projector benchmarks a minimal set of layers covering every distinct layer type :one dense layer for a pure dense model, one MoE layer for an all-MoE model, or one of each for mixed architectures (e.g. DeepSeek’s 3 dense + 58 MoE layers). All layers of the same type share architecture and GEMM shapes, so differences in learned weights do not affect kernel time, and each selected layer is timed for forward and backward separately. For MoE layers a decom-posed measurement captures compute and All-to-All times independently, because A2A will be replaced analytically during upscale while the compute portion is kept verbatim. When PP =1 the per-type time scaled by the layer count of that type yields the full-model time; when PP >1 the pipeline simulator constructs a per-stage time matrix from the model’s layer assignment (respecting 

decoder_first /last_pipeline_num_layers and pipeline_model_parallel_layout ) and simulates the schedule with exact bubble accounting. 

Upscale. Each reduced dimension is restored analytically and the contributions compose. PP is reconstructed by simulating the target schedule (1F1B, Interleaved, Zero-Bubble, ZBV, ILP) from benchmarked per-layer times, distinguishing intra- and inter-node P2P. EP is restored by adding, per MoE layer, the A2A delta ∆ = A2A (C∗) − A2A (Cbench ). Both terms come from the topology-aware collective model (Appendix B): A2A (Cbench ) is the A2A of the downscaled configuration that was actually benchmarked, and A2A (C∗) is the A2A of the full target EP group, priced on inter-node links whenever that group spans nodes. When the decomposed MoE measurement is available, the measured intra-node A2A anchors the model, so only the change from the benchmark group to the target group is extrapolated. TP applies two adjustments per layer: compute is rescaled by bench_tp /target_tp and a TP AllReduce delta is added; with measured AllReduce data, the ratio form target AR =measured AR × [analytical (target )/analytical (bench )] improves accuracy. The order PP → EP → TP is ranked by restoration fidelity: PP is exact (only the P2P latency model contributes residual), EP preserves compute (only A2A is exercised), and TP relies on a compute-scaling assumption that holds well for dense GEMMs but is the least exact of the three. 

Profiling modes. Independently of the downscale / measure / upscale loop, the projector exposes a 

simulate mode that runs without any GPU—layer kernel times come from the Origami GEMM model [30] and a tile-level Flash Attention v3 simulator [27]—and a both mode that runs benchmark 4and simulation side-by-side for cross-validation. The simulate mode supports laptop-scale planning and pre-silicon exploration of unreleased GPU profiles. 

Communication, pipeline, and parallelism: The communication model spans three topology domains—intra-node, inter-node, inter-pod—each parameterized by per-link bandwidth and latency drawn from the hardware-profile YAML, so the same engine projects MI300X xGMI clusters, MI355X scale-up pods, or hypothetical future fabrics without code changes. For each collective the model enumerates the applicable algorithms (AllReduce: bandwidth-optimal ring [22], hyper-cube, Bruck, single-shot × four protocols; All-to-All: direct, hierarchical, PXN/DeepEP; P2P: latency+bandwidth) and selects the minimum-time candidate for the target topology and message size. For MoE models with EP >1, gradient AllReduce is decomposed into expert (across DP/EP replicas) and non-expert components, with expert AR on the critical path because A2A barriers prevent overlap with backward compute. FSDP communication uses a per-layer overlap model with phase-specific ceilings. The pipeline simulator supports six scheduling algorithms (1F1B, Interleaved 1F1B, Zero-Bubble, ZBV Formatted/Greedy, Sea AI Lab ILP [24]); a compare-all flag runs every applicable scheduler. Reference bandwidth/latency values, scheduler bubble formulas, and FSDP ceilings are in Appendix B. 

3.2 Primus Tuning Agent 

The configuration space is jointly optimized over parallelism dimensions, pipeline schedule, micro-batch size, recomputation granularity, precision mode, MoE communication backend, and overlap flags. The agent treats the projection oracle as a cheap analytical surrogate (seconds per candidate on a single node) and converts the expensive multi-node search into a programmatic one. Given a model card, target cluster, and HBM budget, it returns a high-throughput, memory-legal recipe end-to-end with no hand-written Megatron configs. The agent operates in two phases over a shared trial ledger that serves as its session memory. A deterministic seed planner emits 12 candidates by walking single-axis sweeps (memory frontier; MoE backend; pipeline schedule and VPP depth; micro-batch size and distributed optimizer); each axis is anchored on the user-supplied baseline so that single-axis improvements are directly attributable. The seed phase establishes a ledger of (configuration, projected throughput, projected memory, legality) 

tuples that grounds the second phase. An LLM investigation loop then reads this ledger and proposes cross-axis combinations as structured JSON, evaluated iteratively by the same projection oracle (up to 25 trials per round across up to 4 rounds). The loop, structured in the spirit of ReAct [35]-style interleaved reasoning and recursive tool use [36], is implemented in DSPy [13] as two cooperating modules: a Planner LLM emits a hypothesis paragraph plus a JSON list of candidate configurations to evaluate next, and a Recursive Language Model (RLM) [36] executes those candidates one at a time, calling the projection oracle as a tool, writing notes to a per-round scratchpad, and optionally querying a sub-LLM for deeper analysis of intermediate results. Every candidate first passes an analytic memory pre-filter that rejects OOM-bound configurations before any GPU time is consumed (∼25 minutes saved per rejection); survivors are scored via sub-node benchmark projection. A trial is legal iff axis validation passes (PP ×DP ×CP ×TP tiles the cluster), projected memory ≤ cap, and the benchmark returns a parseable throughput; the winner is arg max legal (tok/s/GPU ). The seed schedule, LLM prompting strategy, and trial-scoring rule are documented in Appendix C.

All projections stay within ∼10% of measured throughput across every benchmark GPU count (Figure 2), spanning two model families (dense Llama 3.1, MoE Mixtral 8 ×22B), two precision modes (FP8, BF16), two GPU generations (MI325X gfx942, MI355X gfx950), and FSDP / PP=4-VPP=2 configurations (Tables 1, 3). The Mixtral 8 ×22B result exercises the full pipeline—PP reduction (PP=1 →4), EP A2A modeling, VPP=2 interleaved schedule simulation, and MoE-aware gradient AllReduce decomposition—yet the 8-GPU projection achieves only 1.6% error and the 1-GPU projection (where all communication is modeled analytically) stays within 6.1%; on the dense side, all 12 Llama cells project the 8-node target within ±9%, with Llama 8B FP8 the most stable (Max ∆ = 0.1%) and FSDP-enabled Llama 70B within 8.2% (FP8) and 7.5% (BF16) of measured. For dense Llama the winning strategy is pure data parallelism (TP=PP=CP=1), matching AMD’s published recipe: 8B runs replicated DP, while 70B requires full-shard FSDP2 (ZeRO-3). The Max ∆

column quantifies restoration stability: for configurations with simple analytical stacks (Llama 8B FP8, Llama 70B BF16) the error is nearly independent of benchmark count (Max ∆ ≤ 0.5% ), leaving only a workload-level calibration bias that preserves rank ordering between candidates—the property the Primus Tuning Agent depends on. For configurations that exercise more of the analytical stack (Llama 70B FP8 with FSDP, Mixtral PP=4 VPP=2 EP=8), error decreases as benchmark count grows (Mixtral: +6 .1% at 1 GPU to +1 .6% at 8 GPUs), as analytical components are progressively replaced by direct measurement.

Why contention stays second-order. In benchmark mode the throughput-governing communication is already inside the measurement: the heaviest collective—MoE All-to-All—is intra-node in these recipes (EP ≤ GPUs/node) and directly executed (when EP spans nodes it is modeled analytically instead); pipeline P2P is a single low-volume activation per stage boundary; and DP gradient AllRe-duce is issued during backward and overlapped with compute. The residual is a small additive term, not a first-order error—which is why the 128-node point still lands within 4.7%. For DeepSeek V3 the agent also discovers the throughput-starvation insight, scaling the global batch to the memory ceiling (1,869 → 2,808 tok/s/GPU); the full shape and batch sweeps are in Appendix D.

4.10 MoE Load-Balancing Robustness 

Benchmark runs are balanced by construction. We force load balancing for benchmarking only 1:a random straight-through router plus an exactly even dispatcher make executed load uniform, so the imbalance I = max/mean is 1 and the uniform-load assumption holds exactly. Real training sits close to this: aux-loss (or aux-loss-free) balancing plus large-batch averaging, under which per-expert deviation shrinks as 1/√tokens/expert, gives a steady-state I ≈ 1.1–1.3. When balancing is off , grouped-GEMM time is gated by the busiest EP-rank rather than the mean, and the A2A jumps regime. Sweeping I from 1 × to 4 × on real MI355X ( I ≥ 2 is a deliberate stress test beyond the training regime), a busiest-rank correction (compute gated by I at experts-per-rank E/ EP, plus an A2A regime term) cuts worst-case error from 53–71% to under 10%. On a real trained model 

(DeepSeek V3 671B, native learned balancing) the per-layer imbalance has median 1.38 at EP=8 (2.13 at EP=32), but per-layer spikes fall on different devices, so the step-level pooled imbalance that gates throughput is only 1.05–1.12; at EP=8 the corrected error is ≈2%. Across EP , the penalty depends only on E/ EP, so a fit at one EP predicts the others (Mixtral, EP ∈ { 2, 4, 8}, ±6%). This is what lets us calibrate on the 8-GPU benchmark and project to the 128-node target (Appendix F). 

4.11 Throughput Improvement 

On Mixtral 8 ×22B (4-node MI355X), the agent delivers +27% measured throughput over AMD’s BF16 reference, with ∼25 GPU-min per accepted trial and 7 of 30 trials rejected at zero cost by the memory pre-filter (Section 4.7). At production scale the same workflow selects the measured-optimal recipe end-to-end on DeepSeek V3 671B (1,024 GPU, 4.7% error) and Qwen3-235B-A22B (256 GPU, 1.9% error), confirming the gains hold well beyond the sub-node regime (Section 4.6). 

4.12 Scope and Limitations 

Primus Projection is validated on AMD Instinct GPUs and the ROCm stack, but nothing in the modeling is vendor-specific: hardware profiles are YAML (peak FLOPS, HBM, collective topology) and the microbenchmark runs on any supported GPU ( ∼8 min on 8 GPUs). We have not yet empirically validated on NVIDIA or TPU; porting to NVIDIA requires only a hardware YAML and the 8-GPU benchmark (TPUs also need an XLA collective model). Network contention is not modeled; it stayed second-order in our evaluations (Section 4.6) but could add error in some inter-node EP or sub-node benchmark settings. ZeRO-2 is excluded, and FP8 recipes must be convergence-validated before production. Appendix I covers these, and extensions to new layer types.

95 Related Work 

Analytical performance models. Roofline analysis [32] provides an upper bound on kernel through-put from compute and memory-bandwidth peaks, but ignores fused kernels, pipeline schedules, and multi-dimensional parallelism interactions. Calculon [10] and Galvatron [18] extend this with closed-form analytical models that jointly search parallelism configurations (TP, PP, DP) in milliseconds, enabling co-design sweeps (Galvatron-BMW [31] adds memory balancing). Calculon uses only hardware specifications; Galvatron profiles single-device layer compute but models communication from nominal bandwidth. Neither captures clock-throttling under sustained load, kernel-library drift, or grouped-GEMM wave-quantization in MoE—effects benchmark anchoring resolves automatically. FlexFlow [11] and Alpa [37] require operator- or stage-level profiling on the target cluster. 

Trace-driven and simulation-based approaches. Trace-driven simulators such as ASTRA-sim [25] and Echo/Moye [7] achieve higher fidelity by replaying workload traces but require target-hardware execution traces or extensive calibration; Lumos [15] proposes a toolkit that reaches 3.3% average error on GPT-3 variants on clusters of up to 512 H100 GPUs using trace replay, while ProTrain [34] builds high-fidelity cost models from a runtime profiler to automate memory-management policies, achieving 1.43–2.71 × throughput improvements. Proteus [5] simulates hybrid-parallel strategies with compute–communication overlap but targets CUDA and does not model expert-parallel All-to-All; Vidur [1] simulates LLM inference only. Optimus [23] fits online resource-performance models to estimate training speed as a function of allocated resources, enabling dynamic scheduling in deep learning clusters. These systems are oriented toward profiling and scheduling existing target-cluster runs; in contrast, Primus Projection’s pure-simulation mode runs on CPU and supports pre-silicon design-space exploration without any execution trace from the target, and its hybrid mode requires only a single-node benchmark to project multi-node throughput. 

Hyperparameter optimization: from classical search to LLM agents. TPE [3] and Gaussian-process Bayesian Optimization (BO) [29] showed that surrogate-guided search beats grid search. BO fits LLM-training configuration poorly: it assumes a stationary objective, whereas a 4-byte activation miscount can toggle legality; it treats every trial as an equal-cost black box, whereas many candidates can be rejected analytically before any GPU time; and it ignores cross-axis structure (e.g. the MoE backend interacts only with EP knobs). LLM agents now replace or augment BO: AgentHPO [16] pairs a Creator LLM with an Executor that trains and reports feedback, and LLAMBO [17] frames BO candidate proposal in natural language. Both target model-accuracy objectives; our problem adds a combinatorial feasibility filter and a multi-level surrogate (analytical memory check, then benchmark projection) that neither exploits. 

Configuration tuning for distributed training. DeepSpeed Autotuning [19] grid-searches ZeRO stage and micro-batch on the target cluster but does not consider the joint parallelism shape or the pipeline schedule; FlexFlow [11], Alpa [37], and Galvatron [18] explore parallelism plans with their own cost models but, like the analytical baselines above, lack a benchmark-anchored projection step and do not use an LLM reasoning loop to propose cross-axis combinations. The Primus Tuning Agent is the first, to our knowledge, to couple a benchmark-anchored projection oracle (seconds per candidate, no multi-node cluster required) with an LLM-driven cross-axis investigation loop implemented in DSPy [13] in the spirit of ReAct [35]-style reasoning and recursive tool use [36]. 

## 6 Conclusion 

We presented Primus Projection, a hybrid framework for memory and performance projection of distributed training that benchmarks compute and in-scope communication on as few as one device and analytically reconstructs out-of-scope communication and the full pipeline schedule at the target scale. The Primus Tuning Agent uses it as a fast surrogate to search the joint configuration space end-to-end. Across dense and MoE models on MI325X and MI355X, projections of the validated recipes stay within ∼10% of measured throughput (4.7% at 1,024 GPUs), and the agent delivers +27% over AMD’s Mixtral 4-node BF16 reference in ∼30 minutes of single-node exploration, finding cross-axis combinations no deterministic planner reaches, without multi-node hardware.

# Appendix B: Analytical Models

B.1 Memory Projection 

Memory is estimated using a hierarchical profiler tree that mirrors the model’s module structure. Each profiler implements two methods: one for parameter counts (total or per-GPU with sharding) and one for activation memory per microbatch. 

Parameter and optimizer memory: Per-GPU parameter count accounts for parallelism-induced sharding: TP splits weight matrices; PP assigns layers to stages; EP shards expert parameters while replicating non-expert parameters; CP replicates all parameters; and FSDP/ZeRO shards across DP. With per-parameter byte costs Bw (weight), Bg (gradient), and Bo (optimizer state): bytes_per_param = Bw + Bg + Bo/DP (2) For BF16 mixed-precision with Adam, (Bw, B g , B o) = (2 , 2, 10) (2 B BF16 weight, 2 B BF16/FP32 gradient, 10 B FP32 optimizer state = 4 B master weight + 4 B first moment + 2 B second moment shared) so bytes_per_param = 4 + 10 /DP. Under FP8 mixed precision the weight term drops to 1 B; the distributed-optimizer flag further shards Bo across DP ranks. The projector reads these parameters from the framework configuration so a single equation covers all combinations.

# Appendix C: Primus Tuning Agent Implementation 

C.1 Seed Planner Axis Schedule 

The deterministic seed planner walks a fixed schedule of single-axis sweeps anchored on the user-supplied baseline. Axes are visited in the following order, each producing a small bounded number of candidate configurations: 1. Memory frontier : full → selective → no recomputation, holding all other axes fixed. 2. MoE communication backend : standard A2A vs. DeepEP (only emitted when the model is MoE and EP >1). 3. Pipeline bubble reduction : 1F1B baseline, Interleaved 1F1B with VPP ∈ { 2, 3, 4}, Zero-Bubble, and Sea AI Lab ILP, restricted to schedules that fit the per-layer memory budget. 4. Second-order axes : micro-batch size ∈ { baseline , 1, 2} and the distributed-optimizer flag. For each candidate, the analytic memory pre-filter rejects configurations projected above the cap before any GPU time is consumed. Surviving candidates are scored by the projection oracle and recorded in a shared trial ledger that the LLM phase reads on entry. 

C.2 LLM Investigation Loop 

The LLM phase uses a two-LLM design built on DSPy [13], served through an OpenAI-compatible LiteLLM gateway. The Planner is Claude Sonnet 4.6 ( claude-sonnet-4-6 , ChainOfThought) and the Recursive Language Model (RLM) [36] is Claude Opus 4.6 ( claude-opus-4-6 , ReAct tool loop); we set max_tokens =8000, timeout =300 s, ≤25 candidates/round and ≤4 rounds, with temperature/top-p at DSPy defaults. Verbatim prompts are reproduced in Appendix H. • Planner LLM reads the seed-phase trial ledger (configuration, projected throughput, pro-jected memory, legality, and failure mode where applicable) and emits a hypothesis para-graph together with a JSON list of candidate configurations to evaluate next. • RLM executes those candidates one at a time, calling the projection oracle as a tool, writing notes to a per-round scratchpad, and optionally querying a sub-LLM for deeper analysis of intermediate results. Each round runs up to 25 candidate evaluations; we cap the agent at four rounds, after which the planner is re-prompted with the cumulative ledger. The LLM is permitted to cross-product axes the seed planner treats independently (e.g. recomputation × MoE backend × pipeline schedule), which is the source of the +21.6% advantage over the seed plan reported in Section 4.7. 

C.3 Memory Pre-Filter and Trial Selection 

A trial is legal iff (i) axis validation passes (PP ×DP ×CP ×TP tiles the target cluster), (ii) the projected per-GPU memory satisfies mem ≤ cap = ( HBM − system overhead ) × 0.9, and (iii) the benchmark 15 returns a parseable throughput. The overall winner is arg max( legal , key = tok/s/GPU ). The cap calibration is the basis for the memory pre-filter of Section 4.7, and its predictions agreed perfectly with hardware: every configuration projecting ≤ cap ran successfully on real hardware, every configuration projecting > cap OOMed on the real cluster.

21 Appendix I: Limitations 

Network contention is not modeled. The communication model assumes dedicated bandwidth per topology domain. Concurrent collectives competing for the same xGMI or NIC bandwidth (e.g. TP AllReduce overlapping with FSDP AllGather on the same node) will see effective bandwidth lower than the model assumes. We have not yet observed this as a dominant error source on the configurations evaluated here, but it could add error in some settings at extreme scale. 

Reduced-parallelism fidelity has limits. Sub-node benchmarking necessarily measures a system whose parallelism configuration differs from the target. The +6.6% Mixtral error at the 2-GPU benchmark and the corresponding compounding pattern in Section 4 bound this in practice; longer reductions (e.g. benchmarking PP=4 EP=8 from PP=1 EP=1) introduce more analytical work and correspondingly more residual error. The framework reports both the measured base time and the analytical delta for each restored dimension so that users can audit which step contributed to the residual. 

Hardware homogeneity assumption: All nodes are assumed identical, both in static specification (HBM, CU count, link topology) and in runtime behavior (clock frequency under load). GPU-to-GPU frequency variation within a job and partial-rack failure modes are outside the model’s scope. 

Primus Tuning Agent search space: The deterministic seed planner enumerates a fixed set of axes and the LLM phase explores combinations of those axes; the agent does not introduce optimizations that are not exposed as flags by the projection oracle. Adding a new optimization (e.g. a new MoE A2A backend) requires extending both the oracle and the planner. The search space exposes ZeRO-1 (use_distributed_optimizer ) and ZeRO-3 ( use_torch_fsdp2 ); ZeRO-2 is excluded because wherever it would beat ZeRO-1, FSDP2 is available. 

Precision is a throughput knob only. The agent treats precision (BF16/FP8) as a search axis for throughput evaluation. Projection says nothing about numerical quality: FP8 recipes must be validated for convergence before production use. 

New layer types and accelerators. Extending beyond the evaluated layer types requires only a parameterizable layer: subquadratic layers (e.g. Gated DeltaNet) use a profiled FLOP estimate, and sparse attention a density-weighted FLOP count; validating these is future work. Porting to NVIDIA requires a hardware YAML and the 8-GPU benchmark; TPUs additionally require adapting the collective model to XLA/JAX collectives. 

# Appendix J: Broader Impact 

Primus Projection is an infrastructure tool aimed at reducing the GPU-hours consumed by configura-tion tuning for large model training. The intended positive impact is twofold: • Energy and cost savings. By converting much of the configuration search from multi-node trial-and-error into single-node projection, Primus Projection reduces the GPU-hours required to find a high- throughput configuration. The Mixtral case study in Section 4.7 consumed ∼30 minutes of single-node agent time plus a small number of validation runs, in place of what would otherwise be days of manual multi-node sweeps. • Lower barrier to large-scale training. Smaller research groups without standing access to large clusters can use the simulation backend to explore parallelism trade-offs and the agent to generate candidate configurations, then validate only a few selected candidates on rented capacity. We are not aware of foreseeable negative societal impacts specific to this tooling beyond those of distributed LLM training in general. The framework is released under an open-source license to ensure that the projection oracle, the seed planner, and the LLM agent loop are auditable and modifiable by the community. 22
