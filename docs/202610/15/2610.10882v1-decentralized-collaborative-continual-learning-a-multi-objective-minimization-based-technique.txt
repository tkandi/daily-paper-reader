# Decentralized collaborative continual learning: A multi-objective minimization-based technique 

Yara Zgheib, Student Member, IEEE , Marc Antonini, Member, IEEE , Roula Nassif, Member, IEEE 

Abstract —In this work, we formulate decentralized continual learning within a multi-objective optimization framework. For a given inference task t (corresponding to a common minimizer shared by the cost functions of all agents), agents collecting data in a distributed and streamed manner are only allowed to perform local computations and to exchange information with neighboring agents over the underlying communication graph. As tasks evolve sequentially over time, agents must adapt to newly arriving tasks while retaining knowledge acquired from previously learned ones. This requirement leads to the well-known stability–plasticity dilemma, where stability refers to the ability to retain previous knowledge, while plasticity refers to the ability to learn and adapt to new tasks. To address the stability challenge, agents store subsets of samples from past tasks in local memory buffers. Then, through an appropriate multi-objective formulation, the stored information is incorporated into the learning process so that parameter updates account jointly for the current task and previously learned tasks. The proposed decentralized continual learning approach is analyzed in the mean-square-error sense under general assumptions on the individual cost functions and gradient noise processes. The analysis reveals that cooperation among agents improves the performance of continual learning. In particular, by exchanging information with neighboring agents, decentralized collaborative learning can exploit the diversity of locally observed data and memory buffers to improve the network average mean-square deviation (MSD) across tasks. Finally, simulations illustrate the theoretical findings and the effectiveness of the method in reducing forgetting and improving the average MSD across tasks. 

Index Terms —Decentralized learning, diffusion adaptation, continual learning, gradient noise, mean-square-error stability. 

I. I NTRODUCTION 

The increasing amount of distributed data generated by applications such as smartphones, drones, autonomous vehi-cles, and IoT systems, arriving continuously with evolving distributions over time, has created a need for scalable learning algorithms capable of adapting to dynamic environments. Although this data presents opportunities for advancing intelli-gent systems, it also raises major challenges. Learning models must preserve privacy, operate under limited computational and communication ressources, and continuously adapt to changing data distributions without costly re-learning from scratch or forgetting previously acquired knowledge. These requirements motivate the combination of continual learning 

and decentralized learning , two research directions that have traditionally been studied independently.

In decentralized learning (also known as distributed learn-ing ), multiple agents (referred to as nodes, clients, or de-vices) collaborate to learn either a shared global model or multiple related personalized models without relying on a central server [2]–[7]. By keeping data on local devices and exchanging only model updates during communication with neighboring agents, decentralized learning reduces the risk of sharing sensitive data. Various cooperation rules have been developed to enable effective collaboration among agents, including incremental [8], [9], consensus [10], [11], and diffusion approaches [2], [12]–[14]. In this work, we adopt diffusion strategies because of their scalability, robustness and ability to support continuous adaptation and learning. Further-more, when constant step-sizes are employed to enable con-tinuous adaptation, these strategies have been shown to have superior stability and performance compared to consensus-based implementations [2], [3], [15], [16]. In continual learning , on the other hand, the goal is to emulate how humans adapt to new experiences while retaining previously acquired knowledge. Also known as incremental learning or life-long learning , this approach addresses the challenge of learning from a sequence of evolving tasks while retaining performance on previously learned tasks [17], [18]. Its effectiveness has been proven in various domains, including autopilot systems [19], emotion recognition [20], and Internet of Things (IoT) [21], where data arrive sequentially and their distributions change over time. Such distribution shifts are also common in medical image analysis, where the underlying data distribution evolves due to changes in patient popula-tions, disease characteristics, imaging devices, and acquisition protocols. Consequently, clinical models must incorporate new diagnostic information without compromising previously acquired knowledge, since retraining from scratch is often infeasible and naive fine-tuning may result in catastrophic forgetting [22]. Similar challenges arise in embodied vision applications, where mobile robots and wearable assistants in-teract with dynamic environments. As novel object categories emerge and past observations reoccur over time, these systems must continuously acquire new visual knowledge while main-taining existing recognition capabilities. This highlights the importance of continual learning for long-term autonomous adaptation [23]. Continual learning aims to overcome a major limitation of traditional learning systems, namely, catastrophic forgetting ,where learning new tasks (or models) often degrades per-formance on previously learned tasks [24], [25]. Strategies for mitigating catastrophic forgetting are generally grouped into three categories: i) regularization, ii )

architecture, and iii ) rehearsal. Regularization-based methods incorporate explicit regularization terms that constrain changes to parameters important for previously learned tasks [27], [28]. Notable examples include Elastic Weight Consolidation (EWC) [24], and EWC++, an efficient online variant [18]. Regularization-based methods preserve privacy by avoiding data storage, but may limit adaptation as constraints ac-cumulate, making it harder to learn new tasks effectively. Architecture-based methods are more appropriate for scenarios where there are no constraint on the model size. They identify and preserve task-specific parameters while expanding the network with new parameter branches to accommodate new knowledge [29], [30]. Rehearsal-based methods store a small subset of past training samples in a memory buffer and update the model using both new data and stored samples. The buffer is continuously updated with incoming mini-batches [31]. Among these methods is the Gradient Projection Memory (GPM) approach, which allows a neural network to learn new tasks, while constraining gradient updates to remain orthogonal to subspaces associated with previously learned tasks [32]. Another approach is Gradient Episodic Memory (GEM), which constrains the updates for the current task so that the loss on previously seen tasks (approximated using samples stored in an episodic-memory) does not increase [33], [34]. In online continual learning, memory buffer management has been addressed through gradient-based selection [31], reservoir sampling [35], and exemplar-based methods such as iCaRL [36]. In systems where multiple agents or devices are required to perform continual learning, decentralized learning pro-vides a natural framework for exploiting the distributed data available across agents without directly sharing raw data. Through the exchange of local model updates or parameter estimates, agents can collaboratively adapt to evolving data distributions while benefiting from the knowledge acquired by neighboring agents. Despite its potential, this direction has only recently started to receive attention in the literature. For instance, [37] introduces CoDeC, a Communication-efficient Decentralized Continual learning framework that combines stochastic gradient descent with gossip averaging to learn from spatially distributed private data, while employing the Gradient Projection Memory (GPM) approach to enable incremental learning. Similarly, [38] investigates how the COmmunication-efficient COordinate Ascent (CoCoA) framework can support continual learning through both analytical characterization and numerical experiments. This work considers the domain-incremental setting, where tasks share the same structure but differ in their input distributions. In the current work, we consider a network of agents exposed to tasks arriving sequentially over time–see Fig. 1 for an illustration. The objective is to perform online continual learning, where each agent observes a stream of data samples and processes each sample only once, except for a limited subset retained in a local memory buffer [18]. We formulate in Sec. II a global multi-objective minimization problem that balances learning new tasks and retaining performance on pre-vious tasks, and propose a diffusion-based method to solve it in a fully decentralized manner. We analyze in Sec. III its mean-square-error stability to understand how various factors, such as data characteristics, learning step-size, and regularization balance between plasticity and (knowledge) stability, affect the performance of the approach. We investigate in Sec. IV the impact of collaboration on the average mean-square deviation (MSD) across tasks. Since the average MSD quantifies the 3

distance between the current estimate and the optimal solutions of current and past tasks, reducing it improves both adaptation and knowledge retention. We show that collaboration among agents leads to lower average MSD than the non-cooperative setting, where each agent performs continual learning inde-pendently. This gain results from exploiting data diversity and collective experience across the network, which improve the stability-plasticity trade-off. To the best of our knowledge, this work is among the first to provide a theoretical analysis of con-tinual learning approaches . Finally, the simulations in Sec. V illustrate the theoretical mean-square-error results and show the gains of cooperation in terms of average forgetting and MSD across tasks. Experiments on the real-world FEMNIST dataset [39] further illustrate the effectiveness of the approach.

Although Algorithm 1 follows the standard form of diffusion ATC for minimizing aggregate sum of individual risks, its analysis cannot rely on existing results in the literature of diffusion adaptation [2]. The main difficulty arises from the randomness of the empirical-risk component in Jmulti

In standard analyses, the mean-square error is typically studied through quantities of the form, E∥wo −wk,i ∥2, where the global target minimizer wo is deterministic. In the present setting, however, the target optimizer itself becomes random since it depends on the randomly retained samples. Consequently, the mean-square-error analysis must be revisited to explicitly account for this additional source of randomness.

B. Simulation results with FEMNIST 

In this section, we evaluate the performance of Algorithm 1 on the FEMNIST dataset [39], a federated version of the Extended MNIST (EMNIST) dataset [43], where the samples are partitioned according to the writer of each handwritten character. In this setting, each writer is treated as a separate client, naturally yielding a distributed learning scenario in which every agent has access only to its own local dataset. A characteristic of this dataset is that the data distribution across clients is not independent and identically distributed (non-IID). Since the samples are grouped by writer, different clients may exhibit distinct writing styles and different class proportions . In particular, the number of samples belonging to each character class (e.g., digits such as “ 2”or “ 3” or letters such as “a” or “b”) can vary from one client to another. As learning tasks, we consider binary letter–digit classification problems. Each sample corresponds to a grayscale image of size 28 ×28 representing a handwritten character. C ONCLUSION 

We considered decentralized continual learning under streaming data, where multiple agents aim to estimate tasks evolving over time. To address the stability-plasticity dilemma, we formulated a multi-objective optimization problem for estimating a parameter vector that performs well across tasks. We then proposed a decentralized approach and established its mean-square-error stability. The analysis reveals that cooper-ation can provide benefits in continual learning by improving (knowledge) stability and reducing catastrophic forgetting.
