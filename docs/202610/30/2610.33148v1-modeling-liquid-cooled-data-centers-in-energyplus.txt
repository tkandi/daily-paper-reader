## Abstract 

As AI workloads accelerate data center transition toward high-density racks with direct-to-chip (DTC) liquid cooling, existing simulation tools remain limited in evaluating whole-building and multi-year efficiency. To bridge this gap, we develop a native Energy-Plus object, ElectricEquipment:ITE:LiquidCooled , that models DTC liquid cooling for chilled-water and warm-water architectures as plant-loop components. It couples facility-water loops, rack-side secondary loops, coolant distribution units, and chip-junction tem-peratures within a single solver. Validation against an independent Modelica implementation across 21 operating conditions, includ-ing two full-year runs compared at one-minute resolution, yields loop-temperature RMSE at or below 0.021 K, well within the 0.1 K ac-ceptance threshold. Our case study demonstrates that warm-water liquid cooling reduces annual PUE from 1.43 to 1.25, eliminates on-site water use, and cuts total facility energy by 37.9% relative to an air-cooled baseline. By embedding liquid-cooling logic into EnergyPlus, our work provides whole-building, long-term energy modeling for high-density AI infrastructure within an open-source framework.

## 1 Introduction 

Global data center energy consumption is rising at an unprece-dented rate necessitating modeling to identify sources of energy waste. Estimates indicate that data center energy use could con-tribute to 9.5-15.3% of U.S. electricity consumption by 2030 [33]. In parallel, the scale of new data center campuses is also increasing as over 50% are predicted to exceed 500 MW by 2035, and nearly one-third to exceed 1 GW [7]. Therefore, modeling data centers holistically can identify optimal building strategies that lower their power demands and make them more energy efficient and flexible. As data centers continue expanding to meet growing artificial intelligence (AI) demands, new liquid cooling methodologies need to be explored and deployed. Traditional air cooling uses computer room air handler (CRAH) units to circulate and cool warm air through server rack arrangements. Liquid cooling, by contrast, cir-culates coolant directly through components to absorb and dissipate heat. These approaches differ significantly in their suitability for

supporting AI demands. Traditional air-cooled server racks operate at 7-10 kW, while AI computing racks can reach 30-100+ kW per rack [12, 23]. AI compute chips in these racks can reach heat fluxes up to 106 W /cm 2 [28], making air cooled systems no longer viable given the high power density [27]. Tools capable of modeling data-center liquid cooling at the build-ing level already exist. The Modelica Buildings library [44] sup-ports high-fidelity simulation of liquid-cooling equipment along-side its own thermal-zone and envelope models, and can addi-tionally be coupled to the EnergyPlus engine through Spawn-of-EnergyPlus [43]. Its use at the facility scale nevertheless remains limited in practice. Modelica has a steep learning curve despite decades of software development and focuses more on equipment-level components, limiting its usage for multi-year facility scale runs [26]. Therefore, it remains challenging to assess how liquid cooling interacts with the data center design. Extending the model-ing capabilities at the facility level, using tools such as EnergyPlus, could allow for identification of how liquid cooling interacts with heat recovery systems, IT workloads, and grid-level renewable en-ergy. These interactions determine facility-level efficiency metrics such as power usage effectiveness (PUE), water usage effective-ness (WUE), carbon usage effectiveness (CUE), and energy use efficiency (EUE) [14]. EnergyPlus, a dominant building energy mod-eling software, is well suited for data center facility-level analyses. EnergyPlus supports both auditing whole building simulation for existing data centers and thereby the planning of future ones. It contains a mature library of plant-side equipment models, includ-ing curve-based chillers, cooling towers, dry coolers, pumps, and plant-loop controls. As our work demonstrates, these EnergyPlus components can be reused to serve a liquid-cooled IT load directly rather than being re-implemented. However, EnergyPlus lacks the liquid-cooled IT load object required to interface with these equip-ment models. In this work, we develop a scalable liquid cooling object for En-ergyPlus. We validate the object against an independent Modelica implementation to assess its accuracy in modeling liquid cooling configurations. ## 2 Background 2.1 EnergyPlus for Data Centers 

EnergyPlus is an open-source building energy simulation engine developed primarily by the U.S. Department of Energy (DOE), cur-rently maintained by the National Laboratory of the Rockies, that models thermal dynamics, heating, ventilation, and air conditioning (HVAC), and energy flows across various building systems. Oper-ating on a nodal framework under the assumption of well-mixed zone air, it traditionally simulates multi-zone energy performance at hourly or sub-hourly timesteps. While the DOE provides a suite of Commercial Prototype Building Models representing major com-mercial building types [38], data centers were long absent from these standardized reference models. To fill this critical gap, Sun et al. [35] introduced prototype data center energy models to enable holistic, scalable evaluations of data center cooling efficiency within a standardized building energy modeling environment. Specifically, they modified the ITE object in EnergyPlus, to feature a dedicated flow control mechanism to support the thermal dynamics of air-cooled data center spaces. This air-cooled IT equipment (ITE) object incorporates supply and re-turn approach temperature differences to account for supply air bypass, hot exhaust recirculation, and air containment management strategies. By adjusting the effective supply air volume and return temperature based on these approach temperature differences, En-ergyPlus captures realistic air distribution and temperature offsets without violating the underlying nodal heat balance framework. This integration facilitates accurate energy modeling and compar-ative evaluations across diverse air-cooled data center topologies, cooling strategies, and efficiency standards. The impact of that object, however, extends beyond the simula-tion capability it added, as it established the air-cooled data center as an openly accessible modeling foundation for subsequent research. Because it brings data-center thermal behavior into EnergyPlus, the object can be used with established building-energy toolchains (e.g., OpenStudio, DesignBuilder, Honeybee) and co-simulation in-terfaces, placing data-center research within whole-building and district-scale energy analysis [39]. A growing body of work builds on it, including the physics-based and data-driven modeling of data-center energy, water, and thermal dynamics across alterna-tive cooling configurations [1, 10], the development of predictive and reinforcement learning based control strategies [6, 11, 21, 46], and the construction of open benchmark testbeds for sustainable data-center operation [22]. Building upon these air-cooled modeling foundations, in this work, we develop a liquid-cooled ITE object in EnergyPlus to ex-tend its capabilities toward high-density, liquid-cooled data center architectures. 

## 2.2 Existing tools 

Existing simulation environments that model data-center liquid cooling can be organized along two axes, spatial scale (spanning from chip to loop, facility, and grid) and temporal horizon (microsec-onds to decades), yielding the four scales summarized in Table 1. This work contributes primarily at the building/facility scale by en-abling long-term, including annual and multi-year, whole-building simulation of DTC liquid cooling in EnergyPlus. Each tier is served by a mature tool family, but no single tier alone can express the whole-building, long-term planning under various scenarios that motivates this work. At the component level , computational fluid dynamics (CFD) tools such as ANSYS Fluent/Icepak [2], Siemens FloTHERM [32], Cadence Reality DC [8], and OpenFOAM [24] resolve chip, cold-plate, and rack thermofluidics at sub-second timesteps. They are the standard for cold-plate and rack-layout design verification, but their computational cost restricts them to short transient windows and precludes annual or multi-year facility studies. At the loop level , one-dimensional thermo-fluid network tools, including Simcenter Amesim [31], Simscape Fluids [36], and, most prominently for buildings research, the Modelica ecosystem via the Lawrence Berkeley National Lab (LBNL) Buildings library [44], resolve the dynamic response of pumps, valves, heat exchangers, 

> 2

and controllers. This tier is well suited for plant-level control se-quence tuning and equipment cycling, but scaling it to facility-level analysis is costly, owing to stiff hybrid differential-algebraic equa-tion (DAE) integration, state events from equipment switching, and the translation overhead of detailed envelope models [43, 44]. As a result, annual runs of a fully coupled Modelica envelope-plus-plant model are practical for targeted design and control studies, whereas load-based workflows remain faster and better supported for regulatory analysis [43]. At the building level , the tier occupied by whole-building energy simulators including EnergyPlus [13] and TRNSYS [34], thermal, electrical, water, and carbon flows are solved in a single engine at one-minute to multi-year horizons. This is the only tier at which the efficiency metrics that govern data-center design and reporting are physically defined, since each is an integral over the facility boundary rather than a loop- or component-local quantity. It is also the tier referenced by building codes and rating systems such as ASHRAE Standard 90.4 [3], LEED [41], and California Title 24 [9]. Modelica-based platforms nominally reach this tier through the Buildings library [44] and the Spawn-of-EnergyPlus coupling [43], but their application at the whole-facility scale remains largely research-grade rather than standard engineering practice due to the computational and regulatory constraints noted above. At the grid level , tools such as OpenDSS [15], GridLAB-D [25], PLEXOS [16], and HOMER Pro [37] provide the external context of tariffs, emissions factors, and demand-response signals, but they abstract each facility as a scheduled or constant load and cannot resolve internal cooling behavior. Two consequences follow. First, the tools that best resolve liquid-cooling physics are confined to design-tier horizons, which is why liquid-cooled equipment is well characterized at the component and loop levels but essentially absent from operational, regulatory, and carbon-accounting workflows for whole facilities. As recently high-lighted by Ganesh et al. [17], this temporal and spatial confinement forces the industry to rely on siloed evaluations that isolate single metrics like energy efficiency (e.g., PUE) while neglecting critical trade-offs within the broader energy-water-carbon nexus and dy-namic thermal compliance over annual weather cycles. Second, among building-tier simulators, EnergyPlus is the tool most tightly coupled to plant, envelope, zone air, weather, on-site generation, and grid-emission inputs in a single open-source solver, and it is the reference engine for the code frameworks that govern data-center compliance. Adding a native liquid-cooling object to EnergyPlus therefore fills the specific tier-level gap identified in Table 1, rather than duplicating capability that already exists elsewhere. 

## 2.3 Liquid cooling architectures 

Data-center liquid cooling is commonly classified by how close the coolant is brought to the heat source [5]. In liquid-to-room systems, chilled water feeds CRAH units or in-row coolers that condition room air before it reaches unmodified servers. In liquid-to-rack 

systems, rear-door heat exchangers absorb server exhaust heat at the rack boundary. In liquid-to-chip systems, more commonly called direct-to-chip (DTC), cold plates are bonded directly to central processing units (CPUs) and graphics processing units (GPUs), with residual air cooling handling lower-power components. Bringing the coolant closer to the die progressively raises the allowable coolant temperature and heat-flux density, often at the cost of greater server modification. DTC has emerged as the industry standard for the high-density racks that AI workloads require, and immersion, although attractive in theoretical efficiency, is constrained by cost and the difficulty of retrofitting existing air-cooled halls [45]. The operating temper-ature envelope of any liquid-cooled plant is codified by the 2021 ASHRAE thermal guidelines for liquid cooling [4], which partition facility water supply temperatures into six classes (Table 2). for warm-water designs that leverage free-cooling and heat-reuse potential. These two classes bracket the design space in which the proposed model is validated and applied. 

## 3 Method 

To model DTC liquid cooling of IT equipment within a whole-building energy simulation, we develop a custom EnergyPlus object, 

ElectricEquipment:ITE:LiquidCooled , as a native plant-loop component. Throughout our modeling process we focus on single-phase DTC cooling due to its operational maturity and widespread commercial adoption [45]. Whereas the air-cooled object rejects all IT heat to the zone air, the liquid-cooled object registers on the demand side of an Energy-Plus PlantLoop , the Facility Water System (FWS), so that the heat captured by the cold plates is delivered directly to the building’s chilled- or warm-water plant and handled by the plant’s supply-side equipment (e.g., chiller, cooling tower, or dry cooler). The added cooling path is deliberately quasi-steady and algebraic: at each zone timestep the object evaluates a closed-form chain of energy balances rather than iterating a discretized coolant network, keep-ing the object numerically robust and cheap at annual sub-hourly resolution while still resolving the temperatures that govern DTC feasibility. It is decomposed into three sequentially solved stages: first, a heat-partition step that splits the processor heat between coolant and room air; second, a primary FWS loop modeled as a real plant branch; and third, an algebraic rack-side secondary loop and CDU. Since the object is defined as a demand-side component on the FWS loop, facility-level heat rejection is automatically han-dled by EnergyPlus’s existing plant infrastructure. ## 3.5 Efficiency metrics calculation

## 4 Validation 4.1 Setup 

To verify the correctness of the newly developed EnergyPlus object 

ElectricEquipment:ITE:LiquidCooled , which models a DTC-cooled IT load together with its CDU and facility-water interface, we cross-validate it against the widely adopted Modelica Buildings 

library [44] (LBNL Buildings 13.0.0, solved in OpenModelica 1.26.3). We developed the Modelica model independently as an equation-based, component-level reconstruction of the same plant, reproduc-ing the governing relations (IT heat balance, CDU approach, and loop energy balances) in a mature simulator solved by a different numerical method. Agreement between the two implementations therefore supports the correctness of the EnergyPlus object rather than merely its internal self-consistency.

The facility-water supply temperature is varied from 12 to 45 °C, reaching the defining maximum of every ASHRAE liquid-cooling class up to W45 (Table 2); the rack-coolant flow is varied to give rack temperature rises from 10.3 to 41.0 K; the CDU approach from 4 to 10 K; the facility-water flow from half to twice its design value (0.097 m 3 s-1 ); and the IT load from zero to the full 2,143 kW de-sign power. Two of the conditions are realized as complete plants and simulated over a full year: a chilled-water plant in which an electric chiller backed by a wet cooling tower supplies 12 °C water, and a warm-water plant in which a dry cooler rejects the liquid load directly to ambient air at 45 °C (Table 4). In the remaining 19 conditions the facility-water boundary condition is imposed directly, which is what allows each design parameter to be varied independently and over a range no single heat-rejection plant could deliver. All conditions share the same data hall and the same IT side, simulated using the Typical Meteorological Year 3 (TMY3) weather dataset for Tampa, FL, USA, provided by EnergyPlus. The two an-nual runs are driven by the IT-utilization schedule of the DOE prototype data center model [35] (Figure 3); detailed parameters are summarized in Table 4. The air-side heat-leakage fraction is set to 0.05 in both models so that the liquid heat load is identical. The 5 % air portion itself lies outside the validation scope because the Modelica model represents only the liquid side, while Energy-Plus removes this heat from the zone with an idealized air system (ZoneHVAC:IdealLoadsAirSystem ) that supplies exactly the cool-ing required without modeling any fans, coils, or chillers. Neither model therefore contains an air-side equipment model to compare. Matching the fraction nonetheless ensures the comparison isolates the liquid cooling plant rather than confounding it with a differing heat-split assumption. The two full-plant conditions were simulated over a full year in both tools. EnergyPlus ran at a one-minute timestep and reported every plant variable at that resolution, whereas the Modelica replica was integrated as a single continuous annual run with a variable-step solver (relative tolerance 10 −6, cold-initialized once at the start of the year) and written out on the same one-minute grid. The validation is therefore carried out at a one-minute timestep over the full year. The Modelica models use Buildings.Media. Buildings.Media.Water , so that density follows temperature as it does in EnergyPlus. The Modelica warm-water plant is cold-initialized once at the start of the January-1 run, whereas EnergyPlus begins from its converged annual state. Its facility-water controller therefore needs about the first hour of the year to settle from this cold start, produc-ing a start-up transient that is absent in EnergyPlus. We exclude the first hour of the year from the comparison for this reason, leav-ing 525,541 paired samples per variable. In the chilled-water plant the supply temperature is set directly by the chiller rather than through a controller loop, so it has no such start-up transient. The first hour is nevertheless dropped from both plants, so that the two comparisons rest on an identical set of samples. Sweeping the facility-water setpoint inside a complete plant is not viable: the dry cooler cannot hold a 32 °C supply against Tampa summer air, and driving the electric chiller to such a leaving-water temperature evaluates its performance curves far outside the range over which they were fitted, so an equipment limitation would surface as a residual of the object under test. EnergyPlus reads the facility-water supply temperature from the plant node, which its solver drives to the 45 °C setpoint quasi-statically within each timestep, so the delivered temperature tracks the setpoint without controller lag. The Modelica plant instead re-solves the dry-cooler fan proportional–integral (PI) loop explicitly (integral time 120 s). At each midnight IT-load step the heat rejected to the loop changes abruptly, and the finite-rate controller briefly overcools the water before the supply temperature recovers expo-nentially, returning to within 0.1 K of the setpoint in under 7 min. These excursions occur only at the load steps: every one-minute sample whose error exceeds 0.1 K falls on one of the 31 days on which the utilization schedule changes level, and the chilled-water plant, whose setpoint is enforced directly by the chiller, shows no such transient at all. The deviation is therefore not a plant-physics disagreement but a consequence of the two tools’ modeling scope. EnergyPlus idealizes the plant as tracking its setpoint instanta-neously each timestep, whereas the Modelica model captures the finite-rate response of the physical fan controller.

## 4.4 Validity and scope 

Over 21 operating conditions, covering every ASHRAE liquid-cooling class from W17 to W45, rack-coolant temperature rises of 10 to 41 K, CDU approaches of 4 to 10 K, facility-water flows from half to twice design, and IT loads from zero to full, every sustained temperature residual is at or below 0.030 K. Each of these residuals has an identified cause in the Modelica reference model, either the constant specific heat it uses or the dry-cooler fan con-troller it resolves explicitly where EnergyPlus idealizes the plant. Neither is an error in the EnergyPlus object under test. The validation does not cover the zone-air model or the routing of air-side heat leakage, which in the production models is larger and is handled by a CRAH outside the liquid-side comparison. It also does not cover the heat-rejection plant and its electricity for chillers, pumps, and fans, or full annual energy integrals. The CDU is modeled at a fixed, user-specified approach rather than with a load-dependent effectiveness, the secondary rack loop is represented algebraically at constant mass flow, and all runs use a single climate.

Implementing the liquid-cooled ob-ject natively in EnergyPlus therefore places hybrid air- and liquid-cooled data centers within the whole-building workflows (envelope, weather, schedules, sizing, and code-compliance modeling) in which the program is already established. The two liquid-cooled architectures serve the CRAH coil differ-ently. In the warm-water model the 45 °C facility water is too warm to make cold supply air, so a small air-cooled chiller feeds a separate room chilled-water loop that serves the coil. In the chilled-water model the CRAH coil is placed directly on the main chilled-water loop, because the main chiller already delivers cold water, so no extra chiller is needed. As shown in Figure 7, the chilled-water and warm-water liquid cooling systems show significant advantages over the air-cooled baseline for all key metrics calculated. To summarize the trends between the air-cooled baseline and warm water loop, the annual PUE drops from 1.43 to 1.25 as the WUE goes from 3.45 to 0 and EUE increases from 0.50 to 0.80, or to 1.44 if the liquid-loop heat is fully reused (Figure 7 a-c). For reference, the air-cooled baseline PUE is close to the 2023 U.S. average PUE of approximately 1.4 [30], and both liquid-cooled cases fall within the range of approximately 1.07 to 1.33 simulated for a direct liquid-cooled facility [18]. Re-ductions in site-level water use follow from avoiding evaporative heat rejection [20]. The full-reuse EUE value assumes 100% reuse of the liquid-loop heat extraction (equivalent to approximately 80% of the IT input) as recoverable waste heat. In practice the usable fraction depends on the temperature at which the heat is delivered and on the requirements of the downstream application, so these values represent a theoretical upper bound for applications such as district heating rather than an expected yield. Additionally, we observe that the peak PUE drops from 1.68 to 1.48 from the air cooled baseline to the warm water loop for liquid cooling. The warm water loop achieves this sharp reduction by eliminating the chiller plant and cooling tower from the liquid loop, allowing for efficient heat dissipation through dry cooling to occur. Lastly, we observe that the total facility energy reduces by 37.9% (23,633 to 14,668 MWh/yr) and cooling overhead drops by 65.1% (6,100 to 2,132 MWh/yr), confirming the high energy efficiency gains from DTC liquid cooling. Of the 8,964 MWh/yr total reduction, 53.1 % comes from the IT side itself, as DTC cooling removes the server-fan power that the air-cooled baseline requires, 44.3 % from the cooling plant, and the remaining 2.7 % from the correspondingly smaller UPS losses. Interestingly, the warm-water system does not outperform the chilled-water system across all hours. The warm-water hourly PUE is higher than that of the chilled-water system for 785 hours of the year (9.0%), exclusively at the minimum 25% IT load and in cool, dry weather (mean wet-bulb 12.3 °C compared with 19.0 °C across all 25%-load hours), where the dry cooler’s benefit scales down with the liquid heat while the chilled-water plant’s water-cooled chiller is at its most efficient. Furthermore, the air-cooled chiller serving the room load in the warm-water plant operates at a lower COP than the water-cooled chiller in the chilled-water plant, and this trade-off would not occur if both configurations shared the same chiller efficiency. The gap in those hours never exceeds 0.023 in PUE. Both directions of this trade-off are climate-driven. Tampa’s hot, humid summer limits the free-cooling potential of the dry cooler relative to colder regions, while its mild winter is what produces the crossover hours at all, so the margin between the two liquid-cooled configurations should be expected to shift with location. Evaluating both configurations across a range of ASHRAE climate zones is left to future work. 

## 6 Discussion and Limitations 

The primary contribution of this work is a validated object for whole-building simulation of DTC liquid cooling inside EnergyPlus. The custom ElectricEquipment:ITE:LiquidCooled object em-beds the distinctive physics of a two-loop DTC system inside the same solver that already handles the building envelope, zone-air balance, and central plant. It represents the facility-water primary loop, the rack-side secondary loop, the CDU that couples them, the liquid/air split of the chip heat, and the junction-to-coolant thermal resistance. Liquid-cooled data centers can therefore be simulated na-tively in EnergyPlus at the same whole-building level of resolution previously available only for air-cooled data centers. This makes it possible to study a broader range of cooling-system configurations with high fidelity in applications such as data-center siting and grid coordination, alongside conventional air-cooled designs within a common modeling framework. Because the object is now available inside EnergyPlus, the val-idated plant-level results support a broad class of building- and facility-level studies. These include design-point sizing of DTC cooling plants, annual energy comparisons of alternative plant configurations across climate zones and data-center sizes, and su-pervisory scheduling of IT load at hourly or coarser timescales where each interval can be treated as quasi-steady. Beyond these, the object also exposes the plant-side thermal and flow quantities needed to compute a broader set of efficiency met-rics beyond the widely used PUE, including WUE and the recently proposed EUE [14, 33]. Because EUE credits reused thermal energy, this provides a common basis on which emerging technologies such as waste-heat recovery and district-heat coupling can be quanti-tatively compared against conventional designs. Combined with EnergyPlus’s standard plant components and time-varying grid emission factors, the object further supports sustainability analyses and can serve as a physically grounded input to upstream grid, siting, and scheduling tools. Additionally, although our current models use constant facility-water temperature setpoints, Energy-Plus natively supports dynamic temperature and load-based control strategies. Our liquid cooling module can therefore be applied for both data center planning and supervisory control of cooling oper-ations. Despite these benefits, the proposed liquid-cooled object has limitations that bound its validated use. First, the CDU and the rack-side hydraulics are represented algebraically at a single de-sign point. The coolant delivery temperature is set from a fixed, user-specified CDU approach temperature difference rather than a load-dependent heat exchanger. The secondary coolant flow rate is held constant, and the chip-to-coolant path is collapsed into a single fixed junction-to-coolant resistance rather than a resolved cold-plate model. Because the approach of a real CDU scales with the heat it transfers, a fixed approach over-predicts it at part load

and biases the rack-coolant and junction temperatures upward. The formulation also cannot represent heterogeneous rack populations or variable secondary-pump operation. Second, the object is steady-state and omits thermal mass and hydraulic dynamics, so it does not resolve the transient response of the liquid loop, as the load-step comparison of Section 4 illustrates. It is therefore unsuited to transient or real-time control studies, or to pre-cooling strate-gies that depend on the capacitance of the loop itself. Third, the reported validation covers only the liquid-side coupling between the two models. The zone air balance lies outside the scope of this comparison, and the object’s air-side heat leakage path relies on existing EnergyPlus objects. Last but certainly not least, the case study covers a single data center in one location. The validation itself spans a wide range of operating conditions, but every run uses the same weather file, so the energy and efficiency metric results reported here are specific to Tampa’s climate. Future studies should extend the case study across climate zones, where the trade-off between warm-water and chilled-water cooling is expected to be most sensitive. 

## 7 Conclusion 

Our work adds the ability to model liquid-cooled data centers in En-ergyPlus through introducing a new object, ElectricEquipment: ITE:LiquidCooled , for both chilled-water and warm-water DTC cooling architectures. We validate the object against an indepen-dently developed Modelica model with matching parameters across 21 operating conditions: full-year chilled-water (W17) and warm-water (W45) plants compared at one-minute resolution, and 19 fur-ther conditions spanning facility-water supply temperatures from 12 to 45 °C, rack-coolant flow, CDU approach, and facility-water flow. Loop-temperature RMSE stays at or below 0.021 K throughout, well inside the 0.1 K acceptance threshold, confirming fidelity for whole-building simulation. In a case study for a mid-sized data center in Tampa, FL, USA, the warm-water loop reduced PUE from 1.43 to 1.25, eliminated on-site water use, and cut total facility en-ergy by 37.9% relative to an air-cooled baseline. By embedding liquid-cooling logic directly in EnergyPlus, this contribution ex-tends whole-building energy modeling practice in both academia and industry as data center infrastructure is rapidly shifting toward DTC liquid cooling systems for AI data centers, all within a single open-source framework.
