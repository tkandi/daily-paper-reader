# Trustworthy Distributed Machine Learning in Cloud-Edge-IoT Systems: A Heterogeneity-Aware Survey of Security, Privacy, and Robustness 

Long Dang ∗, Ali Alfatemi ∗, Kaiqi Xiong, Senior Member, IEEE, Mohammed Aledhari, Senior Member, IEEE, 

Abdellah Chehri ∗, Senior Member, IEEE, Shawqi Al-Maliki, and Mohamed Rahouti, Member, IEEE 

Abstract —Distributed machine learning (DML), including con-ventional distributed training and federated learning (FL), en-ables collaborative model development across cloud-edge-IoT systems without fully centralizing raw data. However, distributing data, computation, updates, and orchestration increases the attack surface and introduces coupled challenges across privacy, robustness, reliability, fairness, and governance. This survey presents a security-centered, heterogeneity-aware roadmap for trustworthy DML, with FL treated as a prominent high-risk case. We first characterize the assets, adversaries, trust assump-tions, and coordination patterns that define DML/FL security boundaries. We then analyze how statistical, system, and trust heterogeneity, including non-IID data, resource asymmetry, inter-mittent participation, and cross-silo/cross-device trust differences, affect convergence, fairness, attack detectability, and defense reliability. Next, we synthesize attack-defense interactions across poisoning, backdoors, Byzantine manipulation, privacy leakage, model extraction, network/orchestration attacks, Sybil behavior, and strategic participation. We further compare layered defenses, including robust aggregation, drift-aware optimization, differ-ential privacy, secure aggregation, homomorphic encryption, secure multiparty computation, trusted execution environments, watermarking, provenance, authenticated communication, and monitoring. Finally, we discuss deployment, evaluation, gover-nance, and open research guidelines, emphasizing reproducible, non-independent and identically distributed (non-IID) parti-tions, attack-aware benchmarks, privacy budgets, attack success rate, client-level disparity, latency, communication, memory, energy/carbon overhead, and auditable lifecycle artifacts. 

Index Terms —Distributed machine learning, federated learn-ing, cloud-edge-IoT systems, security, privacy, robustness, non-IID data, heterogeneity, trusted execution, secure aggregation.

I. I NTRODUCTION 

# DISTRIBUTED machine learning (DML) has become a core paradigm for training models across cloud, edge, and Internet of Things (IoT) environments. In these settings, data are often generated by heterogeneous devices, organiza-tions, sensors, and embedded platforms, making centralized collection costly, privacy-sensitive, or infeasible. DML, includ-ing conventional distributed training and federated learning (FL), enables collaborative model development while keep-ing computation and data distributed across resource-diverse participants [1]–[3]. However, distributing learning also expands the attack sur-face. Model updates, gradients, aggregation logic, communica-tion channels, client identities, and orchestration infrastructure may all become targets. These risks are amplified in IoT-oriented deployments, where devices often operate with lim-ited compute, energy, and bandwidth resources, and with lim-ited security hardening. In addition, statistical heterogeneity, especially non-independent and identically distributed (non-IID) data, can induce client drift, slow convergence, widen client-level performance disparities, and weaken assumptions behind many robust aggregation and privacy-preserving mech-anisms [3], [4]. As a result, poisoning, backdoor insertion, Byzantine update manipulation, gradient leakage, model ex-traction, and network-level attacks must be studied jointly with system and data heterogeneity [5]. This survey takes a security-centered view of DML in cloud-edge-IoT systems. Rather than providing tutorial-level coverage of generic distributed computing platforms, basic optimization, or cloud ML services, we focus on how threat models, heterogeneity, privacy mechanisms, robustness limits, and deployment constraints interact. FL is treated as a promi-nent and high-risk instance of DML because it combines dis-tributed data ownership, partial trust, client churn, and non-IID data. The goal is to provide a heterogeneity-aware roadmap for the design, evaluation, and deployment of trustworthy DML systems.

Section VII discusses open research directions, and Sec-tion VIII concludes the paper. II. T HREAT MODEL AND DML T RUST BOUNDARIES 

Distributed machine learning (DML) distributes data, com-putation, model updates, and orchestration logic across work-ers, edge devices, organizational silos, and cloud services. Conventional distributed training often operates within a sin-gle administrative domain. In contrast, FL and IoT-oriented DML commonly involve partially trusted clients, intermittent participation, heterogeneous resources, and non-IID local data [1], [3], [4]. From a security perspective, the central issue is therefore not only how models are optimized, but where trust boundaries arise and what information crosses them. 

A. Assets, Adversaries, and Trust Assumptions 

The primary assets in DML include raw local data, labels, gradients, model updates, global model parameters, aggrega-tion rules, client identities, communication metadata, audit logs, and deployed model artifacts. In regulated IoT domains such as healthcare, finance, industrial automation, and smart cities, these assets may encode sensitive personal, opera-tional, or proprietary information. Protecting them requires simultaneous attention to confidentiality, integrity, availability, robustness, and model ownership. Adversaries may be external or internal. External adver-saries can eavesdrop on, replay, or tamper with model traffic; query deployed models to extract or infer membership; or inject malicious inputs at inference time. Internal adversaries include compromised clients, malicious data owners, curious aggregators, compromised gateways, free-riding participants, and Sybil clients that create multiple identities to bias train-ing or incentives [18]–[20]. Aggregators and cloud providers may be trusted, honest-but-curious, or partially compromised, depending on the deployment model. These assumptions deter-mine whether defenses such as secure aggregation, differential privacy (DP), HE, secure MPC, TEEs, or robust aggregation are appropriate. 

B. DML/FL Coordination Patterns as Security Boundaries 

Coordination architecture determines which entities observe local updates, where aggregation occurs, and how failures or attacks propagate. In parameter-server training, workers send gradients or updates to one or more central servers, concentrat-ing trust and making the servers potential bottlenecks, privacy-leakage points, and targets for poisoning [3], [21]. Collective communication mechanisms such as AllReduce reduce de-pendence on a persistent parameter server in trusted clusters. However, they still require authenticated group membership, secure transport, and resilience to faulty participants [22], [23]. Data-parallel, model-parallel, and hybrid-parallel execution also expose different security surfaces. Data parallelism com-municates gradients or model deltas from local data shards, making it especially relevant to poisoning, gradient leakage, and robust aggregation. Model and pipeline parallelism com-municate activations, gradients, and intermediate states across model partitions, thereby creating additional dependency paths and increasing the risk of fault propagation [24]–[26]. Syn-chronization further shapes security: synchronous training sim-plifies aggregation analysis but is vulnerable to stragglers and 4

denial-of-service behavior, whereas asynchronous or bounded-staleness training improves throughput under heterogeneity but introduces stale updates, replay risk, and weaker anomaly attribution [27]–[29]. 

C. IoT-Specific Exposure Points: Edge Clients, Gateways, Aggregators, and Cloud Services 

IoT-oriented DML adds exposure points not typically present in controlled data-center training. Edge clients may have weak hardware isolation, limited patching, intermittent power, and constrained storage or compute. Gateways and roadside/industrial edge nodes may aggregate updates from multiple devices and therefore become attractive targets for poisoning, traffic analysis, or model substitution [30]. Cloud aggregators and ML-as-a-service platforms provide scalability but also introduce concerns related to multi-tenancy, data residency, access control, and model extraction [31]. Beyond conventional FL threat models, IoT deployments in-troduce physical and operational constraints that directly shape security design [32]. Clients may be battery-powered, intermit-tently connected, memory-limited, weakly patched, or physi-cally accessible to an adversary. Sensing modalities may also differ across devices, e.g., cameras, accelerometers, medical wearables, industrial vibration sensors, vehicular LiDAR/radar, or environmental sensors, producing modality-specific drift and uneven update quality. In addition to network-level tam-pering, IoT systems are exposed to physical-layer disruption, sensor spoofing, jamming, device theft, firmware compromise, gateway compromise, and stale or replayed telemetry. These risks make it insufficient to assume that every participating client is continuously available, computationally capable, or semantically trustworthy. Secure IoT DML therefore requires explicit placement decisions across the client, gateway, edge, and cloud layers: lightweight filtering and attestation near devices; buffering, rate control, and anomaly screening at gateways; aggregation, privacy accounting, and provenance at edge/cloud servers; and rollback or fallback policies for safety-critical deployments [33]. Accordingly, IoT DML should be analyzed as a layered system comprising client-side data and local training; gateway-level filtering and buffering; server-side aggregation and model governance; and cloud/edge deployment of inference services. Each layer can alter the feasibility and cost of privacy, robust-ness, and cryptographic defenses [34]. III. H ETEROGENEITY DIMENSIONS IN IOT-O RIENTED

Common forms include label skew, feature or covariate shift, concept shift, quantity skew, and spatiotemporal skew [4], [35]–[37]. Non-IID data induces gradient dissimilarity between local and global objectives. When clients perform multiple local updates before synchronization, local models may drift toward client-specific optima, slowing or destabilizing convergence and increasing client-level performance disparities [3], [38], [39]. See Fig. 2b, 2c, and 2d for the impact of non-IID data on robust aggregation methods and potential solutions. This is not only an optimization concern. Many Byzantine-robust aggregation rules assume that benign updates are relatively concentrated; under severe heterogeneity, honest updates may already appear as outliers, making malicious updates harder to detect [40], [41]. Non-IID evaluations should therefore report both the par-tition protocol and explicit measures of heterogeneity. Acommon approach is Dirichlet label partitioning, in which each client’s class proportions are sampled from a Dirichlet distribution with concentration parameter α; smaller α values yield more highly skewed client distributions, whereas larger 

α values yield more nearly uniform distributions. Common heterogeneity metrics include Earth Mover’s Distance (EMD), the minimum cost of transporting probability mass between distributions; Hellinger distance, a bounded measure of sepa-ration between probability distributions; Jensen-Shannon (JS) divergence, a symmetric and bounded variant of Kullback-Leibler divergence; and Kullback-Leibler (KL) divergence, a directed measure of relative entropy. Naturally heterogeneous benchmarks include LEAF, FEMNIST, and Shakespeare [37], [39], [42], [43]. These partitioning approaches and metrics are discussed further in the next sections. 

B. System Heterogeneity: Device, Bandwidth, Energy, and Participation Asymmetry 

IoT DML systems also exhibit strong system heterogeneity. Clients may differ in processor type, memory, battery level, sensor modality, local storage, communication bandwidth, and reliability. These differences affect which clients participate, how often they synchronize, and how expensive each defense becomes. System heterogeneity also affects availability and reliabil-ity. Stragglers, dropped clients, gateway failures, software incompatibilities, and unstable networks may delay or bias aggregation. Classical mechanisms such as checkpointing, roll-back, replication, speculative execution, and elastic scheduling remain relevant, but they must be adapted to continuously evolving model states and repeated communication rounds [22], [47]. Algorithm-based fault tolerance and Byzantine-resilient aggregation further link reliability and security, as corrupted updates can arise from either accidental faults or malicious behavior [40], [41], [48]. Energy and latency are also first-class constraints. A statis-tically effective defense may be infeasible on battery-powered sensors, mobile devices, or real-time industrial/vehicular plat-forms. Thus, secure IoT DML requires reporting on commu-nication, latency, memory, and energy overhead, as well as accuracy and robustness. 

C. Trust Heterogeneity: Cross-Device, Cross-Silo, and Multi-Tenant Settings 

Trust assumptions vary substantially across deployment settings. In cross-silo FL, participants such as hospitals, banks, or factories may be identifiable institutions with contractual obligations but strict data-governance requirements [49]. In cross-device FL, participation is large-scale, intermittent, and difficult to vet [50] fully. In multi-tenant cloud ML, the provider, tenants, APIs, and infrastructure introduce additional risks related to access control, isolation, and model extraction. These trust differences directly affect defense selection. Cross-silo deployments may justify TEEs, MPC, contractual audit logs, and stronger identity management [51]. Cross-device deployments often require secure aggregation, capped client sampling, Sybil resistance, DP, and lightweight anomaly scoring. Multi-tenant MLaaS settings require authenticated interfaces, rate limiting, output control, watermarking, and provenance mechanisms. A defense that is suitable under one trust model may be ineffective or too costly under another. Taken together, these three dimensions of heterogeneity provide the analytical lens for the remainder of the sur-vey. Statistical heterogeneity affects convergence, benign-update dispersion, fairness, and poisoning detectability; system heterogeneity determines whether defenses are computation-ally and energetically feasible; and trust heterogeneity deter-mines which privacy, aggregation, cryptographic, provenance, and governance mechanisms are appropriate. Sections IV-VI therefore analyze attacks, defenses, and evaluation practices through these three coupled dimensions rather than treating heterogeneity as a standalone optimization issue. IV. A. Poisoning, Backdoors, and Byzantine Manipulation 

Training-time attacks corrupt data, labels, local optimiza-tion, or communicated updates. In data poisoning, an adversary manipulates local training examples or labels to bias the learned decision function [6], [18]. In model-update poisoning, the attacker directly submits malicious gradients or parameters that need not correspond to any real local dataset. These attacks may target availability by degrading convergence or accuracy or integrity by selectively changing model behavior for chosen inputs or classes. B. Privacy Leakage, Model Extraction, and IP Threats 

DML can leak sensitive training information even when raw data are not explicitly shared. Gradient inversion and related reconstruction attacks can recover features or labels from shared gradients or updates, particularly when updates are fine-grained or insufficiently protected [56], [57]. Membership inference attacks attempt to determine whether a specific record was used during training, with strong likelihood-ratio methods demonstrating substantial risk even under black-box access [58], [59]. Property inference can further reveal aggregate or sensitive attributes about a client population. Model extraction attacks threaten intellectual property by using prediction APIs or exposed inference services to train surrogate models that approximate a proprietary model [60]. These attacks can enable competitive misuse, downstream adversarial transfer, or unauthorized redistribution. Water-marking, fingerprinting, output throttling, confidence masking, query monitoring, and extraction-aware API design are, there-fore, important complements to privacy-preserving training. 

C. Network, Orchestration, and Sybil/Game-Theoretic Attacks 

DML repeatedly exchanges gradients, model updates, meta-data, and control messages across networks. On-path adver-saries may eavesdrop, replay, downgrade, substitute, or tamper with updates, enabling privacy leakage, model replacement, or poisoning in transit [56], [61]. Authentication, encrypted trans-port, update signing, replay protection, secure aggregation, and round-level attestation are therefore necessary system-level protections. Wireless and IoT deployments introduce additional physical-layer (PHY) and medium-access vulnerabilities be-cause training depends on shared, time-varying communica-tion channels [62]. Jamming and selective interference can cause client dropout, delay synchronization, or bias participa-tion toward clients with more reliable connectivity [63]. Spoof-ing, channel manipulation, and compromised gateways or ac-7

cess points can alter client identities, scheduling information, or transmitted updates [64]. In over-the-air aggregation, where wireless signals are superimposed to compute an aggregate, adversarial manipulation of transmission power or waveform may directly bias the resulting update [65]. Wireless backdoor and poisoning attacks can further exploit compromised edge devices and unreliable links while preserving apparently nor-mal clean-model behavior [10]. Evaluations should therefore consider mobility, channel variation, link failures, selective jamming, and compromised network intermediaries alongside conventional poisoning and Byzantine attacks. Open or weakly authenticated FL settings are also vulner-able to Sybil and free-rider attacks. Sybil adversaries create multiple fake clients to amplify poisoning, bias aggregation, or manipulate incentives [18]. Free riders or lazy clients submit fabricated, noisy, or low-effort updates to obtain rewards without contributing useful information [20]. Game-theoretic models are useful for these settings because attackers and defenders adapt strategically through sampling policies, clip-ping thresholds, acceptance rules, incentives, and reputation mechanisms [66]–[68]. 

D. How Heterogeneity Changes Attack Detectability and De-fense Reliability 

Heterogeneity changes both attack feasibility and defense reliability. Under IID-like conditions, malicious updates may be easier to identify because benign updates cluster more tightly. Under realistic IoT heterogeneity, however, benign up-dates vary because of label skew, domain shift, device quality, sensor modality, and participation patterns. This dispersion can cause robust aggregation to reject useful minority-client updates or accept malicious updates that resemble benign outliers [40], [41], [69]. Accordingly, attack evaluation should not rely on a sin-gle partition or clean aggregate metric. It should vary the number and participation frequency of malicious clients, the skew severity, the synchronization regime, the client sampling policy, and the defense configuration. Clean accuracy should be reported together with attack success rate (ASR), backdoor persistence, client-level disparity, privacy leakage, communi-cation cost, latency, and energy. V. A. Robust Aggregation and Drift-Aware Optimization 

Robust aggregation methods aim to limit the influence of faulty or malicious updates. Coordinate-wise median, trimmed mean, Krum, geometric-median-based RFA, and signSGD with majority vote are representative approaches [40], [53]– [55]. These methods can improve resilience to Byzantine behavior, but their effectiveness depends on the concentration of benign updates, the adversary fraction, dimensionality, and non-IID data. Severe non-IID data can substantially degrade the perfor-mance of FL models protected by robust aggregation methods relative to IID settings [41]. Li et al. [41] evaluated these methods under severe data heterogeneity using Dirichlet( α =0.1) . Under federated stochastic gradient descent (FedSGD), they reported poor accuracy with Krum, Geometric Median (GM), Auto-weighted Geometric Median (AutoGM) [87], and Median relative to their IID counterparts. Under Federated Averaging (FedAvg), performance similarly degraded across the evaluated aggregation methods. The evaluated Byzantine attacks include inner-product ma-nipulation (IPM), which crafts malicious updates to oppose the intended aggregate direction; sign flipping (SF), which reverses or negatively scales client updates; label flipping (LF), which corrupts local training labels; and A Little Is Enough (ALIE), which constructs malicious updates within the apparent statistical range of benign updates to evade detection. On CIFAR-10 with FedAvg, Li et al. [41] evaluated Cen-tered Clipping (CC) with a fixed malicious-client fraction of 25% and reported test accuracy under IID and non-IID data, using Dirichlet( α = 0.1) for the non-IID partition. Alharbi et al. [88] evaluated CC under non-IID data using Dirichlet( α = 0.5), varied the number of attackers, and reported test error. Table IV summarizes the results of the two studies separately. Because the studies use different levels of heterogeneity, attacker configurations, and evaluation metrics, their numerical results are not directly comparable. Within each study, however, the results indicate that CC’s performance can deteriorate substantially under poisoning attacks and non-IID data. Non-IID data can alter both attack effectiveness and defense reliability. Backdoor attacks seek to associate inputs containing 8

a trigger with an attacker-selected output. In the experiments of Zawad et al. [89], some evaluated backdoor attacks became less effective as data heterogeneity increased. This result is limited to the experimental configurations examined in that study and should not be interpreted as evidence that non-IID data generally protects against backdoor attacks. Zawad et al. [89] also found that, in their experiments, non-IID training caused benign and malicious updates to exhibit similar overfitting characteristics, which reduced the effectiveness of defenses that used overfitting to identify malicious updates. Drift-aware optimization addresses the non-IID conditions that often undermine robustness. F ED PROX limits local drift through proximal regularization, SCAFFOLD uses control variates to correct client drift, F ED DYN aligns local and global objectives, and F ED OPT variants stabilize server-side opti-mization [3], [38], [70], [71]. Modeling-layer methods such as F ED BN, personalization, clustered FL, and client-specific heads can further reduce domain shift and fairness disparities [35], [72]–[74]. In security-sensitive settings, these methods should be evaluated jointly with poisoning and Byzantine attacks, not only under benign non-IID training. 

B. Differential Privacy and Secure Aggregation 

Differential privacy protects individual training records by limiting the influence of any single example on the released model. Differentially private stochastic gradient descent (DP-SGD) clips per-example gradients. It injects calibrated noise, while Private Aggregation of Teacher Ensembles (PATE) uses noisy aggregation of teacher predictions to train a private stu-dent model [75], [76]. These methods provide formal privacy guarantees but can reduce utility, especially for small clients, rare classes, or highly non-IID data [90]. Secure aggregation protects communicated updates by en-suring that the server observes only the aggregate sum, not individual client updates [61]. It is particularly useful under an honest-but-curious server model and is often combined with clipping and DP. However, secure aggregation does not, by itself, prevent poisoning or guarantee the privacy of the final model; it must be paired with robust aggregation, anomaly detection, and privacy accounting, tailored to the threat model. 

C. HE, MPC, and TEE-Based Protected Computation 

HE, MPC, and TEEs support protected computation when raw data, intermediate values, or local updates should not be exposed. HE enables selected computations over ciphertexts and is most mature for encrypted inference or HE-friendly linear operations. Still, it remains constrained by ciphertext expansion, expensive rotations, bootstrapping, and limited native support for nonlinear operators [77]–[79]. MPC allows parties to jointly compute functions without revealing private inputs, often using secret sharing or mixed protocols; systems such as ABY3 and CrypTen improve practicality but remain sensitive to communication, preprocessing, nonlinear opera-tions, and network conditions [80], [81]. TEEs can execute richer ML logic with lower algorithmic redesign, but they rely on hardware trust, attestation, enclave memory limits, and side-channel resistance [82]. Practically, HE is best suited to carefully redesigned en-crypted inference or selected protected operations; MPC is at-tractive when participants can tolerate communication and pre-processing costs; and TEEs are often engineering-compatible when hardware trust is acceptable. Because energy reporting remains inconsistent, studies should report at least latency, communication volume, memory pressure, and, when possible, joules per inference or per training round. 

D. Watermarking, Fingerprinting, and Provenance 

Model watermarking and fingerprinting protect DML model intellectual property by embedding verifiable ownership sig-nals into parameters or model behavior. White-box methods may embed signatures directly into weights, whereas black-box methods use trigger or behavior-based verification through the prediction interface [83]–[85], [91]. Effective ownership mechanisms should preserve utility, remain stealthy, survive compression or fine-tuning, and support defensible post hoc verification. Provenance mechanisms complement watermarking. Signed model artifacts, versioned checkpoints, client-update logs, secure audit trails, and deployment metadata help establish which data, clients, aggregation rules, and defenses contributed to a released model [86]. In regulated IoT settings, provenance also supports accountability, rollback, incident response, and evidence of compliance. 

E. Defense Composition: Non-Compositional Trade-offs 

No defense provides comprehensive protection, and com-bining defenses does not necessarily yield additive benefits. Secure aggregation improves confidentiality by concealing individual client updates, but this reduced visibility can limit anomaly detection, client scoring, and robust aggregation [4], [40], [61]. Similarly, DP-induced clipping and noise may reduce the statistical separation between benign heterogeneous updates and weak poisoning or backdoor signals. At the same time, aggressive robust filtering may reject useful updates from honest minority clients [75], [90], [92]. The threat model should therefore guide defense com-position, required update visibility, heterogeneity level, re-source constraints, and residual trust assumptions. Compatibil-ity should be evaluated jointly across confidentiality, integrity, privacy, utility, and operational cost rather than inferred from the effectiveness of each defense in isolation. B. Attack-Aware Benchmarks and Reproducibility 

DML evaluations should be reproducible and attack-aware. For non-IID studies, authors should specify the skew type, partition-generation protocol, Dirichlet α or equivalent param-eter, client sampling policy, number of clients, malicious-client fraction, and random seeds. When possible, studies should use open-source evaluation frameworks or release partitions and scripts [9], [42]. For security evaluation, attack settings should include poi-soning, backdoors, Byzantine manipulation, gradient leakage, membership inference, model extraction, and network tamper-ing when relevant to the claimed threat model. Robustness should be tested under multiple skew severities rather than a single near-IID partition. Backdoor studies should report both clean accuracy and ASR; privacy studies should report ϵ/δ ,attack success, and any utility loss; and robust aggregation studies should report breakdown behavior under different malicious-client fractions and non-IID regimes. 

C. Non-IID Data Partition 

Dirichlet partitioning is one of the most widely used approaches for simulating non-IID data [37], [41], [118], [119]. Its concentration parameter α controls the degree of heterogeneity: smaller values, such as 0.1-0.5, generally pro-duce highly skewed client distributions, whereas larger val-ues produce more nearly uniform distributions [41]. Another common approach is class-based partitioning, which restricts the number of classes assigned to each client. For example, the one-class and two-class settings in [39] assign data from only one or two classes, respectively, to each client. To model heterogeneity that varies across both geographic locations and time, Jimenez et al. [37] employed the St-Dirichlet method to generate spatiotemporal non-IID partitions. Alternatively, benchmark suites such as LEAF [42] and OARF [120] provide naturally heterogeneous federated datasets for more realistic evaluation. 

D. Non-IID Metrics 

To determine whether a data distribution is non-IID, a quantitative metric is often used to measure its distance from a global (aggregated) distribution. As research on quantifying the level of non-IID advances, common distribution-based non-IID metrics, including EMD [39], Hellinger distance, Jensen-Shannon divergence [121], and KL divergence [122], are being used to characterize the non-IID in label distri-bution, feature distributions, and data volume distributions across participating clients [123]. For example, during the FL initialization phase, EMD was used to measure the total cost of moving a client’s label distribution to another client ’s [39]. A large cost value indicates a significant difference between the two local distributions [39], leading to poor performance of the FL global model. Furthermore, the Jensen-Shannon divergence quantifies the dissimilarity between pairs of client label dis-tributions, revealing the extent of distributional heterogeneity 11 

across federated image, audio, video, and text datasets [121]. In the FL model aggregation phase, the KL divergence allows the server to adaptively assign weights based on how well a client’s model weight distribution aligns with the aggregated model weight distribution [122]. 

E. Metrics Beyond Accuracy: ASR, Privacy Budget, Disparity, Latency, Communication, and Energy 

Average test accuracy is insufficient for trustworthy DML. At minimum, evaluation should report clean model utility, attack success rate [124], poisoned-versus-clean trade-offs, privacy budget [125], [126], client-level or site-level disparity, convergence behavior [127], communication overhead [128], latency [129], memory [125], and energy [130]. For safety-critical or IoT settings, additional metrics may include tail latency, availability, rollback frequency, fairness by site or district, domain-drift indicators, and service-level objective adherence [99], [131]. Energy and carbon should be reported using a clear bound-ary, such as client-side compute, server-side compute, en-clave, or cryptographic accelerator costs, and network transfer energy. When direct power measurement is unavailable, la-tency and communication volume should be reported as first-order proxies, especially for HE-, MPC-, TEE-, and secure-aggregation-based systems. 

F. Open-Source Evaluation Frameworks 

Evaluation-oriented techniques aim to assess and compare FL algorithms according to diverse dimensions: non-IID data partitions (data heterogeneity) and device heterogeneity [132], data-type and task scalability [121], robustness against ad-versarial threats [46], [133], [134]. Each offers structured guidance for implementing new algorithms, along with stan-dardized evaluation protocols for fair and replicable compar-isons. Beyond test accuracy metrics, FedScale [121] provides software tools to measure system performance in terms of wall-clock time, communication cost, and device availability duration [121]. LEAF [42] provides realistic FL datasets while [135] simulated non-IID data silo cases by using six different partition strategies. Under the effects of data heterogeneity, BYZFL [46], FedSecurity [134], and FLPoison [133] focus on systematically evaluating the robustness of various defense mechanisms against data, model poisoning, and FL privacy attacks using ASR and other specific metrics. Table VI illus-trates a comparative analysis of the open-source FL algorithm benchmarking tools. FLHetBench [132] evaluates FL algorithms under differ-ences in device computational capability and availability. Using this framework, Zhang et al. found that longer wall-clock times and lower resource utilization were associated with lower model accuracy in the device-heterogeneous set-tings they evaluated. Other benchmarking frameworks provide standardized evaluation protocols and metrics for multimodal data [136], large language models [134], and safety-critical domains [137]. 

G. Governance Artifacts and Compliance-Oriented Reporting 

Governance translates technical guarantees into deployable evidence. A trustworthy DML deployment should document the threat model, trust assumptions, client admission pol-icy, aggregation rule, privacy mechanism, non-IID evalua-tion protocol, attack suite, known defense limitations, and rollback or incident-response procedure [139]. Audit logs, signed checkpoints, model cards, data-use records, privacy-accounting reports, and provenance metadata support both internal accountability and external compliance. Security should also be integrated into MLOps [140]. Pro-motion gates should require attack-aware validation before a model is deployed; runtime monitors should track drift, abnormal client behavior, ASR indicators, and privacy-relevant query patterns; and rollback mechanisms should be available when poisoning, backdoors, or severe performance drift are detected. In this view, adversarial robustness is not a one-time experiment but a lifecycle property that must be monitored, documented, and periodically revalidated [141]. VII. O PEN RESEARCH DIRECTIONS 

The preceding sections show that trustworthy DML in cloud-edge-IoT systems cannot be achieved through isolated defenses. Robustness, privacy, heterogeneity management, protected computation, incentive design, reproducibility, and governance must be treated as interacting design requirements. This section summarizes the main open research directions that follow from this security-centered view. 

A. Heterogeneity-Aware Security Benchmarks 

A major barrier to progress is the lack of widely accepted benchmarks for evaluating attacks and defenses under real-istic non-IID conditions. Existing studies often use different partition generators, skew severities, client-sampling policies, attacker fractions, and reporting metrics, making results dif-ficult to compare. Future benchmarks should provide pub-lic partitions, fixed seeds, explicit heterogeneity diagnostics, and standardized attack suites covering poisoning, backdoors, Byzantine behavior, privacy leakage, and model extraction. Metrics such as Dirichlet α, Earth Mover’s Distance, Hellinger distance, Jensen-Shannon divergence, quantity-skew indices, and client-level performance should be reported alongside clean accuracy and attack success rate [9], [11], [37]. 

B. Distribution-Aware Robustness Under Adaptive Attacks 

Robust aggregation remains fragile when benign updates are naturally dispersed due to label skew, feature shift, class imbal-ance, or spatiotemporal variation. Future work should develop defenses whose breakdown behavior is explicitly character-ized under heterogeneous benign updates rather than near-IID assumptions. This includes robust aggregation, client vetting, anomaly scoring, and backdoor detection methods that dis-tinguish malicious deviations from legitimate minority-client behavior. Evaluation should vary skew severity, malicious-client fraction, attacker participation frequency, synchroniza-tion regime, and client-sampling policy, while reporting both global and per-client outcomes [37], [40], [41]. 12

C. End-to-End Privacy Leakage Measurement 

Privacy leakage in DML depends on training mode, archi-tecture, normalization layers, gradient granularity, clipping, mixed precision, microbatching, and whether secure aggre-gation is enabled. Yet privacy evaluations are often con-ducted under simplified configurations. Future studies should jointly report formal privacy budgets, reconstruction quality, membership inference performance, model utility, and system overhead under realistic training pipelines. DP-SGD, PATE, secure aggregation, output control, and cryptographic protec-tion should be evaluated as composable defenses rather than isolated mechanisms [61], [75], [76], [142], [143]. 

D. Practical Protected Computation for IoT DML 

HE, MPC, and TEEs are increasingly relevant for privacy-preserving DML, but their deployment costs remain highly workload-dependent. HE still faces ciphertext expansion, boot-strapping cost, rotation overhead, and limited native support for nonlinear operators. MPC can support richer computation but often shifts cost to communication, preprocessing, and in-teraction. TEEs preserve more standard ML execution patterns but rely on hardware trust, attestation, enclave-memory limits, and side-channel resistance. Future research should prioritize predictable performance, compiler support, operator libraries, autotuning, profiling, and end-to-end integration with DML workflows, especially for latency- and energy-constrained IoT deployments [77]–[82]. 

E. Joint Robustness, Privacy, Fairness, and Efficiency Opti-mization 

Many defenses improve one dimension while weakening another. Differential privacy can reduce leakage but may degrade utility or exacerbate client-level disparities in severely non-IID data. Secure aggregation protects individual updates but can limit server-side anomaly detection. Robust aggre-gation can reduce the impact of poisoning but may sup-press valid minority-client updates. Cryptographic protection can strengthen confidentiality but increase latency, memory, communication, and energy costs. Future work should move toward Pareto-style evaluation that jointly reports clean util-ity, ASR, privacy budget, per-client disparity, convergence, latency, communication, memory, energy, and carbon footprint [109], [110], [121]. 

F. Incentive-Compatible and Sybil-Resistant Participation 

Open and cross-device DML systems are vulnerable to free-riding, sparse participation, collusion, and Sybil behav-ior. Existing defenses often treat malicious clients as static adversaries, whereas real participants may adapt strategically to rewards, privacy costs, computational burdens, and detection policies. Future research should integrate mechanism design, reputation, attestation, contribution valuation, and participation control to encourage truthful behavior while limiting adver-sarial influence. Such methods must remain effective under non-IID data, intermittent availability, and privacy-preserving 13 

aggregation, where direct inspection of local updates may be limited [18]–[20], [144]. 

G. Governance, Reproducibility, and Lifecycle Assurance 

Secure DML research also needs stronger reproducibil-ity and governance practices. Reproducible studies should release partitions, seeds, attack scripts, defense configura-tions, telemetry, and system-cost measurements where possi-ble. Governance-oriented deployments should translate tech-nical controls into auditable artifacts, including threat models, privacy-accounting reports, model cards, data-use records, signed checkpoints, risk registers, and incident-response proce-dures. Future work should map technical guarantees to regula-tory and institutional requirements so that secure aggregation, DP, provenance, and monitoring can support compliance-oriented review rather than remain purely technical claims [9], [121], [145], [146]. Overall, future progress in trustworthy DML will depend on integrated, deployment-aware research that jointly addresses heterogeneity, privacy leakage, adversarial robustness, pro-tected computation, incentive alignment, reproducibility, and governance. The field should move beyond isolated algorith-mic defenses toward lifecycle-oriented secure DML systems that can be evaluated, deployed, monitored, and audited under realistic cloud-edge-IoT conditions. VIII. C ONCLUSION 

Distributed machine learning (DML) enables collaborative model development without requiring complete data central-ization, but it also introduces interconnected risks to security, privacy, reliability, and fairness. This survey examined these risks across conventional distributed learning and federated learning in cloud-edge-IoT environments. A central conclusion is that heterogeneity is not merely an optimization issue. Non-IID data, resource asymmetry, intermittent participation, and unequal trust can affect attack effectiveness, anomaly detection, robust aggregation, privacy protection, and client-level fairness. Security evaluations must therefore state their threat and trust assumptions and test defenses under representative heterogeneity and system con-ditions. No individual mechanism protects the entire DML lifecycle. Dependable deployments require complementary controls for local training, aggregation, communication, model ownership, monitoring, and recovery. Their evaluation should extend beyond clean accuracy to include attack effectiveness, privacy guarantees, client-level disparities, system overhead, and re-producibility.
