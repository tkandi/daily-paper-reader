## ABSTRACT 

AI compute is becoming large and fast-changing electrical loads. Its impacts on data center infrastructure and power grids depend not only on total energy con-sumption, but also on how their power draw moves over sub-second to minute timescales, including peaks, ramps, and synchronized fluctuations across accel-erators. Existing energy benchmarks for large language models (LLMs) report aggregate quantities such as joules per token and rarely release the underlying time-series measurements, leaving these power dynamics poorly characterized and difficult to model. We introduce LLM-P OWER TRACE , an open dataset of time-resolved, per-GPU power traces for LLM fine-tuning and inference, sampled every 100 ms on varying NVIDIA platforms paired with complete workload and system metadata. For fine-tuning, LLM-P OWER TRACE profiles open model fami-lies spanning a wide range of model sizes and fine-tuning methods, together with controlled sweeps over batch size, sequence length, precision, parallelism, and checkpointing. For inference, it covers variations in concurrency, GPU frequency, KV-cache and weight quantization, prompt and output lengths, and request-arrival patterns. Built upon power traces, we define power-grid-facing metrics as well as AI-compute power&energy scaling law for extrapolating beyond the measured set-tings. Together, LLM-P OWER TRACE provides a dataset foundation for modeling, forecasting, and managing the fast-changing electrical behavior of AI compute 1.

## 1 INTRODUCTION 

AI data centers are among the fastest-growing sources of electricity demand (Shehabi et al., 2024; International Energy Agency, 2025). Beyond their substantial energy consumption, their electrical behavior is shaped by the tightly coordinated execution of AI workloads. Modern AI clusters can syn-chronously shift thousands of accelerators among compute-bound, memory-bound, communication-bound, and idle states, producing rapid and possibly structured variations in power demand. These dynamics are already becoming operationally significant, where large-scale training jobs can generate synchronized power swings that require mitigation through software, hardware, and energy-storage mechanisms (Choukse et al., 2025), while LLM workloads can induce irregular power transients impacting power grids (Li et al., 2024). For instance, in July 2024, approximately 1,500 MW of data center load in Northern Virginia was abruptly disconnected almost simultaneously following a single transmission-system fault (North American Electric Reliability Corporation, 2025). As power availability increasingly constrains the expansion and operation of AI data centers, understanding the magnitude, variability, and temporal structure of AI power demand is essential for both data center operation and energy systems planning.

Existing characterizations of AI power and energy use remain predominantly energy-centric (Chung et al., 2025; Oviedo et al., 2026). However, grid operators, data center owners, and AI servers are concerned not only about overall bulk energy or tokens per joule, but also about when power is drawn and how fast it changes. A workload with low total energy consumption can still create operational challenges through high power peaks, steep ramps, or unregulated fluctuations. Such power dynamics can impose additional operational requirements both on data center electrical infrastructure such as feeders, transformers, power distribution and backup units, as well as on power systems such as frequency regulation, ramping capability, and other balancing resources (Chen et al., 2025). Yet the data needed to study AI compute’s power and energy dynamics remain limited. Benchmarks commonly report total energy consumption, energy per token, query, or run (Samsi et al., 2023; Luccioni et al., 2024; Chung et al., 2025; Fernandez et al., 2025; Tschand et al., 2025). LLM serving datasets capture request arrivals and workload statistics but not corresponding power mea-surements (Wang et al., 2025), while cluster telemetry datasets are not organized around controlled, configuration-labeled LLM workloads (Samsi et al., 2021). As a result, researchers seeking to characterize (Maliakel et al., 2026), model and scale (Hoffmann et al., 2022), forecast, or control AI power dynamics (Colangelo et al., 2026) lack open data that directly links workload configurations and execution events to high-resolution power trajectories. To address this gap, we introduce LLM-P OWER TRACE to connect time-resolved GPU power mea-surements with fine-tuning and inference workload characteristics, execution configurations, and hardware properties. It contains 1788 fine-tuning runs and 405 full serving runs on variations of NVIDIA GPU platforms and datasets, each with 100 ms per-GPU telemetry, the full run configuration, and timestamped execution events. Our contributions are threefold. First, LLM-P OWER TRACE 

provides a systematic, configuration-labeled collection of high-resolution power traces for both LLM fine-tuning and inference, enabling characterization of rich power behaviors beyond aggregate or average energy metrics. Second, building on these traces, we define grid-relevant metrics and demonstrate both model-based analysis and empirical scaling laws for identifying key factors and extrapolating power and energy behaviors beyond the measured configurations. Third, we release the data, metric-computation code, and an interactive explorer, together with validation pipeline that supports reviewed community extensions of the dataset. 

## 2 RELATED WORK 

Energy of LLM training and inference. As AI models’ parameters increase sharply over the past decade, AI training and inference’s energy and emission impacts are drawing attention (Strubell et al., 2019; Patterson et al., 2021). For inference, Samsi et al. (2023) benchmark LLaMA across GPU generations and sharding choices, Luccioni et al. (2024) compare tasks and architectures, Fernandez et al. (2024) analyzes LLM-induced transients from a power systems perspective, and reliability organizations now treat large computational loads as a distinct and urgent reliability risk (North American Electric Reliability Corporation, 2025). ## 3 THE LLM-P OWER TRACE DATASET 

LLM-P OWER TRACE records GPU power together with workload configuration and execution metadata. The main fine-tuning collection includes Gemma 1–4, Llama 2 and 3.x, Qwen2 and Qwen3, DeepSeek LLM, and Mistral/Mixtral. The collection includes both dense and mixture-of-experts (MoE) models, with full fine-tuning, LoRA (Hu et al., 2022), and QLoRA (Dettmers et al., 2023). Both data parallelism (DP) and layer-wise model parallelism (MP) are involved. In DP, each worker keeps a model replica. In MP, consecutive model layers are placed across multiple GPUs. The main models, fine-tuning/inference configuration, hardware specifications are summarized in Table 4. Inference traces are collected with vLLM (Kwon et al., 2023) on A100, H100, and H200 GPUs, serving dense Qwen3 (0.6B–32B) and Llama-3.1 (8B, 70B) models and the MoE Qwen3-30B-A3B at tensor-parallel degrees 1–4. Each sweep varies one factor at a time: maximum concurrency, SM clock, KV-cache and weight quantization, input/output length, and the arrival pattern. Arrival patterns are among closed-loop (a fixed number of requests in flight), Poisson, or replayed BurstGPT traffic. Runs also include a request timeline aligned to the power trace. The main configurations are summarized in Table 2, and the full sweep values are listed in Appendix B. 

Hosting and Maintenance The datasets together with an interactive explorer that filters by model family, GPU, workload type, and precision are available in our online website repository. The explorer also accepts community traces in the same format: submissions are validated automatically, stay private until reviewed, and are published only with the contributor’s explicit consent. The complete dataset will be released upon acceptance. The code used to generate the dataset is open sourced 2.

Fine-tuning power trace. In this work, we use fine-tuning workloads to represent the training-side workloads in our dataset. Fine-tuning starts from a pretrained model and retains the standard forward– backward–optimization structure of model training Narayanan et al. (2021); Hu et al. (2022), while the set of parameters being updated depends on the fine-tuning method Hu et al. (2022); Dettmers et al. (2023). Detailed method-specific computation is discussed in Appendix A.2. LLM fine-tuning is an iterative workload with a structured execution pattern. Each run consists of a startup phase followed by a fine-tuning loop (Fig. 2a). The startup phase exhibits two relatively stable power plateaus, corresponding to process startup and runtime initialization. The fine-tuning loop then follows a nested periodic structure determined by gradient accumulation and checkpoint frequency. We refer to one optimizer step as one fine-tuning iteration. Each iteration consists of several gradient-accumulation micro-steps (a forward and backward pass on one micro-batch each), followed by gradient processing and the parameter update. During checkpoint saving, computation largely pauses and power drops to a plateau near the active-GPU baseline, producing a regular power swing at each checkpoint. Within each micro-step, the forward pass produces a short high-power interval, whereas the backward pass is longer and exhibits greater power variation. This difference reflects the additional computation required for backpropagation: for a typical linear layer, the forward pass requires one matrix multiplication to compute the output, while the backward pass requires two to compute the input and weight gradients. 

Inference-serving power trace. LLM inference is also iterative, but its execution structure is organized around requests. Every run splits into startup and a serving loop (Fig. 2b). Startup again shows two stable power plateaus, corresponding to process startup and runtime initialization, with brief bursts from graph compilation and CUDA-graph capture. The serving loop consists of repeated request cycles, each divided into prefill and decode . Prefill processes prompt tokens in parallel, producing a short compute-bound interval with high tensor-core activity and power. Decode generates one token per sequence per step while repeatedly accessing model weights and the growing KV cache, resulting in a longer memory-bound interval with lower tensor-core activity and power. Under closed-loop load, the requests in flight start together, reach their output length at similar times, and are replaced together, so power repeats with a batch-turnover period : the time between these replacements, and the inference counterpart of the fine-tuning iterations. With a fixed number of requests in flight, this period is close to the mean request latency, so it is set largely by the output length and by how fast each decode step runs. Unlike checkpoint saving in fine-tuning, inference cycles do not return power to the GPU idle level for continuous serving, so steady serving appears as repeated prefill–decode power swings rather than large periodic drops. 5Preprint. Despite sharing the same hierarchical structure, the traces exhibit substantial differences in power level, time scale, waveform characteristics, and cross-GPU power distribution. Fine-tuning factors fall into three layers (Fig. 1, Table 7): the workload (model, method, batch, sequence length, precision) determines the computation and data movement per iteration. The execution configuration 

(parallelism, gradient accumulation, checkpointing) determines how computation, communication, and I/O events are ordered, synchronized, and repeated. Finally, the hardware (compute throughput, data-transfer capability, power envelope) determines the dynamic response characteristics of the power trace. Inference factors fall into the same three layers (Table 10): the workload (model, concurrency, input and output length, weight and KV-cache precision, arrival process) determines the computation and data movement per decode step. The execution configuration (tensor parallelism) determines how computation and communication are split and synchronized across GPUs. Finally, the hardware 

(GPU platform, SM clock) determines the dynamic response characteristics of the power trace. Fig. 3 reveals several consistent patterns for LLM fine-tuning. We treat the fine-tuning method as a workload characteristic because it changes the set of trainable parameters and the associated gradient and optimizer states. First, increasing model size, batch size, or sequence length generally increases power, but the increase becomes smaller as power approaches a hardware-dependent envelope. Beyond this point, additional workload is reflected more in longer execution time than in higher power. This limited power headroom also helps explain why ramping does not necessarily increase monotonically with workload size. Second, different workload configurations affect energy efficiency in distinct ways. Larger batch sizes reduce energy per token by amortizing fixed overheads, while MoE models require less effective computation than dense counterparts. Despite lower memory footprint of QLoRA, its fine-tuning is slower and consumes more energy than Full and LoRA. Finally, hardware changes both magnitude and temporal structure of power traces. Compared with earlier GPU models, H100 and H200 achieve lower energy per token and shorter, more regular fine-tuning iterations, but operate at higher power levels with steeper ramps. First, heavier AI load, whether more requests in flight, longer prompts, or larger models, raises power only until the GPU reaches its power limit; beyond it, additional work appears as longer request latency rather than higher power. ## 6 CONCLUSION AND DISCUSSION 

In this work, we present LLM-P OWER TRACE , an open dataset and benchmark of time-resolved GPU power traces for LLM fine-tuning and inference, with grid-facing metrics, and power and energy scaling laws. Beyond aggregated energy and joules per token metrics, our profiled traces show that the power waveform of AI workloads exhibit structured relationships with choices of AI model, hardware, execution, and AI serving parameters. LLM-P OWER TRACE provides a novel and necessary connection of fine-grained AI compute power modeling with data center and energy system tasks. In the future work, we will maintain and update the dataset through the community pipeline, and add multi-node training, server- and facility-level measurements, practical serving policies (e.g., disabling or tuning chunked prefill, speculative decoding), and incoming generation of accelerators.

B.2 DISAGGREGATED -S ERVING MODEL CALIBRATION AND VALIDATION 

The calibration traces provide the measurements needed to identify both the capacity and power models. Fixed-length prefill-limited runs provide service rates across input lengths, which fit the achieved fraction of peak compute and an effective attention coefficient. Decode runs provide mean iteration times across batch sizes and context lengths, which fit the achieved fraction of peak memory bandwidth together with a fixed per-step cost and a per-request cost. Power calibration uses per-instance mean GPU power and serving throughput from 3 fixed-length input-length settings, the request-rate sweep, and the Mooncake and Azure workloads, with each throughput normalized by the capacity limit of the same role under the same workload. Least-squares fits estimate the capacity coefficients from these timing relationships. The power measurements are pooled across workloads, and bounded nonlinear least squares fits one capped ramp per role, using 905 per-GPU observations for prefill and 781 for decode. Only the request-rate sweep spans the rising part of the decode ramp and identifies its slope and knee; the other workloads contribute one operating point per deployment. The fitted coefficients and the 2 ramps are applied to all evaluated deployments without deployment-specific adjustment, so the comparison below measures agreement under one shared calibration rather than performance on measurements excluded from every fit. 23 Preprint. Weight precision. Weight precision is the inference-side analogue of the fine-tuning method: it changes how much data each decode step moves. It shortens the batch-turnover period in every model and GPU pair and cuts energy per token by a median 30% (FP8) and 39% (GPTQ-INT4). Its effect on power depends on the platform: mean power falls on H100 and H200 (by up to 20%), and ramps change inconsistently (Figs. 26, 33). Weight quantization therefore shows up mainly as faster, cheaper execution rather than as a different waveform. An FP8 KV cache changes even less: energy per token falls by a median 2.7% (at most 5%) and the ramp changes by less than its run-to-run spread (Fig. 34).
