# Reactive Optimizers

# Abstract 

We study a reactive optimizer that uses a pretrained language-model agent to inspect training dynamics and rewrite executable optimizer code during a single training run. The agent can change update rules and schedules for individual parameter groups, without optimizer-specific meta-training or memory transferred from other runs. We evaluate this approach, OPAL, on language modeling and image generation when a recipe has not been tuned for the target setting. Across the evaluated settings, it matches or improves on selected fixed recipes. Analysis of its decisions shows changes to learning-rate schedules, optimizer structure, numerical precision, and momentum. Controlled experiments also show adaptation to repeated data corruption and reduced overfitting. We report these behaviors together with the agent’s computational cost and implementation requirements. This document is a static version of our blog post. https://xingjianbai.com/reactive-optimizers/ .We release the code at https://github.com/xingjian-bai/opal .

# Introduction 

Today, the recipe used to optimize a deep neural network is typically completely determined before training even begins. A great deal of human ingenuity has gone into priors regarding how training should progress: adaptive step sizes (Kingma and Ba, 2015), preconditioning (Gupta et al., 2018; Vyas et al., 2024), orthogonalized updates (Jordan, 2024; Liu et al., 2025), schedules tied to the training step (Loshchilov and Hutter, 2017; Hägele et al., 2024), and learning rates scaled with model size (Yang et al., 2022). Automated discovery (Lu et al., 2024; Prime Intellect, 2026a; Chen et al., 2023; Metz et al., 2022) searches across many runs for better optimizers. Whether hand-crafted or automatically discovered, the optimizer then runs unchanged through the end of training, adapting, if at all, according to rules written in advance. This scope leaves the following questions unanswered: How should an optimizer respond to an unexpected loss spike or stall? What if one module’s gradients start to oscillate while the others keep improving smoothly? And when a new type of architecture trains poorly with Adam or Muon, is the architecture worse, or has it not yet met an optimizer suited to it? In this work, we attempt to expand the scope of conventional optimizers and make them reactive to the training itself, leading to an ever-adapting optimizer inside a single training run. This reactive optimizer reasons on the spot and writes the weight update rule for each parameter group. Directly writing optimization code, it has the freedom to implement complicated schedules, respond to training accidents, and customize the optimizer for the single training episode in which it exists. Building on earlier attempts to adapt optimizers during training (Metz et al., 2022; Daniel et al., 2016; Jaderberg et al., 2017) and to let a model direct its own fine-tuning (Zweiger et al., 2025), the reactive optimizer is entirely zero-shot, with nothing learned or retained from repeated runs, and puts the optimizer’s entire algorithm up for rewriting. 

1arXiv:2610.33826v1 [cs.LG] 24 Oct 2026 We start with a practical situation, with tasks from image generation and language modeling, in which a reactive optimizer can do much better than static ones. We then turn to the emergent properties of the reactive optimizer, including sophisticated scheduling, suppressing loss spikes, and escaping overfitting. Lastly, we ask what all this costs and how practical it is at scale. We expect the reactive optimizer to grow only stronger with each generation of agents, to be most useful exactly where no recipe exists yet (for a new architecture or a new dataset), and to be one step toward solving the puzzles of deep learning optimization. 

# 1 Training without a recipe 

We first consider a practical setting in which the training budget permits one substantial run but not a broad search over optimization recipes. For example, suppose a PhD student has designed a new model or selected a new dataset for a transformer, with one GPU for a day and a modest budget for LLM API calls. A conventional approach is to adopt a standard recipe or one from a related task; if that recipe is unsuitable, training may exhibit loss spikes or stalled progress. The underlying question is broader: when bounded by GPU time, researcher time, or wall-clock time, how can a deep learning model be trained to its best performance without knowing the optimal optimization recipe in advance? New architectures, modalities, languages, and scientific data all fall into this setting, where few people can afford a sweep at scale. A reactive optimizer offers another way to use that budget: it spends LLM tokens in place of GPU/human hours. With optimizers reacting to training incidents and adjusting themselves, much of the burden of recipe tuning can be reduced. 

Results in brief 

We start from the optimization track (Track 3) of modded-nanogpt (Jordan, 2026), a language-model pre-training task (training a 162M GPT on FineWeb) with many recipes tuned specifically for this one setting. From this task, we create two slightly differing natural settings: (1) the same model trained on an alternate language dataset (we select Chinese, Thai, and Kyrgyz), and (2) the original setup with a quarter of the batch size. Neither comes with a recipe tuned for that setting, so we compare the reactive optimizer with the natural options one would have if they wanted to one-shot the new training: adopting five leaderboard recipes with natural adjustments. In all these new settings, the reactive optimizer outperforms or matches the best baseline (Figure 1). We also apply the reactive optimizer directly to Goldfish (Chang et al., 2026), which proposes a dataset of 350 languages and trains one small GPT per dataset with a standard recipe not tuned for each language. This makes it an ideal ground to test the optimizer’s adaptiveness in one shot. On the Amharic dataset, the official recipe ends at 83.48 bits per sentence on held-out FLORES-200 text (lower is better); the reactive optimizer, initialized with this same recipe, ends at 81.11 in one shot (on Swahili, we obtain a similar gain). Sweeping the learning rate of the standard recipe finds a static recipe that ends ahead of the reactive optimizer, at 80.89, but the reactive optimizer initialized from that swept recipe ends ahead again, at 80.73. Figure 2 shows how an agent updates the optimizer code on the fly. Concretely, we allow the agent to rewrite a file, updater.py , used in every step of optimization. We use code as the entire interface since this allows the agent to easily alter any part of the optimization. Yet, before the agent can make changes, it must observe, on its own, how training is progressing, leading to the second program it can rewrite, analysis.py .What information should the agent use to make its decisions? Raw training states would be impractical: the weights, activations, and gradients would swamp its context. Instead, the statistics and geometry of training dynamics could be helpful for decisions, such as the gradient norm of each parameter group, the size of an update next to the weights it changes, the cosine between consecutive gradients, and which tensors exhibit unusually large gradient spikes. Therefore, as with the optimizer, we give the agent the most freedom: it writes a program, analysis.py , to extract whatever it wants to know from the training dynamics. Its self-written probes run throughout training, taking from the state of training whatever the agent asks for and handing it to the agent for its next decision. We complete the bidirectional loop. Through analysis.py , training reaches the agent in whatever form the agent chooses to see it, and through updater.py , the agent’s decisions reach every step of training. The two programs let the agent devise new optimization algorithms along the way with maximum flexibility. 

# 3 What is dynamically changing during optimization? 

In this section, we look at what the reactive optimizer does throughout training, examining the decisions behind these results. As a summary, Figure 3 shows when, how, and why each agent edits its code. The rest of this section examines these adaptations type by type. 

3.1 Learning-rate schedule 

Frontier training runs come with elaborate hyperparameter schedules, such as the five stages of DeepSeek-V3’s learning rate (DeepSeek-AI et al. (2024); Figure 4, upper left), with such schedules sometimes being written by humans during the training process (Zhang et al., 2022). Engineers can make only so many decisions, each weighing only so many signals. A reactive optimizer can take over this work and has no such limit: it decides frequently, and at each decision, it can access the entire history of the run. As Figure 4 shows, the schedules our agents built are intricate, with separate multi-stage schedules for each parameter group, and differ from run to run. Several of them raise a learning rate mid-run, a move rarely seen in modern schedulers, yet naturally helpful in many scenarios (Loshchilov and Hutter, 2017; Smith, 2017). Moreover, the four examples in Appendix B show the evidence they cited for their decisions.

We observe that the agents base these decisions on the diagnostics from analysis.py . Some examples are: the cosine between a module’s consecutive gradients as a sign of overshooting (“Deduced from clues” in Appendix B); A/B testing between two learning rates every few hundred updates, comparing how fast the loss falls under each (“A-B-A test”); trial and error, with the learning rate raised, lowered, and raised again (“Trial and error”); and the trend of the loss, which makes agents start their cooldown earlier than planned (“Re-planned cooldown”). Each of these changes of schedule involves observation of the training dynamics and reasoning upon said observations. 

3.2 Optimizer design 

The reactive optimizers also change which rule updates which tensors. In our experiments, many switch from the initially given AdamW to the well-known hybrid, Muon, for the attention and MLP matrices while remaining with AdamW for the rest. Later, they make more specific moves (Figure 5). The optimizer can also decide where precision matters. On modded-nanogpt Track 3, it measured that the entries of the token embedding are about 15 in size, where adjacent bfloat16 values are 0.0625 apart, so as the learning rate decayed, rounding would throw away a growing share of each update. From update 1,040, it kept that specific matrix’s Adam moments in float32 and wrote its weights back with compensated rounding and left every other tensor as it was.

3.4 Loss spikes 

In our large-scale training, OPAL also limits the damage of gradient spikes with module-specific clipping: another SiT agent traced 17 of the 18 gradient spikes it logged to the timestep embedder, and added a clipping threshold for each tensor, set from that tensor’s own recent gradient norm, so that a spike in the embedder no longer cuts the updates of every other tensor; its old global threshold stays only as a backstop. Neither move is standard practice, but both have precedents: clipping each tensor against its own history (Wang et al., 2026), and damping the one layer that spikes, as GLM-130B did for its embedding layer (Zeng et al., 2022). Spikes can also come from the data, through the interaction of particular batches with the model state (Chowdhery et al., 2022), and clipping does not in general protect against noisy labels (Menon et al., 2020). In a controlled experiment, we insert four stretches of random images and labels into CIFAR-10, and train a ResNet-18 with OPAL, which is not told about the corruption, and with static optimizers (Figure 7). The first two stretches cost OPAL 50 and 66 points of test accuracy, about as much as the baselines. In between, it added loss damping, replaced it with clipping, and then wrote a gate that holds back batches whose loss is far above recent clean ones. After it tuned that gate, the third stretch cost 12 points and the fourth none, while the clipped baselines and Stable-SPAM (Huang et al., 2025) still lost 28 to 76 points in each.

Its strategy combines progressively higher learning rates with weight averaging, and it chooses its final average on the development set, which its own analysis program calls “extended SWA” (Izmailov et al., 2018). 

Summary of analysis 

Above, we show that reactive optimizers can adapt their recipe to the dataset, to individual modules, and to the behavior of each run, a trait unseen in conventional optimizers. Such customization and self-adaptiveness allow them to craft sophisticated schedules and navigate complex training landscapes. Still, tuning hyperparameters, suppressing loss spikes, and watching out for overfitting are things human experts can do, albeit less conveniently and at a higher cost. As agents get smarter, with hundreds of papers in their memory, thousands of tokens in their chain of thought, millions of training signals in their context, and every step in their hands, we are convinced that reactive optimizers will only become better, developing mechanisms during training more arcane than the ones demonstrated here, beyond what human experts could tune by hand. Such mechanisms may include tracking the second-order curvature of the loss landscape, auditing the variance of each data batch’s signal, or implementing never-before-seen optimization schemes.

# 4 Real-world applicability 

Is a reactive optimizer practical? The very first concern is the time spent waiting for reasoning decisions. In its simplest design, training is stopped while the agent reasons, inducing significant overhead. In our OPAL implementation, we instead provide the option to allow training to continue while the agent thinks, and swap in the agent’s edits at a later update (e.g., a median of 65 updates later on Goldfish). This should have little impact on performance, since most optimizer decisions are not especially time-critical. With this option, the runs in Figure 9 add only a little time on top of the static run (1.01× to 1.14×). At a larger scale, the current implementation still needs work to support sharded training and optimizer compilation; we leave these practical concerns to later work. When comparing API tokens to GPU hours, the reasoning of a reactive optimizer costs little compared with the training compute. At about 100 decisions in a single training run, the LightningDiT agent costs $6.42 at Claude Opus 5.5 list price, on top of about 74 H100-hours of training (about $510 at AWS’s current on-demand price); equipping it on larger training would only make this relative cost smaller. Admittedly, counted in FLOPs, any reactive optimizer would be impractical given the size of frontier agents. Here we simply want to point out that, for most researchers, GPU hours and token access to closed-source models are two different commodities and as the frontier continues to advance, the price of a given capability will continue to fall. Another limitation is that the reactive optimizer needs room to observe and react; its gains shrink when a run is short and its recipe is fine-tuned. The modded-nanogpt track is such a setting: 3,250 updates of a recipe that many people have tuned for nearly this exact task; our gains there are smaller than in the other tasks we tried, which train for about 8 to 120 times as many updates.

That remaining overhead is the cost of running what the agent wrote: an update rule that costs more per step than the recipe’s (such as Muon’s orthogonalization, or keeping a tensor in float32) and the measurement cost of its analysis program. 

Moreover, the reactive behavior introduces another source of uncertainty, potentially making training less reproducible. Most experiments in this work are run once, a few three times; reruns on the released package are in the OPAL repository. Fine-tuning platforms are bringing training to everyone. Tinker (Thinking Machines Lab, 2025) and Prime Intellect’s Hosted Training (Prime Intellect, 2026b) let users fine-tune open-weight models for their own applications without managing the GPUs behind them. On these platforms, however, the mental burden of a good training recipe still falls on people. A reactive optimizer fits naturally on top of these platforms, offloading that burden to agents and helping each user personalize a model to their own needs. 

# 5 Conclusion 

Until now, optimizers have been written before training. Agents, as a cheap form of reasoner with a long context window and expert knowledge, make something new possible: an optimizer that keeps deciding while the network trains in reaction to optimization events. We have demonstrated that such a reactive optimizer watched the training dynamics and rewrote its own code as training unfolded, for different groups of parameters, at the step it deemed proper. On toy and scaled-up experiments, most of these runs ended ahead of the recipes from which they started. The analysis and update mechanism we design is meant to be as simple and general as possible. Wrapping a standard optimizer, it assumes nothing about the model, the data, or the training dynamics. It is useful today wherever no recipe exists yet. For example, a model on a biological or other scientific dataset, an architecture for which no recipe was written, or a language for which no one has tuned a recipe are all excellent use cases for such a reactive optimizer. This is because the optimizer’s agent takes the role of a human expert at countless training steps, where no expert has ever had the time to sit, making similar decisions in response to events in the run. Seen this way, a reactive optimizer is a natural thing to build, and its gains over static ones are to be expected. Furthermore, a training run holds far more information than humans can use, almost all of which is ignored today. A reactive optimizer has all this information within reach. Imagine a world where the notion of a monolithic optimizer is gone; what replaces it is an optimization intelligence that 

10 adapts to every specific run, and it might not be gradient-based, but zeroth-order (Dahal et al., 2026), language-based or more. Perhaps, in the future, we should delegate optimization decisions to agents, not humans. In the past decade, deep learning models have come to possess unreasonably effective power through training. Yet the very heart of their making, the optimizer, is still mechanistic. This work is an attempt to breathe the magic that training has given us into the core of training itself, forming a loop that we hope will create more capability in the future.

# Appendix A. Related work 

Learning optimization rules and adapting them to training feedback have a long history. Early work already considered improving learning strategies themselves and learning context-dependent weight updates, through self-referential learning systems and neural fast-weight controllers (Schmidhuber, 1987, 1992). Andrychowicz et al. (2016) train recurrent networks to produce parameter updates, while Li and Malik (2016) formulate optimization as a policy learned through reinforcement learning. Daniel et al. (2016) learn step-size controllers, and VeLO scales meta-training across optimization tasks (Metz et al., 2022). These methods already make updates depend on the evolving training state. For reinforcement learning, Optim4RL and OPEN learn update rules tailored to its optimization difficulties (Lan et al., 2024; Goldie et al., 2024). AdaQN instead selects a shared target online from critics trained with different hyperparameters (Vincent et al., 2025). Population-based training discovers hyperparameter schedules while jointly training a population of models (Jaderberg et al., 2017). SEAL uses reinforcement learning to train a language model to generate its own fine-tuning data and update directives, including optimization hyperparameters (Zweiger et al., 2025). Our setting uses a pretrained general-purpose agent to inspect one ongoing run and rewrite its executable optimizer code, without optimizer-specific meta-training. The distinction lies in adapting executable update code within the run, rather than in the ability to respond to training feedback itself. 

# Appendix B. Learning-rate decisions 

Deduced from clues 

The SiT agent’s analysis program measured the cosine between consecutive gradients of each parameter group. Where it was negative, the agent lowered the learning rate: the timestep embedder’s went to 0.3× and then to 0.15× of the official rate, with this stated reason: 

> Step 7,506, Decision 6
> Timestep-embedder learning rate goes from 0.3× to 0.15× (1.5e-5) after update 7506, because its cosine is still around −0.22.

Where the cosine stayed slightly positive, for the Muon matrices, it raised the learning rate. 

A-B-A test 

The RAEv2 agent alternated two learning rates over consecutive stretches of a few hundred updates (A-B-A) and compared the training loss under each. After one such test, it switched to 2× from update 3,100, with this stated reason: 

> Update 3,000, Decision 12
> The A-B-A test points the other way from my provisional read at update 2750: 2× gives faster real progress than 1.5×.

# Appendix C. Experimental settings and decision records 

C.1 Overfitting experiment 

A ResNet-18 trained on CIFAR-10 for 120 epochs without data augmentation. Among the 50,000 training images, 10% are held out with their correct labels as a development set; among the rest, 10% get a wrong label, drawn uniformly from the wrong classes and fixed for the run. The agent saw training statistics, along with dev loss and dev accuracy; it was not told about the specific setting (that some labels were wrong), and it never saw the test performance. We make the network normalize its logits (a centered form of LogitNorm, (Wei et al., 2022)) to prevent loss-hacking behavior. AdamW runs from learning rate 3e-3, which is also the starting recipe for the reactive optimizer. OPAL’s final weights are an average of its weights from epochs 96–117, chosen on the development set. The AdamW EMA baseline uses a moving average of its weights, also chosen on the development set.
