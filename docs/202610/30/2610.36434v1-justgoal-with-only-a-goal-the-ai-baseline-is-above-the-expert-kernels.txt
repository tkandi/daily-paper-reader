## Abstract 

A small number of GPU kernels often determines the efficiency of large lan-guage model training and inference. High-performance kernel development still typically draws on expert knowledge, from direct expert implementa-tion, to collaboration with general-purpose models, to specialized agents that encode expert-designed evaluation methods, domain knowledge, and search workflows. These approaches differ mainly in how expert knowledge enters the development process. We ask how much additional value that knowledge still provides for kernel optimization as foundation models grow stronger. To study this question, we construct a goal-only baseline and call its concrete experimental configuration JustGoal: a general-purpose agent receives only the optimization objective and constraints, with no kernel-specific evaluation infrastructure, knowledge base, or search workflow, and builds its own evaluation loop. On NVIDIA B200 GPUs, JustGoal outper-forms open-source libraries on 8 of the 10 operators entering the comparison, with per-operator geometric mean speedups of 1.14–2.34 ×, and surpasses the fastest publicly released specialized-agent kernel on 5 of the 9 opera-tors that have one. This marks a capability threshold: without task-time injection of kernel-specific knowledge or optimization workflows, current frontier models can already surpass human-maintained implementations on a range of operators. In a controlled same-model comparison with Fable 5.1 on single shapes, the KDA-0.5 harness reaches 0.95 × the performance of JustGoal, and single-mechanism ablations find no consistent benefit from domain knowledge or predefined workflows. These results show that the effectiveness of specialized machinery cannot be presumed from its com-plexity or from results on earlier models. It should be established by a clear gain over a goal-only baseline under the same model and task scope, and reevaluated when the foundation model changes. 

## 1 Introduction 

The computational efficiency of large language model training and inference depends crit-ically on a small set of GPU kernels. Developing high-performance implementations of these kernels requires jointly optimizing tiling strategies, memory hierarchy utilization, asyn-chronous execution pipelines, and hardware-specific instructions. These design choices are tightly coupled, and their optimal configuration changes across hardware generations. Con-sequently, the development of high-performance kernels has long relied on leading experts devoting weeks or even months to manual optimization. The FlashAttention series exempli-fies this recurring investment: achieving high performance on successive GPU architectures has required substantial redesigns of its algorithms and execution pipelines (Dao et al., 2022; Shah et al., 2024; Dao et al., 2026).

They may also encode evaluation methods, domain knowledge, and search workflows into specialized kernel agents such as KDA, AVO, AKO, and CAKE. These agents have matched or surpassed expert-written implementations on selected operators (MIT HAN Lab, 2026; NVIDIA, 2026; AKO Team, 2026a; Ye et al., 2026). What these approaches share is their reliance on expert knowledge. As foundation models improve, capabilities that previously had to be supplied explicitly may become native to the model. This raises two related questions: how much additional value does expert knowledge continue to provide for kernel optimization as model capabilities increase, and how far can a model optimize kernels when given only the task objective and constraints, with as little predefined expert knowledge as possible? To examine these questions, we construct a goal-only baseline and call its concrete ex-perimental configuration JustGoal. It provides no kernel-specific evaluation infrastructure, domain knowledge, or search workflow. A general-purpose agent receives only the task ob-jective and constraints. It builds its own evaluation loop and optimization process. We observe the role of expert knowledge from two measurable perspectives. First, we compare kernels generated by JustGoal with human-maintained open-source libraries and publicly released specialized-agent kernels, asking what performance level a goal-only configuration already reaches. Second, under the same model and task scope, we compare JustGoal with a specialized harness that adds evaluation infrastructure, domain knowledge, and search workflows, directly measuring the marginal value of these explicit expert mechanisms. Our experiments cover 11 operators spanning attention, linear attention, sparse attention, and mixture-of-experts (MoE). We independently verify every kernel in the comparison. From the first perspective, JustGoal is faster than human-maintained open-source libraries on 8 of the 10 operators entering the comparison, and faster than the best publicly released specialized-agent kernel on 5 of the 9 operators that have one. This marks a capability threshold: without task-time injection of kernel-specific knowledge or optimization work-flows, current frontier models can construct evaluation feedback, identify performance bot-tlenecks, and surpass human-maintained implementations on a range of operators. From the second perspective, in the scope-matched Fable 5.1 comparison, the full KDA-0.5 har-ness produces no overall gain on single-shape tasks, and single-mechanism ablations show no consistent gain from domain knowledge or predefined workflows. When the goal expands from optimizing one shape to covering every workload in an operator definition, the harness still provides a gain. To measure the net gain that the full specialized harness adds beyond the model’s own capabilities, it must be compared with a goal-only baseline under the same model and task scope. This net gain should be reevaluated whenever the foundation model changes. 

## 2 Related Work 

2.1 High-Performance Kernel Design 

High-performance kernels must be co-designed around a model architecture and target hard-ware. On the one hand, even when the operator remains unchanged, changes in hardware resources and execution mechanisms can make an existing implementation inefficient. On the other hand, evolving model architectures introduce new operators and computational patterns. These two lines of evolution jointly shape kernel design and continually create new development and optimization demands. 

Redesign driven by hardware evolution. High-performance kernel design requires aligning an algorithm’s computational and data movement demands with the resources of the target hardware. The FlashAttention series exemplifies this hardware–algorithm co-evolution: FlashAttention uses tiling to reduce data movement between high-bandwidth memory (HBM) and on-chip SRAM (Dao et al., 2022); FlashAttention-3 reorganizes com-putation and data transfers around Hopper’s asynchronous execution capabilities (Shah et al., 2024); and FlashAttention-4 further adapts the algorithm and execution pipeline to changes in the balance of hardware resources, addressing bottlenecks such as exponential 2evaluation and shared-memory access (Dao et al., 2026). This progression illustrates that, even when an operator’s mathematical definition remains unchanged, achieving high per-formance requires experts to reassess bottlenecks and adapt implementations to the target hardware. 

New demands driven by model evolution. Evolving model architectures not only in-troduce new operators but also change computational dependencies, state updates, and data-access patterns, making existing kernels difficult to reuse directly. For example, DeepSeek provides FlashMLA for multi-head latent attention (MLA) (DeepSeek-AI, 2025b). With the introduction of DeepSeek Sparse Attention (DSA), it also provides the corresponding sparse attention kernels through FlashMLA and implements the indexer’s scoring kernels in DeepGEMM (DeepSeek-AI, 2025c;a). As a linear-attention architecture, Gated DeltaNet (GDN) combines gating with the delta rule, and Kimi Delta Attention extends it with a finer-grained gating mechanism. Both create computational patterns with recurrent state updates (Yang et al., 2025; Kimi Team, 2025) that require specialized implementations distinct from standard attention. Operator implementations further branch into separate kernel paths for prefill and decode, forward and backward, and different precision and ac-cumulation strategies. Sequence length and batch size further change the optimal design. Continued model evolution expands the kernel space along multiple dimensions, requiring sustained expert design and optimization for high-performance kernel development. 

2.2 LLM-Based Kernel Generation 

Existing LLM-based kernel generation methods generally build on a general-purpose agent and add specialized mechanisms for different challenges in kernel optimization: execution feedback and search organize iteration, independent validation ensures reliable evaluation, and domain knowledge and diagnostic tools help models locate performance bottlenecks. 

Execution feedback and search. KernelBench evaluates models’ ability to generate faster, semantically equivalent kernels from PyTorch reference implementations and explores iterative refinement using execution and profiling feedback (Ouyang et al., 2025). Existing work uses different search mechanisms to organize this optimization: METR uses parallel tree search (METR, 2025), AI CUDA Engineer uses evolutionary search (Sakana AI, 2025), K-Search guides search with a co-evolving world model (Cao et al., 2026), and AdaExplore distills recurring failures into reusable validity rules (Du et al., 2026). AVO introduces au-tonomous agents into the mutation step of evolutionary search, producing attention kernels that outperform expert implementations on some evaluated configurations (NVIDIA, 2026). 

Evaluation reliability. Optimization can exploit flaws in evaluation protocols. KernelBench-Verified reports that a GPT-5.5-generated ReLU kernel simply returns its in-put for known test shapes, yielding an apparent 374 × speedup (Zhang et al., 2026). To guard against such failures, kernel agents commonly incorporate independent validation, in-cluding sanitizer and anti-cheating checks in AKO4X (AKO Team, 2026c) and independent acceptance checks by Codex in KDA (HAN Lab Kernel Mafia, 2026). 

Domain knowledge and development environments. Timing reveals that a kernel is slow, but not why. Another line of work therefore equips agents with domain knowledge and diagnostic tools. CudaForge uses a Judge agent to interpret NCU metrics and guide revisions by a Coder agent (Zhang et al., 2025); KDA identifies the interface between models and the engineering loop as a primary bottleneck, motivating KernelWiki, built from production-code pull requests, an NCU profiling skill, and an IKET (in-kernel event tracing) profiling skill (MIT HAN Lab, 2026; HAN Lab Kernel Mafia, 2026); AKO4X maintains DSL-specific skills and a library of operator pitfalls (AKO Team, 2026c); and CAKE lets agents directly edit typed IR, with the compiler providing localized diagnostics before compilation (Ye et al., 2026).

## 3 JustGoal: With Only a Goal 

3.1 JustGoal 

The box in Figure 1 contains the complete input received by the agent. Here, “goal-only baseline” denotes a control principle: give a general-purpose agent only the objective and constraints, without adding kernel-specific machinery. JustGoal instantiates this principle as the experimental configuration used in this paper. It retains only the general-purpose Claude Code agent substrate and access to the target hardware, while removing the entire kernel-specific layer listed in Table 1. The target hardware in all experiments in this paper is B200. Item 1 requires a clean environment precisely to ensure this. The model builds its own evaluation setup. The agent writes the compilation, correctness-checking, and timing code within its optimization loop: it first measures the reference kernel’s device time as the target to surpass, then writes a naive fp32 implementation as its correctness oracle. 4This is the target operator we want: {operator config }. First measure its device time on a B200. Starting from scratch, and never looking at or copying the reference implementation, write a kernel that is at least 1.2 × faster in device time. 1. Use an isolated, clean environment; do not use existing skills, memory, or other local files. 2. Make sure the kernel is correct. Preferably, first write a slow naive fp32 implemen-tation and compare it with the reference; their difference gives a lower bound on the tolerance, which you multiply by a factor and then use to compare your implemen-tation against the slow fp32 implementation. 3. Do not ask questions; run in --dangerously-skip-permissions mode. 4. Use the B200 cluster for the GPU. 

Figure 1: The complete task input for JustGoal (English translation; the original Chinese prompt is given in Appendix A). We explicitly note that the prompt contains one piece of expert guidance: item 2 instructs the agent to set the tolerance by multiplying the reference implementation’s error relative to the naive fp32 implementation by a factor. This is a common numerical-validation practice and does not involve kernel-optimization knowledge. 

3.2 Evaluation Protocol 

We separate kernel generation from final acceptance. The evaluation in this section is conducted independently by the authors after generation and determines whether code is included in the reported results. 

Baseline: open-source library kernels. Candidates are limited to human-maintained library implementations: the official FlashAttention-4 kernels and FlashInfer (including FlashKDA). For each shape, we select the fastest candidate that passes correctness checks and is included in our evaluation. 

Baseline: kernels generated by kernel agents. Candidates include kernels submitted by kernel agents to the MLSys’26 contest and publicly released kernels from KDA-0.5, AKO4X, CAKE, and AVO (MIT HAN Lab, 2026; AKO Team, 2026c; Ye et al., 2026; NVIDIA, 2026). The contest kernels are likewise produced by kernel agents, so they belong to this category rather than to the open-source libraries. For each shape, we likewise select the fastest candidate that passes correctness checks and is included in our evaluation. No publicly released specialized-agent kernel for FA4 backward is available for this comparison. Per-shape sources and selection details are provided in Appendix C. 

Correctness verification. FA4 uses the official FlashAttention standard; every other operator uses the FlashInfer-Bench standard. Every implementation in the comparison must pass the corresponding standard. 

Timing methodology. All kernels are timed by the authors with FlashInfer-Bench in a uniform environment. Before each measurement, the L2 cache is flushed (cold L2). CUPTI records the interval from the start of the first kernel launched by the entry point to the end of the last, excluding JIT compilation, cache flushing, and synchronization overhead.

4.2 Overview: Comparison with Open-Source Libraries and Kernel Agents 

This section gives an overview of JustGoal’s results on 11 operators and 42 shapes and an-swers two different questions: whether JustGoal’s kernels are faster than human-maintained open-source libraries, and whether they are faster than the kernels of specialized kernel agents. Each shape is one session, with Fable 5.1 as the primary model; a few sessions were completed by other models after a model fallback. We report geometric mean speedups per operator; per-shape latencies, the models actually used, and baseline sources are in Appendix C. 

Against open-source libraries. The blue bars in Figure 2 are JustGoal’s results against open-source libraries. Excluding the DSA top-k indexer, whose baseline protocol remains unverified, 10 operators enter the comparison, and JustGoal is faster than the library on 8 of them, with geometric means from 1.14 × on FA4 backward to 2.34 × on GDN forward; it falls behind on FA4 forward (0.77 ×) and Kimi Delta Attention (0.99 ×). 

Against kernel agents. The orange bars are the kernel agents’ kernels against the same open-source library, taking for each shape the fastest of the MLSys’26 contest kernel and 6the publicly released kernels of the four agent systems; the ratio of the blue bar to the orange bar is therefore JustGoal’s speedup over the agent kernel. Nine of the 10 plotted operators have a comparable agent kernel (FA4 backward has none); JustGoal is faster than the fastest agent kernel on five of them, ties on MoE FP8 (1.79 × versus 1.80 ×), and falls behind on FA4 forward (0.77 × versus AVO’s 1.18 ×), DSA forward (1.66 × versus 1.72 ×), and Kimi Delta Attention (0.99 × versus CAKE’s 1.27 ×). These agent kernels were produced by each system with its own choice of model and effort, so this comparison is between the kernels themselves; what the harness itself contributes under the same model is answered in Sections 4.3 and 4.4. 

Relation to the released KDA-0.5 kernels. On the nine DSA, GDN, and MoE shapes, the kernels released in the KDA-0.5 repository reach a geometric mean of 1.29 ×, and single-shape JustGoal reaches 1.20 × on the same nine shapes, close to the release; per-shape values are in Appendix C. Our reproduction of its harness with Fable 5.1 reaches 0.91 × (same-GPU paired protocol, Table 7 in the appendix); the model and effort behind the release are not public, and this gap bounds the fidelity of the reproduction. The comparison with human-written libraries needs no further argument: JustGoal is faster on 8 of the 10 operators, and faster than the fastest agent release on 5 of the 9 operators that have one. Whether specialized machinery adds value beyond this result is a separate question. The next section first examines how the gap between full configurations changes across models; the following scope-matched comparison directly measures the mechanism gain.

4.4 Harness and Task Scope: The Fable 5.1 2 ×2

To distinguish the influence of harness configuration from that of task scope, we construct a 2×2 comparison with Fable 5.1: {JustGoal, KDA-0.5 harness } × { single shape, full workload coverage }. Full workload coverage requires one run to cover every workload in the operator’s FlashInfer-Bench definition, allowing per-shape dispatch or specialization. Table 2 reports per-operator geometric mean speedups for the four configurations; per-shape results are in Table 6 in the appendix. 8At single shape, JustGoal and the KDA-0.5 harness achieve geometric mean speedups of 1.18 × and 1.12 × over the reference kernels, respectively, so the specialized harness provides no overall improvement in this setting. With full workload coverage, the corresponding speedups are 0.71 × and 0.97 ×, giving the KDA-0.5 harness a 1.37 × advantage over Just-Goal. The harness’s relative advantage thus emerges as task scope broadens, although both configurations achieve lower overall performance than in their respective single-shape settings. On the tested tasks, these results show that Fable 5.1 can already achieve good performance when optimizing a single shape, but covering all shapes in one run does not yet match the overall performance of independent per-shape optimization. One possible explanation is that the model still struggles to cluster shapes by their computational characteristics and decompose the optimization task into subproblems that can be addressed separately. This explanation requires further experimental validation: the present comparison neither establishes the specific cause of the performance decline nor attributes it to any individual harness mechanism. These observations are limited to Fable 5.1; the other two models were not evaluated under all four configurations, and their results are reported in Table 8 in the appendix.

4.6 Optimization Case Study 

We use GDN (Gated DeltaNet) forward on long sequences (8192 tokens in 20 sequences of length 15 to 3371) to show how JustGoal crosses the contest kernel from scratch. The reference is the MLSys’26 contest kernel from the KDA-0.5 submission, which the agent could only call as a black box; the audited trajectory never reads its source. Fable 5.1 ran 8.9 hours without intervention. Latencies are the session’s own L2-flushed kernel span; the reference is 125.1 μs. Appendix B gives the full record and a second case, FA4 backward GQA. The trajectory (Figure 4) has three stages, each ending when the agent measured that its toolchain had run out of headroom. Triton started at 302 μs (0.41 ×) and the agent put its floor near 146 μs. After microbenchmarking mma.sync on B200, it hand-wrote CUDA kernels and, guided by NCU stall counters and in-kernel cycle counts, reached 165 μs at 1.3 hours; further overlap and cluster work only reached 153.8 μs. At 4.2 hours it judged the path near the shared-memory bandwidth limit and moved the recurrence to B200’s tcgen05 tensor cores, checking descriptor encodings and TMEM layouts with probe kernels. The first tcgen05 version was slower (235 μs); warp specialization, fusing the transposed O product into the recurrence’s MMA group, and running the intra-chunk kernel on the remaining SMs crossed the reference at 6.2 hours. The final version measures 95.6–97.3 μs over three processes (1.29–1.31 ×; 1.32 × under the paired re-measurement), with errors within 1.30 times the reference’s. 10 Profiling, toolchain switching and hardware probing were all the agent’s own choices. The prompt names no profiler, supplies no domain corpus and prescribes no stages, whereas KDA-0.5 encodes the same behaviors as NCU and in-kernel tracing skills, KernelWiki and three-stage prompts (Table 1). Given only the goal, the model carried out the expert workflow on its own. 

## 5 Discussion 

The value boundary of a specialized harness changes with foundation-model capability and task scope. In the equal-scope Fable 5.1 comparison, the full harness produces no overall gain on single shapes, and single-mechanism ablations show no consistent benefit from do-main knowledge or workflows. At whole-definition scope, however, the harness still helps the model advance more workloads simultaneously. This suggests that the primary value of specialized machinery may be shifting from raising the peak performance of a single target to helping models organize a broader task scope. Task scope is also critical. The perfor-mance decline under full workload coverage shows that making one implementation cover all workloads of an operator is a generality tax, and current models cannot yet partition the workloads well enough on their own to avoid it. This leaves two paths. One is to make the agent’s own partitioning ability a design goal of kernel agents and assess progress through the performance difference between single-shape and full-workload configurations in Table 2. The other is deep customization: in high-value scenarios with fixed configurations, no single implementation has to cover every workload, the generality tax disappears entirely, and the single-shape column of Table 6 is the level reached once it does. We call the latter Forge Engineering. Each implementation optimizes performance only for its target configuration but must remain correct for any input of that configuration; the costs are optimization sessions that grow with the number of configurations, plus per-configuration verification. JustGoal surpasses expert implementations on some tasks studied in this paper, suggesting that, as foundation models improve, the relative cost-effectiveness of having experts hand-write every kernel from scratch is declining. Meanwhile, the gain from a specialized harness varies with the model and task scope, showing that expert mechanisms must be matched to the actual capabilities of the foundation model. We believe a more promising path is to combine the depth of expert knowledge with the model’s broad prior knowledge, autonomous experimentation, and scalable search to approach hardware performance limits. Concretely, JustGoal can serve as a starting point that is updated with the model: let the model explore independently first, then have experts analyze optimization trajectories, profiling evidence, and failure cases to identify specific limitations in bottleneck diagnosis, hardware knowledge, search organization, or workload coverage, and add knowledge, diagnostic tools, or constraints accordingly. The value of each added mechanism should still be measured by its gain over JustGoal using the same model. 

## 6 Limitations 

Our experiments focus on selected operators and shapes on NVIDIA B200 GPUs; the appli-cability of our findings to other hardware and tasks remains to be validated. Comparisons with publicly released kernels concern the kernels themselves; the systems use different models and optimization budgets. The same-model comparisons in Section 4.3 separately examine the harness. KDA has released its general workflow, base prompts, and related skills, but the KDA-0.5 results repository primarily provides the final kernels and retest code rather than the complete prompts, workflow configuration, and search process used for that version. Our reproduction can therefore only follow the available public materi-als and may differ from the original system. KDA-0.5 also bundles domain priors with scope-organization mechanisms, and we do not ablate these two groups separately. Their distinction in Section 4.4 is our interpretation of the data. Our correctness validation remains limited by test coverage. FA4 uses the official FlashAt-tention standard, and every other kernel uses the FlashInfer-Bench standard. Passing error-tolerance checks on a finite set of test inputs does not guarantee correctness for all valid 11 inputs and execution conditions. The reported performance results should therefore be un-derstood as results obtained after passing the adopted validation procedures, rather than a comprehensive guarantee of production readiness. 

## 7 Conclusion 

We first identify a change in current model capability. Given only objectives and constraints, without predefined kernel-specific evaluation infrastructure, domain knowledge, or search workflows, a general-purpose agent can construct its own evaluation pipeline and iteratively optimize kernels. On NVIDIA B200 GPUs, JustGoal is faster than human-maintained open-source libraries on 8 of 10 operators and faster than the fastest specialized-agent re-lease on 5 of 9. Without task-time injection of kernel-specific knowledge or optimization workflows, current frontier models can establish evaluation feedback, identify performance bottlenecks, adapt optimization strategies, and cross the performance line represented by human-maintained implementations on a range of operators. We further propose an evaluation principle that does not depend on the current level of model capability. JustGoal is not a new agent architecture, but the experimental configu-ration used to observe the performance level of a goal-only setup and measure the marginal value of explicit expert mechanisms. Specialized machinery should be considered effective only when it clearly outperforms this baseline. When model capabilities change, the returns of existing mechanisms should be measured again rather than inherited from earlier results. The current model still benefits from a harness at broader task scope; whether future models do so should also be answered by a scope-matched comparison. We hope future work will not only apply new models to existing systems but also use a goal-only configuration under the same model and task scope as the baseline, reexamine each component’s contribution against it, and determine which components to retain, adapt, or remove.

B.1 FA4 Backward GQA 

We use FA4 backward GQA ( B = 8, N = 4096, H = 16, HKV = 2, D = 128, causal, bf16). The reference is FlashAttention-4 by Tri Dao et al. (Dao et al., 2026), which the agent can invoke only as a black box through its public API. We audited the complete trajectory and confirmed that the agent did not read its source code. Fable 5.1 chose Triton Gluon to implement the kernel and completed the task in four hours without human intervention. The agent first measured the reference kernel’s device time and wrote a naive fp32 imple-mentation as a correctness oracle. Its first kernel took 2771 μs and passed correctness checks on the first attempt, but was approximately 80% slower than the reference. The agent then profiled stall reasons with NCU, added ablation switches and %clock instrumentation to measure cycles per stage. It found that MMA alone required only about 830 μs, while soft-max and atomic accumulation of dQ accounted for most of the time. By reusing TMEM and switching dQ accumulation to TMA bulk reduction, it reduced latency to approximately 1320 μs after 2.5 hours. Over the next 1.4 hours, more than a dozen new versions improved the best latency only to 1278 μs (Figure 5). The agent wrote microbenchmarks and measured TMA reduction throughput of only 25–30 B/clk per SM, with roughly 2800 cycles required to release a staging buffer. Disabling dQ reduction reduced the main kernel’s latency to 931 μs. These observations pointed to the same cause: reduction reused the staging buffer for dO, blocking dO loads. The agent therefore allocated a dedicated 32 KB staging buffer for reduction and moved dO loads to drain warps using cp.async , leaving TMA exclusively for reduction. This change reduced latency to 1166 μs in a single step. Under the unified cold-L2 paired re-measurement, the final kernel runs in 1321 μs against 1676 μs for FA4, a speedup of 1.27 ×. The final kernel accumulates dQ in FP16 and rounds dS to BF16, so its internal numerics differ from FA4; its dQ, dK and dV all pass the gradient tolerance of the official FlashAttention standard, with errors at 20–33% of the allowed budget. B.2 GDN Forward, Long Sequences 

The workload, reference and measurement protocol are those of Section 4.6; this is the full record of the 8.9-hour run. The agent first wrote a Triton version that passed correctness at once but took 302 μs, 0.41 ×

the reference (Figure 4). Sweeping tile and warp configurations showed a fixed per-step overhead in the recurrence kernel and register spills in the intra-chunk kernel, with a floor of about 146 μs for the Triton path, so it abandoned Triton, first wrote microbenchmarks for 

mma.sync throughput and dependency latency on B200, and then hand-wrote two CUDA C++ kernels with mma.sync and ldmatrix , reaching 194.5 μs at 0.5 hours. It then checked spills with cuobjdump , located hot spots with NCU stall and source counters, and counted cycles per phase inside the kernel, bringing the recurrence from 4025 to 1993 cycles per step and the total to 165 μs at 1.3 hours. The next two hours gained little: overlapping the three kernels on two streams with per-block flags made things slower; several launch failures and deadlocks were debugged with compute-sanitizer; thread-block clusters with TMA multicast let 8 CTAs share one broadcast of the W/K tiles; and globaltimer timelines of every CTA’s work items showed a busy ratio of only 0.74 with about 5 μs gaps between items, so it switched to static round-robin assignment to remove the atomics. It consulted the PTX ISA documentation and CUTLASS headers, wrote probe kernels to verify descriptor encodings, swizzles and TMEM layouts, and rewrote the recurrence kernel. The first tcgen05 version took 235 μs, about 3 μs per step, bottlenecked by 24 serial TMEM waits. Finally it made the intra-chunk kernel 15 process one v head per CTA with the longest sequences first, reaching 94.6 μs at 8.5 hours; the final version measured 95.6 to 97.3 μs over three independent processes, 1.29–1.31 × the reference, with output and state errors at 1.00 and 1.30 times the reference error. Under the same-GPU paired re-measurement it reaches 1.32 × over the MLSys’26 contest kernel shipped with KDA-0.5.
