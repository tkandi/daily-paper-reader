## Abstract 

Sparse Mixture-of-Experts (MoE) models offer scalable capacity for Web applications, but uneven routing creates computation and communication bottlenecks. Existing placement and replication methods redistribute this workload, yet additional expert copies compete with token-related buffers for limited GPU memory. We present LRMoE, a memory-aware MoE training system that jointly recalibrates backward demand and balances the retained workload. During forward execution, it plans retention quotas and expert placement under resource constraints while preserving all routed computations. During backpropagation, a load recalibration metric estimates each token-expert pair’s contribution to the expert update and selects pairs within these quotas. A capacity-aware routing algorithm distributes retained pairs among eligible instances of their original experts. Physical compaction then forms smaller com-munication payloads and variable-size grouped GEMMs, reducing backward traffic, computation, and execution-buffer demand. Ex-periments on Qwen3-30B-A3B and GLM4.5-106B-A12B show that LRMoE reduces cumulative MoE-layer training time to fixed-loss targets by 21.2-44.8% relative to SYMI.

## 1 Introduction 

Web-scale search and recommendation services rely on learned models [1, 9]. Training these models is an infrastructure concern: finite GPU memory and interconnect bandwidth constrain the work-loads that a cluster can support. Sparse Mixture-of-Experts (MoE) architectures increase parameter capacity while activating only a small subset of experts per token [2, 5, 13, 21, 25]. For MoE-based Web services, this shifts part of the scaling challenge from dense computation to managing routed work across devices. We study this Web-infrastructure problem at the training-system level: how to coordinate expert placement, communication, and backward work-load under a fixed per-GPU resource budget, rather than improve a particular search or recommendation model. 

> ∗Zhida Jiang and Zhen Chen are co-corresponding authors.

Expert Parallelism (EP) distributes experts across GPUs and ex-changes routed token–expert pairs through all-to-all communica-tion [6, 13, 21]. Each pair contributes to forward and backward communication, expert computation, and saved-activation storage. Input-dependent routing can concentrate work on GPUs hosting popular experts, delaying synchronous execution [35]. Replication distributes this work but consumes memory and introduces state-transfer and gradient-synchronization costs. Even balanced place-ment can therefore leave more backward work than the available memory and communication capacity can accommodate. The re-sulting resource-management problem concerns both where routed pairs execute and how much backward work is retained [28, 35]. Existing systems adapt expert placement and execution to uneven loads. FasterMoE shadows popular experts, FlexMoE adapts placement, and SmartMoE searches hybrid parallelization plans [8, 19, 31]. SYMI decouples model parameters from opti-mizer states, while LAER-MoE reconstructs expert instances from sharded parameters [17, 26]. UltraEP responds to realized post-gating loads, and TopoEP incorporates communication topology into planning [28, 35]. These techniques improve utilization, but replication still consumes expert storage and incurs state-transfer and gradient-synchronization costs. For a fixed admitted token– expert pair set, placement and replication change where work executes without reducing its logical computation. Replicas also compete with token buffers for memory. Under a constrained budget, redistributing that workload can therefore leave total demand above the available service capacity. Selective-Backprop reduces backward work by prioritizing high-loss training examples [10]. We instead select routed token–expert pairs by their estimated contributions to the expert update. Once an MoE layer receives its incoming gradients, these can be com-bined with saved activations and routing weights to characterize pairwise contributions [7]. At a protected expert projection, a pair’s weight-gradient contribution is the outer product of its weighted incoming gradient and saved projection input. We derive the Load Recalibration Metric (LRM) from the squared norm of this contribu-tion, allowing pairs to be ranked without materializing individual weight-gradient matrices. LRM is a projection-level proxy for select-ing backward contributions under a limited budget. Because these gradients are unavailable during forward execution, we preserve all routed forward computations and defer contribution-aware selec-tion until backpropagation. MegaBlocks addresses irregular expert execution with block-sparse computation [6]. Our selection must likewise translate into smaller physical workloads. Removing work arXiv:2610.37720v1 [cs.AI] 27 Oct 2026 Jiaming Hu, Zhida Jiang, Zhaolong Xing, Yu Li, Zhen Chen, and Ke Zhang 

from an unconstrained path does not accelerate execution, and reducing total work without rerouting can leave imbalances intact. The selected backward update remains approximate, so its effect on learning must be evaluated empirically rather than inferred from pairwise scores alone. We present LRMoE, a memory-aware MoE training system that combines contribution-aware selection with resource-constrained quota planning and load routing. Its two-phase design separates how much backward work the system can afford from which pairs fill that budget. After forward gating reveals the routed load, the controller sets retention quotas for each expert and plans replica placement. These decisions account for full forward execution and expected backward work within memory and communication lim-its. The full forward pass then preserves the context needed for backpropagation. Once gradients arrive, LRM selects the highest-scoring pairs within each expert’s quota. Capacity-aware routing assigns retained pairs to eligible instances, and physical compaction groups their gradients and saved activations by destination and ex-pert. The resulting dense payloads reduce backward all-to-all traffic. Variable-size grouped GEMMs use the actual retained row counts without padding workloads across experts. An inverse mapping re-stores gradients to the layout expected by surrounding layers. This coordination reduces backward communication, expert computa-tion, and execution-buffer demand, while requiring selection and packing overhead to be included in evaluation. Our contributions are: 

• We introduce a contribution-aware calibration metric and reduce backward communication and computation through grouped-GEMM workloads. 

• We develop memory-aware routing that re-balances re-tained workloads across eligible expert instances. 

• On Qwen3-30B-A3B and GLM4.5-106B-A12B, LRMoE re-duces cumulative MoE-layer training time to fixed-loss targets by 21.2-44.8% versus state-to-the-art baselines, a separate GPU replay shows a 57.8% reduction in the subto-tal of expert-placement, replica, and token-buffer memory relative to fixed ownership. 

## 2 Background and Motivation 2.1 MoE and Load Balancing 

In Transformer models [27], a Mixture-of-Experts (MoE) layer replaces a dense feed-forward block with experts and a learned router. For each token, the router activates only a small subset of experts, increasing model capacity without executing every pa-rameter [5, 13, 25]. Expert parallelism (EP) partitions these experts across GPUs: it dispatches each routed token to the GPU hosting its selected expert and combines the returned expert outputs [6, 21]. We call one token–expert selection a routed pair . Routing makes the workload data dependent. Pair counts change across inputs, layers, and iterations, producing unequal expert computation and all-to-all traffic across GPUs. Because a collective and an MoE layer finish at the pace of their slowest participant, the busiest GPU becomes a straggler while others wait [8, 19, 26, 28, 31]. Training-time routing regularizers encourage balance in expectation, but cannot ensure balance for every realized layer invocation. Runtime balancing is therefore still required for the instantaneous load. BASE layers use balanced token assignment, whereas Expert Choice fixes expert bucket sizes [14, 34]. Gating Dropout reduces communication by bypassing global routing for some tokens [15]. These approaches modify forward routing, while our selection operates on backward contributions. 

## 2.2 Limitations of Existing Methods 

Production MoE training must accommodate model parameters, gradients, optimizer states, and runtime buffers within a fixed GPU budget. When many experts share each GPU, persistent training states leave limited memory for additional expert instances and transient execution buffers. Offloading moves training states to host memory [12, 24]. Input-dependent routing further concentrates de-mand on selected experts, so sufficient aggregate resources do not necessarily imply that every device can serve its assigned work-load. Existing systems provide spatial flexibility through expert placement, replication, and adaptive parallelization [8, 19, 31]. Effi-cientMoE adapts expert scheduling to predicted loads [30], while NetMoE rearranges samples to exploit routing locality [16]. SYMI and LAER-MoE reduce state-management costs [17, 26], while Ul-traEP adapts to realized post-gating loads [28]. These mechanisms can improve utilization and communication locality, but they pre-serve the logical expert computation required by a fixed admitted token–expert pair set. Their ability to accommodate hot experts therefore remains constrained by the capacity available for exe-cuting that work. Consider a common backward service window. ## 3 LRMoE Design 3.1 Design Overview 

Figure 2 presents the design of LRMoE, which combines layer-wise load calibration, replica-aware load routing, and compact grouped-GEMM execution. The workflow separates forward-time resource planning from backward-time contribution selection. The model router first determines each token’s logical experts and routing weights, while the placement plan identifies their physical exe-cution instances. After resource admission, the complete forward pass dispatches every routed token–expert pair, executes the expert computations, and combines their outputs into the dense representa-tion passed to the next layer. The runtime preserves the activations, required expert intermediates, routing weights, and pair identi-ties, together with the forward executor and parameter version, establishing the correspondence needed for subsequent backward execution. Before expert forward begins, the controller observes the routed demand 𝑛 𝑒 of each expert and determines the backward retention quotas 𝐾 𝑒 under the admitted placement and guarded resource ca-pacities. Quota planning accommodates as much backward demand as the declared capacity model permits, while accounting separately for the complete forward workload, saved-state residency, replica storage, and execution buffers. During forward planning, a capacity-constrained integer allocation assigns 𝑋 𝑒𝑔 retained pairs of expert 𝑒 to GPU 𝑔 , with Í𝑔 𝑋 𝑒𝑔 = 𝐾 𝑒 and nonzero assignments permitted only at eligible instances of that expert. The planner minimizes the peak assigned GPU load for the fixed placement rather than dividing each expert’s demand equally among replicas. During backward, rerouting realizes these instance-level quotas using the selected pair identities, preserving their logical experts and assigning every retained pair exactly once. Eligibility requires the correct expert parameters and access to the matching forward state at the recorded version. Because the selected identities are unknown during planning, the admitted state-access and commu-nication envelope must remain valid for any selected set satisfying the quotas. Backward realization therefore neither introduces new replicas nor changes the previously admitted workload. The compact runtime translates the retention mask and rout-ing assignments into physical communication and computation. It gathers retained gradient rows and their matching activations and intermediates in a consistent pair order, grouping them by destination and expert instance. Counts and offsets define compact All-to-All payloads and variable-size grouped GEMMs. FC2, the acti-vation function, FC1, and backward Dispatch use the same retained identities, omitted rows are not processed in full-sized masked ten-sors. Returned input-gradient contributions are scatter-added at their original token and routing-slot positions in zero-initialized dense buffers. This restores the layout, not discarded contributions, router gradients follow a separate path. Replica parameter gradients are summed at logical owners before the required accumulation and distributed reductions, one optimizer update per logical expert, and weight synchronization.

## 3.4 Repacking GEMM with Load Calibration 

Figure 4 shows how selective dropping is converted into an actual reduction in expert computation. After identifying the retained token-expert pairs, we remove the dropped rows and repack the Jiaming Hu, Zhida Jiang, Zhaolong Xing, Yu Li, Zhen Chen, and Ke Zhang 

corresponding gradients and saved expert states into contiguous, expert-specific buffers. The retained row counts and offsets are then passed to a variable-size grouped GEMM. Each expert therefore processes only its retained rows, without computing over zero-filled rows or padding the input back to its original size. This distinction is essential. Zero-masking the dropped rows while preserving the original tensor shape leaves the GEMM size and its memory requirement largely unchanged. Repacking instead reduces the row dimension of all four expert-backward GEMMs: the FC1 and FC2 data-gradient and weight-gradient operations. The resulting gradients are equivalent to those produced by the zero-masked implementation for the same retained pairs, apart from ordinary floating-point differences. After the expert computation, the retained results are scattered back to their original positions, and zeros are restored only before the next dense-layer boundary. Smaller packed GEMMs also reduce the physical capacity re-quired for overloaded experts. Expert replication is provisioned to accommodate the peak number of rows assigned to each expert. Once selective dropping reduces this workload, fewer physical in-stances are needed to keep the per-replica load within the GPU capacity. In Figure 4, the original expert loads are reduced from 

[4, 2, 3] to [3, 1, 2]. An expert whose retained workload now fits on one GPU no longer requires an additional replica. The set of logical experts remains unchanged, our method reduces only the redundant physical replicas, along with their parameter memory, temporary buffers, and communication.

## 4.7 Ablation Study 

Table 2 compares our LRM against alternative backward selection criteria over a sustained 10,000-step training trajectory, contrasting it with a squared gradient norm approach, an absolute gradient magnitude baseline, and a pure random selection mechanism. Our recalibration metric achieves the lowest mean ΔPPL across both evaluated architectures, registering an average deviation of just 0.012474 for Qwen3-30B-A3B and an exceptionally tight 0.003464 for GLM4.5-106B-A12B. Both gradient-only metrics outperform random selection but produce larger PPL deviations than LRM. The ordering is consistent across both models: the squared gradient norm is the stronger gradient-only baseline, followed by the abso-lute gradient magnitude, while random selection yields the largest deviations. This empirical result strongly supports our core design hypothesis: properly selecting retained token to expert pairs re-quires considering the established routing strength and the saved expert-state magnitude alongside the raw backward gradient signal. In distributed Expert Parallelism, two routed pairs can receive nearly identical upstream gradient norms but possess entirely dif-ferent initial routing weights or saved activation magnitudes. A gradient-only rule assigns them identical importance, even when their actual mathematical contributions to an expert weight projec-tion differ drastically. Our LRM accurately and efficiently accounts for these diverse factors. Crucially, it achieves this comprehensive scoring without requiring the computationally prohibitive materi-alization of full per-pair weight-gradient matrices. The tight PPL comparison provides strong empirical validation that this efficient rank-one local approximation preserves global model convergence and training quality over extensive training horizons. 

## 5 Related Work 

MoE routing and load balancing. BASE layers balance token assignment, while Expert Choice fixes expert buckets and varies the number of selected experts per token [14, 34]. StableMoE distills and freezes a router to stabilize assignments [3]. Gating Dropout by-passes global routing for some tokens to reduce communication [15]. These methods change forward routing. FasterMoE shadows pop-ular experts and can alter expert selection to avoid network con-gestion [8]. FlexMoE adapts placement, while SmartMoE searches hybrid parallelization plans [19, 31]. SYMI separates parameters from optimizer states, and LAER-MoE reconstructs expert instances from sharded parameters [17, 26]. UltraEP and topology-aware TopoEP respond to realized post-gating loads [28, 35]. LRMoE in-stead selects backward pairs under quotas and balances them across instances of their original experts. 

Selective backward computation. Selective-Backprop priori-tizes high-loss training examples [10]. In contrast, LRMoE ranks routed pairs within each expert’s quota. Its projection-level score uses the factorization underlying efficient per-example gradient-norm computation [7], applied to saved projection inputs and weighted incoming gradients. 

Memory and communication reduction. ZeRO partitions training states to remove redundancy [22]. ZeRO-Offload and ZeRO-Infinity extend memory management across GPU, CPU, and NVMe [23, 24]. ES-MoE offloads expert parameters and pipelines execution [12]. FlashAttention reduces attention HBM accesses through IO-aware tiling [4]. These methods do not select expert-update contributions. RCMoE quantizes all-to-all exchanges and sparsifies all-reduce gradients [29]. Our row selection instead reduces token-dependent communication and expert computation without changing parameter dimensions. 

Parallel execution and overlap. PipeDream pipelines mini-batches across partitioned layers, while Alpa automatically plans parallel strategies [18, 33]. Within MoE layers, MegaBlocks uses block-sparse computation for irregular loads without capacity-based token dropping [6]. Lancet, COMET, and FSMoE co-schedule and overlap communication with computation across different gran-ularities [11, 20, 32]. ## 6 Conclusion 

In this work, we present LRMoE to tackle load imbalances and memory bottlenecks in Expert Parallelism. Unlike balancers that merely redistribute a fixed workload, LRMoE tightly coordinates contribution-aware load recalibration with capacity-aware rout-ing under strict memory and bandwidth constraints. It selectively retains high-value token-expert pairs to fit within planned quo-tas and routes them across admitted expert instances. Compact grouped GEMMs then translate this reduced workload into smaller communication payloads and lower computation costs without zero-padding overhead. By keeping the forward pass intact for ac-curate contribution scoring, LRMoE advances MoE balancing by jointly controlling the size and spatial distribution of backward computation.

## 7 Derivation and Optimality of Token–Expert Recalibration 

This appendix proves Proposition 3.1 and the optimality of the re-sulting drop rule under the fidelity objective used by our recalibra-tion controller. The first result is an exact, projection-level identity for one routed pair. The second result concerns selection: for a fixed number of equal-size rows to remove, dropping the rows with the smallest scores minimizes the sum of their isolated projection-gradient perturbations. We state the assumptions explicitly because this selection result is not a claim about the nonlinear loss or the entire optimizer trajectory.

## 7.4 Scope of the result 

The proof relies on four declared conditions. First, the forward route and saved projection inputs are fixed, recalibration acts only on the completed route’s backward contributions. Second, 𝜙 𝑢 is the actual input to the protected linear projection. For a nonlinear multi-layer expert, the same formula is exact for the selected projection but is not the squared norm of the expert’s complete parameter-gradient contribution. Third, the closed-form drop rule assumes equal row costs and a fixed cardinality. Unequal byte costs lead to a knapsack problem, while replica, topology, and HBM constraints lead to Eq. (17). Fourth, the objective protects a local projection-gradient quantity. It is neither an identity for nonlinear loss change nor a guarantee about future optimizer states or model quality, those effects are evaluated empirically.
