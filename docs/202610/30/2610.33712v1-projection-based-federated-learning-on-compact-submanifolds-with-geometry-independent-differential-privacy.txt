## Abstract 

Federated learning trains a shared model across clients without exchanging their data, yet the updates they transmit still leak information about individual records. Enforcing differential privacy is harder still when the model parameters lie on a Riemannian manifold. Existing methods rebuild the noise added for privacy in the geometry of each new iterate, aggregate through intrinsic maps that often lack a closed form, and prove convergence only in restricted regimes. We introduce DP-RFedProj , a record-level differentially private federated learning method for compact submanifolds that aggregates through closed-form projections and privatises gradients in a fixed ambient geometry. Each client may run any stochastic first-order optimiser, take several local steps, and participate only intermittently. An explicit record-level f -DP guarantee holds for the entire training transcript, independently of the manifold, the aggregation scheme and the local optimiser. We establish convergence of DP-RFedProj when each client runs Riemannian SGD locally and the server aggregates by projected averaging, and show that privacy enters the rate through a term scaling with the manifold’s intrinsic dimension rather than the ambient one. Synthetic experiments on federated PCA over the Grassmann manifold corroborate these findings. On EEG motor-imagery classification with SPDNet, whose weights lie on the Stiefel manifold, the method outperforms a Euclidean baseline at every privacy budget considered. In both experimental settings, our accounting requires five to six times less noise than that of the closest prior method for the same budget, which translates into higher utility.

1.1 Setting and motivation 

Federated learning (FL) enables a set of clients, each holding its own local data, to collaboratively train a shared model under the coordination of a central server, without exchanging raw data (McMahan et al., 2017; Konečný et al., 2016). It is well suited to settings where data are scarce or subject-specific and where centralised pooling is precluded by confidentiality or regulatory constraints, as in medical imaging (Sheller et al., 2020) or satellite imagery (Ben Youssef et al., 2024). The standard protocol proceeds over a sequence of communication rounds. In each round, the server broadcasts the current global model to the clients, each client trains it locally on its own data, and the resulting updates are transmitted back and aggregated into a new global model that seeds the next round. When the updates are Euclidean parameters, the canonical aggregation scheme is FedAvg (McMahan et al., 2017), which forms the next global model by arithmetically averaging the client updates. In a range of applications, however, the model parameters are constrained to a Riemannian manifold. Such structure for instance arises when signals are classified through their symmetric positive definite (SPD) spatial covariance matrices, as in EEG brain–computer interfaces (BCI) (Barachant et al., 2012) or image recognition (Li et al., 2017), and it underpins architectures such as SPDNet (Huang & Van Gool, 2017; Brooks et al., 2019), whose orthonormal weight layers lie on the Stiefel manifold. Riemannian federated learning adapts the protocol to this geometry: clients optimise locally with Riemannian gradient methods, and the server aggregates parameters constrained to the manifold. Aggregation is the central difficulty. The arithmetic mean of the client parameters may fall off the manifold, and even when it stays on it, it can ignore the underlying geometry. The natural replacement is the Riemannian (Fréchet) mean, which rarely admits a closed form and is instead approximated through the manifold’s geometric maps, as in the classical Karcher flow (Karcher, 2014). Federated learning is often motivated by privacy, but keeping data on-device does not by itself protect it. We consider an honest-but-curious server: it follows the training protocol but observes all messages exchanged by the clients. The updates a client transmits are computed from its records and leak information about them: reconstruction attacks recover training examples from shared gradients (Zhu et al., 2019), and membership-inference attacks reveal whether a particular record was used (Shokri et al., 2017). Randomisation severs that link, since adding calibrated noise makes the output nearly as likely with or without any single record. Differential privacy (DP) (Dwork et al., 2006; 2014) makes this precise, bounding how much the output distribution can change when a single record is altered. It comes at two granularities: record-level DP makes any single training example undetectable, while client-level DP conceals a client’s entire dataset and thus whether that client took part at all (Geyer et al., 2017; McMahan et al., 2018). We target the former. A record enters each local update through the gradient contributions it induces, and the standard approach to protecting it is to bound these contributions by clipping before masking them with noise. To certify privacy, one must ensure that an adversary cannot tell whether any particular record was used, even after observing all the messages exchanged throughout training. In the Euclidean setting, privacy accounting has tightened across successive frameworks: from the original ( ε, δ )-DP bound (Dwork et al., 2014), loose under composition, through its Rényi-DP refinement (Mironov, 2017; Balle et al., 2018), which composes sharply but needs care under subsampling, to the f -DP / Gaussian-DP calculus of Dong et al. (2022), which tightens both in closed form. On a Riemannian manifold, the geometry complicates both steps. The norm used for clipping generally varies from point to point, and adding a noise vector to a point may not return a point of the manifold. A natural way to preserve the required vector-space structure is therefore to perturb gradients in the tangent space. Han et al. (2024) formally introduce this tangent-space Gaussian mechanism and privatise Riemannian SGD (DP-RSGD), while Utpala et al. (2023) add a variance-reduced variant (DP-RSVRG), later used by Huang et al. (2026b). Existing Riemannian privacy guarantees (Huang et al., 2026b) rely on geometry-dependent sensitivity bounds and composition of per-round guarantees. In contrast, Zheng et al. 1.2 Related work 

These challenges place our work at the intersection of federated learning, Riemannian geometry, and differential privacy. We first review existing approaches to Riemannian federated learning, then discuss projection-based methods on compact submanifolds, and finally position our approach relative to the only prior work. On general manifolds, aggregation goes through the exponential and logarithmic maps and a transport. Li & Ma (2022) gives the first Riemannian FL framework, RFedSVRG, averaging by a Karcher-flow step with a variance-reduced local solver, later extended with second-order information (Xiao et al., 2024; 2025). Huang et al. (2026a) instead aggregates the gradient streams accumulated along the local trajectories (AGS), which needs only a retraction and a vector transport and admits multiple local steps under partial participation, at the cost of an analysis specific to Riemannian SGD: aggregating gradients rather than iterates leaves no room for a different local optimiser. For schemes that aggregate the clients’ final iterates, no analysis combines multiple local steps and partial participation, even without privacy. Neither addresses privacy, and both rely on maps that on Stiefel are unavailable in closed form or recovered only by an iterative solve (Table 1). On compact submanifolds, the nearest-point projection offers an alternative to general retractions, both for keeping local iterates on the manifold and for aggregating them at the server. Zhang et al. (2024) adopt it to support multiple local epochs, and Wang et al. (2025) carry the same construction to the zeroth-order setting, substituting a gradient-free estimator for the Riemannian gradient. Neither, however, addresses privacy, and both use a per-client control variate to mitigate client drift. This couples aggregation to the local optimiser and confines their analyses to full participation, since every variate must be refreshed each round. For SPDNet, Pautrel et al. (2026a) first proposed projection-based aggregation rules for the Stiefel weights of its BiMap layers, without privacy, and Pautrel et al. (2026b) considered the same FedSPDNet setting in a personalised mode. We follow this projection-based line but add a record-level privacy layer and drop the control variate, leaving aggregation a pure function of the final iterates (Table 1). Bringing privacy, federation, and manifold structure together, PriRFed (Huang et al., 2026b) is, to our knowledge, the only prior method to combine all three. It targets record-level privacy under an honest-but-curious server, but on a general manifold: it aggregates through a retraction and its inverse, privatises with the tangent-Gaussian mechanism of Han et al. (2024) in the underlying geometry, and analyses convergence for the DP-RSGD and DP-RSVRG local solvers. The noise construction is rebuilt at every iterate, and the training run is accounted by composing per-round ( ε, δ ) pairs. Convergence is established only when multiple local steps and multiple clients are not combined. Aggregation, finally, needs a retraction and its inverse in tandem, which on Stiefel is available in closed form in at most one direction. 31.3 Contributions 

No existing method brings together projection-based aggregation, partial participation, multiple local steps, and differential privacy. We close this gap with DP-RFedProj , a record-level differentially private Riemannian federated learning algorithm for compact submanifolds under the Euclidean-induced metric. Even without privacy, the method is the first iterate-aggregation scheme for Riemannian FL whose convergence is established with multiple local steps and partial participation together. Moreover, it is the second method after PriRFed to bring record-level differential privacy to Riemannian FL, and the first with a guarantee that does not depend on the manifold. Our contributions are fourfold. • A projection-based, optimiser-agnostic private FL algorithm. Closed-form projections do the geometric work at both stages of a round: they keep each client’s local steps on the manifold, and they aggregate the returned iterates at the server. We use two aggregation rules on that basis, both first proposed by Pautrel et al. (2026a) for the Stiefel weights of SPDNet, without privacy, and stated here on any compact submanifold: ProjAvg , which projects the ambient mean, and RLAvg , which retracts the average of the tangent-lifted displacements at the current iterate. Both fit a common template that also covers the prior Karcher-flow and retraction-based rules, which differ from ours only in the submission and the server map (Table 2). Because private local training is decoupled from aggregation, each client can pair any of them with any stochastic first-order optimiser consuming minibatch gradients (SGD, momentum, Adam). • A geometry-free privacy guarantee in closed form. We privatise each client’s gradients through a fixed ambient geometry, drawing the noise ambiently and projecting it onto the tangent space at each iterate. The resulting guarantee is a closed-form expression in the f -DP/GDP calculus, with record-level subsampling amplification and no dependence on the number of participating clients. It holds across the template’s aggregators and is independent of the manifold and of the local optimisers. Against the closest prior accounting, which composes per-round ( ε, δ ) pairs by advanced composition, it certifies the same budget with five to six times less noise in the settings of our experiments (Sections 6 and 7). • Convergence under partial participation and multiple local steps. Taking Riemannian SGD as the local solver, we give the first convergence analysis on compact submanifolds combining projection-based aggregation, partial participation, and multiple local steps under data heterogeneity, bypassing the logarithmic, exponential, and inverse-retraction maps entirely. The result is new even without privacy: setting σdp = 0 recovers the first such guarantee for an iterate-aggregation scheme. The analysis covers the clipped algorithm for any threshold, with the clipping bias as an explicit term. The leading privacy term enters the rate through the manifold’s intrinsic dimension rather than the ambient one. • Numerical experiments. On federated PCA over the Grassmann manifold, where the analysis applies fully, the cost of privacy scales with dM and 1 /k as predicted, and our accounting reduces PriRFed’s optimality gap by a factor of 10 to 27. We then instantiate the framework on EEG motor-imagery classification with SPDNet (Huang & Van Gool, 2017) on three MOABB datasets (Chevallier et al., 2024). We compare DP-RFedProj applied to SPDNet against the Euclidean network EEGNet (Lawhern et al., 2018), trained by standard private federated averaging. SPDNet outperforms EEGNet at every privacy budget and at full and half client participation. Among the aggregation rules, our projection-based scheme matches the accuracy of the retraction-based one while aggregating an order of magnitude faster. Code reproducing all experiments is available at https://anonymous.4open.science/r/dp-rfedproj-83B0/ .Section 2 fixes the background, Section 3 presents the algorithm, Sections 4 and 5 give its privacy guarantee and convergence analysis, Sections 6 and 7 report the synthetic and EEG experiments and Section 8 discusses limitations and perspectives.

Submanifolds and the induced metric. A smooth manifold M of dimension dM, embedded in a Euclidean space ( E, ⟨· , ·⟩ ) of ambient dimension D, is a subset locally diffeomorphic to RdM via smoothly compatible charts. The tangent space TxM at x ∈ M is the dM-dimensional subspace of E spanned by velocities of smooth curves through x. The design below is the one instantiated by PriRFed (Huang et al., 2026b): both the privatisation and the aggregation are carried out in the manifold’s own geometry. It is the baseline our construction departs from.

2.4 Limitations of the intrinsic pipeline 

The design of Sections 2.3.1–2.3.2, instantiated by PriRFed (Huang et al., 2026b), carries four limitations, which the rest of the paper lifts. (1) A mechanism bound to a moving geometry. Both operations of (7) are anchored at the current iterate, so the noise construction must be redone at each of them, either via a fresh gx-orthonormal basis, or the transport of Utpala et al. Existing analyses of iterate aggregation cover multiple local steps only when one client participates, and several clients only when each takes one step (Li & Ma, 2022; Huang et al., 2026b) but never both, the regime federated training actually runs in. This holds with and without privacy. AGS (Huang et al., 2026a) covers both, but aggregates gradient streams and is thus tied to Riemannian SGD. The lifting map is again the obstruction: it is anchored at a base point that moves at every local step, so a client’s successive increments are never directly comparable, and reconciling them drags the curvature of the manifold into the constants. The multi-step results moreover assume the data identically distributed across clients, setting heterogeneity aside. 

## 3 Projection-Based Private Federated Learning on Compact Submanifolds 

3.1 Structural properties 

Lifting the limitations of Section 2.4 costs generality: where PriRFed assumes a general Riemannian manifold, we assume two structural properties. (P1) Euclidean-induced metric (1): the base-point norm ∥·∥ x is the restriction of the ambient norm ∥·∥ to 

TxM, so gradient formation, clipping, and noise sampling reduce to applying the tangent projector Px

to ambient quantities. (P2) Compactness of M: it guarantees a manifold projection Π : E → M , which returns updates to M

through the retraction R x(u) = Π( x + u). Each property allows us to lift a distinct limitation. Property P1 detaches the mechanism from the geometry (Limitation 1): clipping and noise act on iterate-independent ambient quantities, so no per-iterate construction is needed. Property P2 supplies the projection the algorithm is built on. It replaces the retraction and its inverse by the single map Π, applied at both the local and the aggregation stage, and the nonlinear lift by the linear tangent projection (Limitation 3). Specialising to the nearest-point projection (2) gives a map with controlled regularity in a tube around the manifold. The convergence analysis uses proximal smoothness and tube stability to control the projection error that Π introduces at each aggregation. This allows us to analyse multiple local steps and several clients together, under heterogeneity (Limitation 4). Limitation 2 needs neither property: we lift it by re-accounting the mechanism in the tight f -DP/GDP calculus. 9Modularity guides the design: server aggregation depends on each client’s private local training only through its final iterate, so the two FL stages are fully decoupled. Sections 3.2 and 3.3 develop them in turn, and Section 3.4 assembles them into Algorithm 1.

Choice of aggregator. ## 4 Privacy Analysis 

The honest-but-curious server observes the whole transcript VT (5), from which no client’s record should be inferable: we require VT to be private under the record-level adjacency of Definition 1. We adapt the 13 standard DP-SGD privacy argument (Abadi et al., 2016; Dong et al., 2022) to projection-based federated learning, and obtain a record-level guarantee for the whole training run. Section 4.1 recalls the tools of the f -DP calculus, Section 4.2 states the guarantee, and Section 4.3 derives, under additional asymptotic assumptions, what drives its privacy level. 

4.1 Privacy accounting in the f -DP calculus 

We measure privacy with the trade-off-function calculus of Dong et al. (2022), which quantifies by hypothesis testing how hard two adjacent outputs are to distinguish, and composes tightly. 

Definition 4 (f -DP and GDP (Dong et al., 2022, Def. 2–4)) . ## 8 Conclusion 

We introduced DP-RFedProj , a record-level differentially private federated learning algorithm for compact submanifolds. Its privacy guarantee comes from privatising gradients in a fixed ambient geometry, so it holds for any manifold, aggregator and local optimiser. Its convergence analysis comes from aggregating through closed-form projections, and covers multiple local steps, partial participation and heterogeneous clients together. On federated PCA, the experiments match the theory: the cost of privacy grows with the intrinsic dimension and decreases with the number of clients per round. The framework requires a compact submanifold under the Euclidean-induced metric. This excludes iterate-dependent metrics without an ambient projection, such as the affine-invariant metric on SPD matrices. The convergence rate is proved for Riemannian SGD at a constant stepsize, while the EEG experiments use Adam with a schedule. The accounting charges the full stream of privatised gradients rather than the submissions the server observes. This keeps it optimiser-agnostic, but leaves room for tighter, optimiser-specific analyses. Several directions extend this work. Subject-level privacy is a natural next step for EEG: a subject records strongly correlated trials, so protecting one trial bounds what the server learns about that trial, not about the subject. Personalisation, where each client keeps part of the model local (Pautrel et al., 2026b), raises the question of how the shared and local parts split the privacy budget. Finally, batch normalisation, Euclidean or Riemannian (Brooks et al., 2019), breaks the per-record sensitivity bound. Privatising its statistics, or using per-sample normalisations compatible with differential privacy (Ponomareva et al., 2023), could recover accuracy without weakening the guarantee. 

## Broader Impact Statement 

This work aims to make collaborative training on sensitive records, such as EEG recordings, safer. Its guarantee holds only under the stated threat model: an honest-but-curious server and record-level protection. Privacy also costs accuracy, so the budget should be chosen for the protection it affords, not for the accuracy it permits.

D.1 Architectures D.2 The batch-normalization problem 

Lemma 8 bounds the sensitivity by 2 C/B because each per-sample gradient depends on its own record only, so substituting one record changes one term of the minibatch average. In training mode, batch normalization uses the minibatch mean and variance, so every per-sample gradient depends on all B records. Substituting one record then changes all B terms, and the sensitivity grows to 2 C (Ponomareva et al., 2023, Sec. 5.5.1). We therefore use EEGNet without batch normalization in every run, so that the guarantee covers all training records. This choice also puts the two architectures on an equal footing, since SPDNet uses no batch normalization either. 

D.3 Hyperparameter selection 

Hyperparameters are selected on the validation accuracy of the last iterate, averaged over three tuning seeds disjoint from the ten reported ones. For SPDNet with ProjAvg and for EEGNet, we select ( η0, B ) on logarithmic grids at fixed T , then the horizon T as the smallest one within one standard error of the best. We do this centrally, then in the federated setting without privacy, then again for ( η0, B ) at each privacy budget, with T kept at its non-private value. D.6 Privacy accounting 

The accounting charges the subsampling rate q = B/m ⋆ of the smallest client and the τ = ⌈mmax /B ⌉ local steps of the largest, over T τ steps. Under privacy, client i runs ⌊mi/B ⌋ ≤ τ steps on fresh minibatches drawn without replacement, so τ is an upper bound. For each target ( ε, δ ), σdp is the smallest noise scale whose certified ε for Cq (Gμ0 )⊗T τ meets the target. It is calibrated for each seed, since m⋆ and mmax depend on the split. PriRFed accounting (Figure 4a) holds the mechanism ( C, B, q, τ, T ) fixed and changes only the outer accounting. PriRFed (Huang et al., 2026b, Thm. 7) turns each round into a pair ( εr , δ r) and composes the T rounds, optimized over the split δ = ˆδ + T δ r . We feed it our numerically certified per-round privacy profile Cq (Gμ0 )⊗τ

in place of its published closed form. PriRFed also amplifies each round by client sampling (Huang et al., 2026b, Lem. 1). We do not amplify by client subsampling, since the server draws and records St (Remark 7).
