Abstract —In federated learning, mitigating class imbalance is essential to improve minority-class performance. A common approach to address this problem is to augment minority-class samples to achieve local class balance. Existing approaches treat augmentation as a heuristic and do not establish how the amount of augmentation influences the convergence of federated learning, leading to excessive augmentation and increased training time. To address this limitation, we first establish the relationship between augmentation and the convergence behavior of fed-erated learning. Leveraging this insight, we propose DAFL, a distributed augmentation framework that determines the mini-mum augmentation required for each client-class pair by jointly minimizing augmentation and training time while constraining global class imbalance, thereby improving minority-class F1-score. Experimental results demonstrate that DAFL consistently improves minority-class F1-score while substantially reducing training time, particularly under severe global class imbalance and high label proportion imbalance. 

Index Terms —Federated learning, resource-constrained clients, class imbalance, label proportion imbalance. 

I. I NTRODUCTION 

With growing interest in training machine-learning models directly on edge devices, Federated Learning (FL) has become a promising paradigm for privacy-preserving training across distributed edge devices. However, the distributed nature of FL gives rise to statistical heterogeneity, where clients observe different data distributions due to variations in operating conditions and geographical locations. Consequently, the local datasets are often class imbalanced. For example, in health-care monitoring, battery-powered smartwatches collect ECG signals from users with varying cardiac conditions, causing some disease classes to be rare or even absent on individual devices [1]. Such class imbalance degrades the minority-class performance of the resulting global model [2], [3]. A common approach to mitigate class imbalance is to augment minority-class samples before local training. However, augmentation increases the computational burden of local training by gen-erating and processing additional samples. Since many edge devices are battery-powered [4], [5], this additional computa-tion increases energy consumption and prolongs the training process. Therefore, it is important to design FL strategies that

A. Motivation 

Existing approaches to class imbalance in FL can be broadly categorized into non-augmentation-based and augmentation-based methods. Non-augmentation-based methods improve model learning without generating additional samples. These methods explicitly address class imbalance through techniques such as class reweighting and aggregation strategies [6]–[9] or tackle more general forms of statistical heterogeneity using loss function modifications and aggregation schemes [10]– [16]. While the former are primarily designed to improve minority-class performance, the latter seek to reduce the adverse effects of client drift and accelerate convergence under non-IID data distributions, thereby providing only indirect benefits to minority classes. Furthermore, since these methods do not increase the diversity of minority-class samples, their ability to improve minority-class performance is limited under severe class imbalance. Although some approaches designed for statistical heterogeneity also improve convergence speed [12], reducing the overall training time is generally not their primary design objective. Augmentation-based techniques [17]–[21] alleviate class imbalance by generating synthetic minority-class samples, thereby improving minority-class performance under severe imbalance. However, this improvement comes at the expense of additional computational overhead during both data gener-ation and local model training [22]. Therefore, these methods are less suitable where reducing training time is critical. Interestingly, [23] shows that achieving a desired global class balance is sufficient to improve the minority-class F1-score, suggesting that balancing every client’s local dataset may be unnecessary. However, [23] primarily focuses on privacy-preserving augmentation using homomorphic encryption, in-troducing substantial computational overhead. Motivated by the above discussion, we investigate aug-mentation from a different perspective. Rather than asking how much augmentation is needed to maximize minority-class performance, we ask whether augmentation can be optimally allocated across clients to jointly improve minority-class per-formance and reduce the overall training time. B. Contributions 

To address the above-mentioned challenges, we establish a relationship between data augmentation and the conver-gence behavior of FL by analyzing the mismatch between local and global class distributions across participating clients. Our analysis shows that reducing label proportion imbalance through augmentation accelerates convergence, thereby pro-viding a principled foundation for augmentation-aware feder-ated learning. Guided by the theoretical analysis, we formulate distributed augmentation as a latency-aware optimization prob-lem that determines the minimum class-specific augmentation required across participating clients to achieve a desired global class distribution while minimizing augmentation overhead. Subsequently, we present DAFL, a server-coordinated dis-tributed augmentation framework that computes client-specific augmentation assignments. By reducing both label proportion imbalance and unnecessary augmentation, DAFL accelerates convergence while improving minority-class performance. Ex-tensive experiments on the CIFAR-10 dataset under varying degrees of class imbalance demonstrate that DAFL consis-tently outperforms existing baselines by achieving higher minority-class F1-scores while requiring less augmentation and shorter training time to attain the target performance. Remark 1. Existing analyses, such as [12], [13], model data heterogeneity using a single bounded-dissimilarity constant. In contrast, we decompose heterogeneity into a label proportion imbalance term ( I) and a conditional gradient heterogeneity term ( Γ), thereby explicitly quantifying the contribution of each term to the convergence bound. Since I depends only on class proportions, it can be directly reduced by augmenting under-represented classes locally, whereas Γ cannot. 

In Theorem 1, the terms involving I and Γ characterize the impact of label proportion imbalance and conditional gradient heterogeneity, respectively, on the convergence time. Hence, we identify label proportion imbalance and conditional gradi-ent heterogeneity as two sources of client drift in FL. We note that both label proportion imbalance and conditional gradient heterogeneity depend on the local and global distributions. However, unlike label proportion imbalance, conditional gradi-ent heterogeneity is influenced by the quality of the augmented samples, which depends on factors such as the augmentation technique used. Consequently, it is challenging to bring the conditional gradient heterogeneity term as a parameter for optimization via the augmentation framework. This motivates us to focus on label proportion imbalance, which forms the basis of the optimization problem developed subsequently. 

A. Label Proportion Imbalance Measure, I

From Theorem 1, it is clear that reducing I accelerates convergence by mitigating client drift. In particular, we can force I = 0 by ensuring that every client has the global class proportion, i.e., πk = π, ∀k.

B. Proposed Algorithm for DAFL 

To realize A∗, the server requires knowledge of nk, which is distributed across the clients in the federation. We refer to the resulting collaborative process as distributed augmentation, in which the clients in a federation collaborate with the server to perform sufficient augmentation. Here, the clients first share their local class counts with the server to obtain N. Given 

N, the server then solves Problem 1 to obtain A∗. Problem 1 is a non-convex integer optimization problem. Table IV reports the number of rounds and training time required for convergence, along with minority-class F1-score, accuracy, and macro F1-score on the test dataset for NIC, WCEL, LB and DAFL. Fig. 2 further plots the minority-class F1-score, accuracy, and macro F1-score against training time for the run with random seed 42. From Table IV and Fig. 2, three key observations can be made. First, DAFL is most effective when both J and I are high. In D2, D4, D5, and D6, DAFL achieves a higher minority-class F1-score than the baselines while requiring less training time. This improvement arises because reducing J and I improves minority-class per-formance and accelerates convergence, respectively. Second, low J and high I leave little scope to improve the minority-class F1-score, hence, DAFL mainly reduces the convergence time. In contrast, for low I and high J, DAFL improves the minority-class F1-score at the cost of increased training time. This explains the performance observed in D1 and D3. In D6, since J is moderate, DAFL provides a minor improvement in the minority-class F1-score at the cost of reduced accuracy and macro F1-score compared to other baselines. This is because the other baselines improve minority-class performance less than that of DAFL, resulting in a lower minority-class F1-score but higher accuracy and macro F1-score than DAFL as plotted in Fig. 2p-2r. Third, since LB requires more augmentation than DAFL, when minority classes contain very few samples, repeated rotation and flipping generate duplicate images. This results in overfitting, leading to a poor minority-class F1-score. Consequently, DAFL outperforms LB in minority-class performance while using fewer augmented samples in five of the six datasets. The exception is D3, where excessive augmentation to reduce I causes overfitting. Finally, Table V compares DAFL with FedProx and Focal Loss on D2, the most imbalanced setting, to analyze how effectively DAFL addresses severe class imbalance.
