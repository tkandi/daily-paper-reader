# Abstract 

Sparse attention limits the keys used by each query, but finding those keys can still require a full-prefix scan at ev-ery layer. We introduce MLIPS (Multi-Layer Indexer Pool Sharing) , a training-free method that shares candidate search while retaining each consumer’s own scoring and selection. A producer publishes a top-M pool larger than the attention budget K; subsequent layers select their own top-K from that pool. Measurements across three sparse-attention model families show substantially higher cover-age from broad pools than overlap between final selections. We implement layerwise sharing, persistent decode reuse, and a coarse producer in vLLM for DeepSeek-V4-Flash. Across four long-context suites, three consumer layers per producer give 1.14 –1.40 × speedups and 14 .8–27 .4% lower GPU energy, with observed accuracy changes of −1.49 to 

+0 .29 percentage points in single runs. More extensive sharing exposes task-dependent quality losses. Sampled prefill traces also reveal recurring selected KV positions across query groups and layers. We characterize this shared working-set structure and use it to motivate Top-G, an ex-ploratory bounded pool construction for prefill. Its deploy-ment, task accuracy, and serving benefits remain open. 

# 1 Introduction 

Sparse attention makes long-context language models more efficient by restricting each query to a small set of keys. Yet selecting those keys can remain expensive. In DeepSeek Sparse Attention, a Lightning Indexer scores the visible pre-fix before choosing the top-K positions used by attention (DeepSeek-AI, 2025, 2026a). Attention then operates on a bounded selection, while the indexer’s search grows with context length. Repeating that search across sparse layers and queries leaves substantial work to optimize. Sharing selections is one established response. Index-Cache lets intervening layers inherit a retained indexer’s top-K, with training-free calibration or training-aware dis-tillation (Bai et al., 2026). DeepSeek-V4.1-Flash trains deeper indexers to re-select from a larger pool produced by an earlier layer (DeepSeek-AI, 2026b). These approaches motivate a deployment question: how much candidate search can an existing checkpoint share without retraining its indexers or changing its attention operator? Our starting observation is that agreement on broad can-didate sets is much stronger than agreement on final se-lections . On DeepSeek-V4-Flash traces spanning contexts up to one million tokens, an adjacent producer’s top-512 

contains 23 –34% of a consumer’s independent top-512 , de-pending on the suite. A 16 ,384 -entry producer pool raises that coverage to 72 –85% . The same distinction appears in MiniMax-M3 and GLM-5.1 at their native selection granu-larities (Section 3). A consumer can therefore retain many of its preferred candidates without repeating the full scan, provided that it can rank the candidates for itself. We introduce MLIPS (Multi-Layer Indexer Pool Shar-ing) . A producer runs the full indexer, selects its own top-

K, and publishes a wider top-M pool. Each consumer scores those candidates with its own indexer and selects its own top-K. Consumers preserve their attention compu-tation and the checkpoint’s organization of KV state. The restriction is in the search domain: a consumer can re-rank pooled entries, but cannot recover an omitted one. Layer-wise sharing applies during prefill and decode. Persistent decode reuse extends a pool across later generation steps; a coarse producer further reduces candidate-generation work by scoring groups of keys. We implement these paths in vLLM and evaluate DeepSeek-V4-Flash on four long-context suites. With three consumer layers per producer and no reuse across de-code steps, MLIPS gives 1.14 –1.40 × speedups and 14 .8–

27 .4% lower GPU energy. Observed accuracy changes range from −1.49 to +0 .29 points (Table 1). The traces also expose a broader opportunity: nearby queries and sparse layers repeatedly select a shared work-ing set of KV positions. We characterize this structure and suggest Top-G, a bounded pool assembled from a query group’s producer selections (Section 6). This is an empiri-cal observation and an exploratory construction; turning it into a serving mechanism remains future work. 1arXiv:2610.27438v1 [cs.LG] 20 Oct 2026 Our contributions are: • Evidence for candidate reuse. We distinguish final-selection overlap from broad-pool coverage across three model families and characterize its dependence on query position and sharing extent. • An inference-time sharing method and serving evaluation. We develop MLIPS’s producer–consumer design, implement layerwise and persistent decode reuse with a coarse producer option, and measure ac-curacy, wall time, and GPU energy. • Shared working-set structure in prefill. We mea-sure recurring selected KV positions across queries and layers and outline Top-G as a way to exploit that structure. 

# 2 Background and Related Work 

Sparse attention and candidate search. An indexer as-signs relevance scores to causally visible keys and selects the top-K indices for attention (DeepSeek-AI, 2025). The indexer scores and the attention weights are separate: atten-tion computes its own weights after selection. We denote the visible search domain by N entries. In DeepSeek-V4-Flash’s compressed sparse-attention (CSA) layers, these are compressed entries, with approximately N = L/ 4 for context length L, and K = 512 (DeepSeek-AI, 2026a). A separate local-attention window remains active. A full indexer scans N entries even though the global attention branch uses only K. This creates the opportunity to share candidate search. 

Pools, selections, and schedules. A producer publishes 

M candidate indices, where K < M ≪ N ; a consumer scores those candidates and selects its own top-K. Se-lection inheritance instead directly adopts the producer’s top-K. Pools contain indices, not shared attention scores or KV vectors. We use h for the number of consumer sparse layers served by each producer and qh for the num-ber of subsequent decode steps served before a rebuild. Exact producer–consumer distance is denoted d when dis-cussing an individual layer pair; sparse-layer distance need not equal Transformer-layer distance. Schedule averages at different h can include different producer placements and several distances. 

What coverage measures. Let CK be a consumer’s top-

K under full search and let PK and PM be the producer’s selection and wider pool. We measure

K . (1) Selection overlap RKK describes agreement between fi-nal selections. Pool coverage RKM describes the con-sumer’s preferred entries available for re-selection. Nested producer sets imply RKM ≥ RKK . Neither metric measures retained attention mass or downstream accuracy. Across model families, counts use native selection units— compressed entries, blocks, or tokens—so equal pool sizes need not have equal cost. 

Cross-layer reuse. IndexCache studies top-K inheri-tance, using calibration to choose retained indexers without weight updates or distillation to train them (Bai et al., 2026). IndexShare and LongCat Sparse Attention also share selec-tions through trained architectures (Z.ai, 2026b; Meituan LongCat Team, 2026). DeepSeek-V4.1-Flash is the clos-est pool-based design: during decode, a full indexer con-structs a wider candidate pool and deeper Reindex layers score that pool independently; the restriction is introduced during post-training (DeepSeek-AI, 2026b). MLIPS evalu-ates pool re-selection on existing checkpoints and extends reuse across successive decode steps. It preserves the host checkpoint’s attention-state organization. 

Coarse search and query sharing. HISA scores mean-pooled block representatives before fine selection (Xu et al., 2026). MLIPS’s coarse producer combines this type of filtering with pool sharing across layers. PIVOT forms a proxy from jointly available queries and runs one shared prefix scan; its Refine variant re-scores a broader candi-date set for each query (Liu et al., 2026). This establishes query-group candidate search. Our persistent decode pool instead serves queries that arrive at later steps. The ex-ploratory Top-G study examines producer-selection mem-berships across query groups and layers, rather than con-structing a proxy query. 

Routing and KV storage. Cross-Layer Attention and YOCO share KV state across layers (Brandon et al., 2024; Sun et al., 2024); You Only Index Once combines shared routing with YOCO (Sun et al., 2026). MLIPS shares can-didate indices without requiring KV sharing. Restricting the active working set may support offloading, but does not itself reduce total KV storage. We analyze that opportunity separately in Appendix F. Together, these distinctions posi-tion MLIPS as a study of training-free candidate reuse and its measured quality–efficiency tradeoffs.

However, each schedule averages over a different set of layer pairs. With one consumer per producer ( h = 1 ), for example, the third sparse layer is a producer; with two (h = 2 ), it consumes the first layer’s pool. Such changes can produce local increases in the average even as sharing grows. Pool width and sharing extent should therefore be evaluated together, checking individual layer pairs as well as the schedule average.

# 4 MLIPS 

## 4.1 Share candidates, retain independent se-lection 

A producer runs the checkpoint’s indexer over the visible domain, keeps its own top-K, and publishes the top-M

candidate indices. Each consumer gathers its own indexer keys at those positions, computes fresh scores, and selects its own top-K for attention. Only candidate addresses are shared: the consumer uses its own query, indexer keys, at-tention weights, and KV entries. Figure 2 summarizes the mechanism. The approximation is the restricted search domain. Re-scoring preserves the consumer’s ranking within the pool, but cannot recover an omitted key. For nested producer sets, 

PK ⊆ P M implies RKM ≥ RKK ; this is a coverage prop-erty, not a guarantee of equal task accuracy. Appendix A gives the formal argument and operation accounting. 

## 4.2 Layerwise and persistent decode reuse 

Layer sharing (1D MLIPS). We partition the n indexer-equipped sparse layers into groups of at most h + 1 . The first layer in each group produces a pool; the following h

consumers re-select from it. The producer count is p =

⌈n/ (h + 1) ⌉, and the consumer count is c = n − p. The final group may be shorter. Pools are built independently for each query, so layer sharing applies during both prefill and decode. Changing h changes producer placement as well as the maximum consumer distance; it is not simply a continuous increase in one fixed pair’s distance. 

Persistent decode pools (2D MLIPS). A producer can retain its pool for qh subsequent decode steps. It rebuilds once every qh + 1 steps and re-scores the stored candidates in between; consumer layers also re-score the pool at each step. Each later query therefore receives fresh scores, al-though the candidate membership is older. This persistence serves queries as they arrive, extending the reuse schedule along the query axis. A stored pool can omit older keys that become relevant and compressed keys created after the build. The local-attention window still provides access to recent tokens, but does not guarantee that restricted global selection matches full search. Our measurements use qh ≤ 3 and compres-sion ratio r = 4 . Admitting newly materialized keys and triggering adaptive refreshes are possible extensions, not evaluated corrections to these results.

C1D = pI (Q, N ) + cI (Q, M ). (3) Averaging over one decode rebuild cycle gives the scoring-work reduction

pN + [ p qh + c(qh + 1)] M . (4) For a concrete example, DeepSeek-V4-Flash has n = 21

## 4.4 Coarse candidate generation 

A coarse producer first searches groups of keys, then re-fines its selection within the retained groups. We call this variant MLIPS with a Coarse Producer (MLIPS-CP). It reduces the producer scan by grouping P consecutive compressed keys and scoring one mean representative per group. The producer keeps b groups with bP = M , pub-lishes their members as the candidate pool, and performs a fine selection for its own top-K. Consumers perform their usual re-selection within that pool. This combines coarse-to-fine indexing (Xu et al., 2026) with cross-layer candidate sharing. Its modeled reduction is 

RCP (P, h, qh ) = nN (qh + 1) 

pN/P + n(qh + 1) M . (5) The main coarse configuration uses P = 2 and b =8,192 . Pooling can filter out useful groups; consumer re-finement cannot restore them. We evaluate this additional restriction separately from exact-producer sharing. Ap-pendix A contains the parameter grids and an explanation of representative-score dilution. Appendix B describes the vLLM integration, buffers, CUDA-graph handling, and selection checks. 

# 5 Evaluation 

## 5.1 Setup 

We evaluate four long-context suites: LongBench-v2 ( 503 

items), RULER-1M ( 130 items, ten per task), MRCR-v2 8-needle ( 784 items), and GraphWalks ( 1,150 items) (Bai et al., 2025; Hsieh et al., 2024; OpenAI, 2025b,a). The serving stack uses DeepSeek-V4-Flash in vLLM on one NVIDIA GB300 node, with four GPUs at 1,400 W each and tensor parallelism 4. The pool width is M = 16 ,384 .Chunked prefill uses an 8,192 -token budget, with at most 

24 concurrent sequences and 24 requests in flight; prefix caching is off. Expert parallelism and speculative decod-ing are disabled, and CUDA graphs are enabled. Stock and MLIPS use the same checkpoint and software; con-figuration flags select the indexer path (Appendix B). Ap-pendix D.1 gives precision, software, and measurement de-tails. We report each suite’s task score on a 0–100 scale, end-to-end workload speedup relative to stock, and GPU energy saved. Energy integrates four-GPU power samples taken every 500 ms, from the end of warm-up to workload com-pletion; it excludes startup, compilation, and graph capture, as well as CPU, host-memory, and network energy. Ev-ery configuration is a single serving run. Two stock boots differed by up to 1.3% in time and 0.3% in energy, with score differences of approximately one to two points or less across suites. This limited comparison does not estab-lish accuracy equivalence or statistical significance. We re-port observed differences and retain each experiment cam-paign’s own baseline. 

## 5.2 Layer sharing: consistent serving gains 

Table 1 evaluates h = 1 , 2, 3 with no persistent decode reuse ( qh = 0 ). Every configuration is faster and uses less GPU energy than its stock run. At h = 3 , speedups range from 1.139 × on MRCR-v2 to 1.398 × on RULER-1M, with energy savings of 14 .8–27 .4% . The correspond-ing score changes, in table order, are −0.40 , −0.29 , −1.49 ,and +0 .29 points. These observations support sharing a broad candidate pool while preserving each consumer’s independent selection; the search restriction still changes some task scores. The size of the gain depends on how much work reaches the sharing path. All RULER-1M prompts exceed its roughly 65 K-token activation threshold, whereas about half of MRCR-v2 prompts do not. The speedup differences are consistent with that distinction, although the aggregate measurements do not isolate indexer time from other serv-ing costs. Measured speedups are therefore the relevant per-formance result; the operation model in Section 4 explains the opportunity rather than predicting end-to-end gains di-rectly. Quality is not monotone in h. RULER-1M loses 1.95 

points at h = 2 , but only 0.29 at h = 3 . GraphWalks also has a reported long-context BFS dip at h = 2 (Ap-pendix D.2); its cause remains unresolved. Extending shar-ing to h = 5 , 6 increases speed further, reaching 1.572 × on RULER-1M at h = 6 , but its score then falls by 4.42 points. The other three suites have smaller aggregate changes in these runs. Thus the evidence favors modest sharing as the initial operating region, while deeper schedules require workload-specific quality checks. Appendix D.2 retains all extended configurations. Energy and latency also need separate measurements. At 

h = 1 , mean GPU power falls by 2.2–5.2% , so energy savings exceed the fraction of wall time saved. At deeper schedules, mean power can rise even as the run finishes sooner. Lower indexer work consequently does not imply a fixed power reduction.

# 6 Shared Candidate Working Sets 

MLIPS shares a candidate pool across layers for each query. The traces also suggest that nearby queries repeatedly se-lect a common set of KV positions. This raises a broader question: can a bounded pool represent the working set of a group of queries across several sparse layers? Shared index metadata and coordinated KV staging are possible uses. Their bene-fits require measurement: G > M can increase consumer scoring, and index coverage alone establishes neither task accuracy nor speedup. Construction over a whole query group can also depend on later queries; a serving design must specify information availability and causal masks. The present result is evidence for exploitable structure and a proposed way to represent it. It leaves deployment and full-query validation open. 77 Limitations and Future Work 

Evidence and generalization. Serving results cover one checkpoint and hardware stack; the cross-model evidence concerns index coverage. Each serving configuration is a single run. Two stock boots provide a limited variabil-ity reference, not an equivalence test or uncertainty esti-mate for every comparison. Repeated paired evaluations and matched implementations of the closest reuse baselines are needed to establish robustness and comparative bene-fit. The operation model also excludes selection, gathering, and memory costs; phase-level profiling would clarify why scoring reductions translate unevenly into latency savings. 

Adaptive reuse. Uniform schedules expose weak layer pairs and unexplained quality changes, including the h = 2 

dip. A useful next step is to select producer locations and pool widths using calibration, then evaluate the chosen schedule on held-out workloads. Persistent decode reuse needs refresh rules that balance pool age, new-key admis-sion, and shifts in query relevance. Generated-token fi-delity and task accuracy should accompany timing results; prefill locality alone cannot settle those questions. 

From shared structure to Top-G. The prefill traces identify recurring selected positions, without establishing KV-vector similarity or a deployed compression scheme. Testing all query rows, additional prompts, and alternative constructors would show how robust the apparent work-ing sets are. A serving design must account for pool-construction cost, causal information flow, per-query mask-ing, task accuracy, and the cost of retrieving pooled entries. The current sampled recall measurements do not establish a serving speedup. 

Memory management. Candidate pools may bound a consumer’s immediate working set, but realizing lower GPU residency requires cache management and transfers. The offloading analysis in Appendix F is therefore a capac-ity opportunity rather than a measured memory reduction. Future evaluation should report peak resident memory and transfer overhead together with latency and quality. 

# 8 Conclusion 

MLIPS shares candidate search across sparse-attention lay-ers while retaining each consumer’s own scoring and se-lection. Broad producer pools cover substantially more consumer choices than final-selection overlap suggests across three model families. On DeepSeek-V4-Flash, three consumer layers per producer give 1.14 –1.40 × serving speedups and 14 .8–27 .4% lower GPU energy across four suites, with observed accuracy changes of −1.49 to +0 .29 

points in single runs. More extensive sharing exposes qual-ity limits. Recurring selected KV positions across pre-fill queries and layers further motivate Top-G, whose con-struction and deployment require continued study. # B Implementation Details 

We implement producer and consumer kernels for prefill and decode in vLLM’s DeepSeek-V4 sparse-attention path. The checkpoint weights, attention operator, scheduler, and KV-cache path are retained. Baseline and MLIPS use the same software, with configuration flags selecting the in-dexer path. 

Integration and buffers. The implementation overrides the Lightning-Indexer selection stage and a gather module on vLLM’s DeepSeek-V4 path. Producers and consumers have separate prefill and decode kernel sites. An exact pro-ducer obtains its own top-K and the wider top-M pool from one scoring pass; a consumer gathers and scores its own keys at the pooled positions. A pool row contains M

int32 indices: 64 KiB at M = 16 ,384 . Total metadata de-pends on query-row and persisted-pool counts, even though each row’s size is fixed at a given M .During prefill, producers overwrite a transient candidate buffer before their consumers use it. During decode, per-producer buffers retain pools across rebuild cycles. Rows with at most M compressed keys use stock indexing, corre-sponding to 4M = 65 ,536 tokens at r = 4 . This threshold helps explain why workloads with more short contexts ob-tain smaller gains. 

Coarse representatives. Representatives are computed in fp32 from dequantized keys and quantized into the in-dexer key format. In prefill, running sums track keys ap-pended to each group. In decode, completed representa-tives are addressed through the KV-page block table, with a running sum for an incomplete group. One Di = 128 

MXFP4 representative uses 64 value bytes and four scale bytes. There is one representative per P keys; actual allocated overhead additionally depends on page layout and temporary state. The cache can be replaced by on-the-fly pooling at the cost of rereading the original keys. Coarse production is used above 32 ,768 compressed entries (131,072 tokens); shorter rows use exact production. 11 Decode graphs and recency. The persistent path cap-tures rebuild and reuse variants separately. A field in the batch descriptor selects the replay outside the CUDA graph, avoiding a Python decision fixed at capture time. Evalu-ation disables speculative decoding. Within-step specula-tive grouping and reuse across successive steps are different schedules; their combined acceptance behavior and perfor-mance are not measured here. For longer reuse windows, one possible policy is to ad-mit newly materialized compressed keys and remove low-priority pool entries. Such a policy needs its own selection rule and validation. It does not address older keys that be-come relevant after the pool was built. 

Selection checks. The exact producer’s top-K differs from stock by at most 0.02% on the million-token prompts in the reported check, with mismatches attributed to equal scores. A probe at (h, qh ) = (1 , 1) performs a full scan on reuse steps and compares it with selection from the stored pool. Over ten RULER-1M prompts, mean overlap is 0.974 

and median overlap is 1.000 . This quantifies the probe’s selection fidelity; it is not an end-task accuracy test or a general guarantee against implementation errors.

Scope of the evidence. These are count-based index re-calls on sampled prefill activations, not attention-mass re-tention. Four-query bursts support query offsets only up to three. ## D.1 Model, software, and measurement pro-tocol 

The served checkpoint is the released DeepSeek-V4-Flash. Its routed MoE expert weights are FP4, with E8M0 scales over blocks of 32 values, executed through vLLM’s MXFP4 kernels. Attention projections, the shared expert, and the indexer query projection use FP8 E4M3 weights with 128 × 128 blocks. The MoE router, indexer compres-sor, and normalization weights are bf16. Computation runs in bf16, with activations quantized on the fly to FP8 for the FP8 GEMMs and to MXFP8 for the expert GEMMs. The KV cache is FP8 and the indexer key cache is FP4. We use vLLM 0.22.1 in the NVIDIA vLLM 26.06 con-tainer. Each run occupies four GPUs of a single NVIDIA GB300 node, with a 1,400 W power limit per GPU and tensor parallelism 4. Expert parallelism and speculative de-coding are off; CUDA graphs are on. The chunked-prefill budget is 8,192 tokens, the maximum number of concur-rent sequences is 24 , the context limit is 1,048 ,576 tokens, and prefix caching is off. The client maintains 24 requests in flight. The exact-producer pool has M = 16 ,384 en-tries. Rows below the pooling threshold use the stock path as described in Appendix B. The four suites contain 503 LongBench-v2 items, 130 

RULER-1M items (ten in each of 13 task categories), 784 

MRCR-v2 8-needle items, and 1,150 GraphWalks items. Scores are reported on a 0–100 scale, with ∆acc in percent-age points. Speedup is stock workload wall time divided by configuration wall time. nvidia-smi ; energy integrates the four-GPU total from the end of warm-up to workload completion. Boot, JIT compilation, and CUDA-graph capture are excluded. The exact sharing grid and max-representative experiment use the first stock rows shown in Tables 5 and 12. 22 E Exploratory Top-G Construction and Trace Study 

## E.1 Capture and measurement protocol 

The Top-G study uses LB2 traces captured while running MLIPS. The capture contains eight sequences and 21 com-pressed sparse-attention (CSA) layers at physical layers 

2, 4, . . . , 42 . One CSA-layer step therefore spans two phys-ical model layers. Each recorded query supplies unordered producer Top-K and Top-M index sets, with K = 512 

and nominal M = 16K . The dump lacks aligned producer-layer scores, so the constructor below uses set membership. Array position within a captured Top-M set is not treated as an importance ranking. Pass identifiers are trace prove-nance and do not affect construction. Four consecutive queries are recorded every 256 original query positions. Groups of 64, 256, and 1,024 recorded rows consequently represent nominal windows of 4,096, 16,384, and 65,536 original positions. Sequence-wide shar-ing uses all eligible recorded rows in a sequence. These labels describe sampling spans, not fully observed query groups. We cannot infer how recall changes when all inter-vening queries participate: additional queries may provide useful evidence but also compete for the same budget. All reported pools have budget G = 32 ,000 . Measure-ments require context length at least G and average recall on capacity-eligible recorded rows. The primary aggre-gate in Figure 4 covers sequences 0–6 of the eight cap-tured sequences and subsequent CSA-layer distances 1–6. Producer-layer fit, at distance zero, is excluded from reuse results. The headline results are restricted to these seven sequences. Per-sequence results below use the 11 producer layers with a complete ten-step consumer horizon. Each sequence–window–distance cell is a query-cell-weighted mean over eligible recorded rows and those producers. For a recorded query q, write Kℓq and M ℓq for producer memberships. The pool satisfies Equation 6, and evalua-tion uses Equation 7. Recall is defined only for nonempty consumer selections. The pool is frozen before examining any consumer layer; consumer Top-K labels are used only for evaluation.

## E.3 Aggregate and per-sequence evidence 

For a fixed budget of 32,000 indices, the mean recall across distances 1–6 is 77 .6% , 69 .4% , 52 .9% , and 33 .6% for nom-inal windows of 4,096, 16,384, 65,536, and the full se-quence, respectively (Figure 4). The first-to-sixth-distance decrease is 82 .4% to 73 .5% for the smallest window. At 65,536 positions, the sampling span is sixteen times larger while mean recall remains 52 .9% . These results reveal reuse across queries and layers, together with a clear ca-pacity boundary as the query scope expands. The ordering also holds separately for each primary se-quence, 0–6: the mean over ten consumer distances de-creases as the nominal window grows. For the 4,096-position window, per-sequence ten-distance mean recall ranges from 65 .3% to 84 .1% , and recall at distance ten ranges from 59 .9% to 80 .6% . The aggregate trend is con-sequently not confined to one input, although the coverage level remains workload dependent. 

## E.4 What remains to be established 

The trace study measures coverage of recorded selected in-dices. It does not evaluate every query, end-task accuracy, construction overhead, consumer selection cost, or serv-ing performance. The constructor is an exploratory probe rather than a validated runtime policy. Comparisons against producer Top-K frequency, fixed Top-K/Top-M quotas, private producer Top-M pools, and a future-selection or-acle would help isolate which parts of the construction ex-plain coverage; the present aggregate alone does not estab-lish their relative merit. Shared candidate metadata and coordinated KV staging are potential applications, but a pool of G entries may ex-ceed each query’s original Top-M budget and increase con-sumer scoring. A shared index set also does not imply that different layers share KV values. Realizing a residency benefit requires layer-specific data movement and mem-ory measurements. 25 F Candidate Working Sets and Of-floading 

Sharing candidate indices does not reduce the total KV state that an autoregressive model must preserve. A fu-ture pool may contain entries absent from the current one. Those entries must remain recoverable, for example in host memory, if they are evicted from the GPU. This section an-alyzes a possible resident working set, not an implemented or measured memory-saving mechanism. 

A simplified capacity model. Suppose each producer keeps all N compressed positions resident, and each con-sumer keeps the indexer keys and attention KV for its current M candidates resident. Under that policy, the compressed-entry working set over n layers is pN + cM ,giving the modeled ratio 

Rresident = nN pN + cM . (15) This equals the layerwise dot-product reduction because both counts assign N positions to a producer and M to a consumer. The identity is a property of this simplified pol-icy, not an equivalence between compute savings and mea-sured GPU memory. Attention itself reads only the selected top-K; alternative staging policies could use a different res-ident set. If a producer also keeps only M entries during its qh 

reuse steps, the cycle-average ratio becomes 

Ravg = nN (qh + 1) 

pN + [ p qh + c(qh + 1)] M . (16) For qh > 0, this is a time-average, not a reduction in the capacity needed for a rebuild step. The values match Ta-ble 3, so we do not repeat that table. Both models exclude weights, local windows, other attention layers, metadata, transfer buffers, and runtime allocation overhead. 

Transfers and deadlines. Consumer keys that enter a re-freshed pool must be fetched before re-scoring; the selected KV entries must arrive before attention. The pool becomes known only when the producer publishes it, leaving a short window before the first consumer. Persistent pools can lengthen this window on later reuse steps. Achieving the producer’s cycle-average working set additionally requires reloading its full search domain before the next rebuild. Pool turnover, transfer bandwidth, staging buffers, and scheduling determine whether the policy is practical. The current top-K-in-pool recall measurements do not measure pool-to-pool turnover, so they cannot quantify this transfer volume. An offloading evaluation should report actual peak GPU residency, transferred bytes, latency, GPU energy, and task quality together. 26
