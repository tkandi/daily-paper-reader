# ABSTRACT 

Federated learning for capacity-heterogeneous clients and federated continual learning are each well studied on their own, but their intersection – clients holding differently sized models while every client’s data stream also drifts over time – remains underexplored. We study this setting di-rectly, training a ResNet-18 fleet from scratch on Split-CIFAR-100 (10 tasks, class-incremental), and introduce DROD (Dual-Ratio Ordered Dropout), which builds on FjORD’s Ordered Dropout by using two independent reduction axes to extract sub-models: a shortcut ratio and a branch ratio. On a shared parameter/FLOP budget ladder, DROD achieves the highest mean accuracy among the heterogeneous-model federated learning strategies we compare. Its advantage over the two strongest baseline geometries persists across every replay-buffer size we test and under ER, DER++, and BiC; under EWC, all three geometries collapse alike. 

# 1 Introduction 

Model-heterogeneous federated learning (FL) tackles device disparity by allocating capacity-tailored submodels to participating clients, using channel-width slicing [Diao et al., 2021, Alam et al., 2022, Horvath et al., 2021], depth scal-ing [Kim et al., 2023], joint width-depth allocation [Ilhan et al., 2023], or importance-aware pruning [Wu et al., 2024]. However, these approaches are evaluated almost exclusively on stationary, single-task distributions. Conversely, feder-ated continual learning (FCL) studies non-stationary task streams, but predominantly under the assumption of identical client architectures. The intersection of these two settings – continual learning across compute-heterogeneous edge fleets – remains underexplored. Existing methods in this setting [Zhang et al., 2025, Yu et al., 2025a] avoid direct weight aggregation, relying instead on knowledge distillation into a server model with a frozen, pre-trained backbone. FedDCL [Zhang et al., 2025] does train from scratch, but only in its grayscale benchmark, where both the server and the client models are two- to four-layer CNNs over MNIST, EMNIST, and Fashion-MNIST – a considerably lighter regime than a full ResNet-18 on CIFAR-100; its own CIFAR-100 experiments freeze the server Vision Transformer and train only the classifier head. In contrast, we directly aggregate weights across heterogeneous client capacities and train the full ResNet-18 architecture entirely from scratch on Split-CIFAR-100. These model-heterogeneous FL methods differ mainly in which structural dimension a smaller client cuts and how its submodel is chosen: a fixed contiguous band of channels [Diao et al., 2021], a band rotated across channel indices between rounds so that every parameter is eventually trained by every tier [Alam et al., 2022], a joint width–depth split [Ilhan et al., 2023], whole layers rather than channels [Kim et al., 2023], or a magnitude-selected weight mask [Wu et al., 2024]. FjORD [Horvath et al., 2021] differs in kind rather than degree: instead of fixing one submodel per client, it resamples a width rate at every local step (capped by the client’s capacity), so training itself sweeps a family of nested widths and induces an importance ordering on the channels – Ordered Dropout . What all of these share is a single reduction axis and a scalar notion of “capacity”. We observe that a residual block instead exposes two independent reduction ratios: a shortcut ratio s on the block’s carried stream and a branch ratio b on its residual transform. Hence, we propose Dual-Ratio Ordered Dropout (DROD), which uses both ratios at once rather than a single width. arXiv:2610.25220v1 [cs.LG] 18 Oct 2026 DROD: Dual-Ratio Ordered Dropout 

# 2 Method: Dual-Ratio Ordered Dropout (DROD) 

Background: Ordered Dropout (OD). FjORD samples a width p, capped by each client’s capacity, at every local iteration. 1 Retaining contiguous channel prefixes makes narrower submodels nested within wider ones and trains low-index channels more frequently, inducing an importance ordering. 

Two independent ratios. DROD uses two ratios: a shortcut ratio s ∈ (0 , 1] sets the width of x, branch ratio 

b ∈ (0 , 1] sets the width of F (x)’s hidden layer 2 as a fraction of the already-s-narrowed stream, so the block’s two convolutions become W1 : sC → bsC and W2 : bsC → sC . Figure 1 shows the two ratios acting on one block, and how DROD sets them apart from width slicing. Setting b=1 recovers FjORD’s block exactly: the hidden layer is then as wide as the stream, and s alone controls the block. At s=1 the block reduces to nesting the hidden width alone, a construction that MatFormer [Devvrit et al., 2024] applies to the transformer feed-forward block. Every weight in the block is sized by (s, b ) alone. A k × k convolution with Cin input and Cout output channels has 

k2Cin Cout parameters, so summing W1 (sC → bsC ) and W2 (bsC → sC ) gives 

params(block) = 2 k2 b s 2 C2. (1) At fixed spatial resolution, FLOPs scale with the same bs 2 factor. The shortcut ratio s affects the channel dimensions of both sides of each convolution and therefore enters quadratically, whereas the branch ratio b controls only the interme-diate width and therefore enters linearly. FjORD reports the same behavior for its single rate – FLOPs and parameters there fall as 1/p 2 at width p – DROD simply separates the two powers of that exponent into two independently settable ratios instead of coupling them into one. 

The precursor: shortcut-tiering. As b → 0, the residual branch F (x) vanishes, leaving the block as an exact identity. 3 Before deriving DROD, we tested this limiting case on its own as shortcut-tiering where blocks are reduced to exact identities (deepest-first within each stage) while every tier retains the full shortcut pathway and shares a single classifier head. Shortcut-tiering outperformed plain (FedAvg) width slicing at both buffer totals where we ran that comparison ( 200 and 12 ,800 ). Its limitation, however, is that it can only remove entire residual branches, placing a hard ceiling on the amount of model reduction it can provide. 4.

The interior: DROD. Width slicing ( b=1 ) and the shortcut-tiering limit ( b→0) bound the broader two-ratio design space. DROD explores it by varying b ∈ (0 , 1] together with s ∈ (0 , 1] . By Equation (1), params(block) is propor-tional to bs 2, so fixing the block’s budget at a fraction ρ of the full block’s parameters constrains (s, b ) to the curve 

bs 2 = ρ, not a single point (see Appendix Figure 3). We parameterize that curve by θ ∈ [0 , 1] :

s = ρ(1 −θ)/2, b = ρθ .θ=0 is width slicing; θ=1 is the other endpoint of this particular nonzero-budget curve, a full-width shortcut with branch ratio b=ρ. It does not reach shortcut-tiering, which lies at b=0 and is recovered only as a limiting case when the branch budget vanishes. At our mid tier ( ρ=0 .2514 ), θ=0 .5 lands at s=0 .70 , b=0 .49 (Figure 1(c)). 5

Why θ is a good axis to sweep. Varying θ changes how a fixed budget is divided between the shortcut and residual branch. Across the sweep, parameters and inference FLOPs remain matched within 0.5% , so the same configurations satisfy both budgets. Moreover, GroupNorm keeps the branch-output magnitude nearly constant as b shrinks: a 16 ×

reduction in branch width changes ∥F (x)∥ by only 0.05% . Thus, θ primarily controls the branch’s rank rather than its magnitude.

Relation to prior work. Like FjORD and other nested-network methods, DROD trains submodels of different sizes within a shared model. Its closest structural analogue is MatFormer, which varies a Transformer’s feed-forward hidden width while keeping its input and output dimensions fixed. DROD brings the same separation to residual networks, allowing clients to reduce the residual branch without narrowing the shortcut by the same amount. 

# 3 Experimental setup 

Task stream. We use Split-CIFAR-100: the 100 classes are cut, in a fixed order shared across all seeds, into T =10 

disjoint tasks of 10 classes, reaching every client simultaneously at synchronous boundaries. Training is fully class-incremental, using a single shared 100-way head with unmasked cross-entropy and no task identifier during either training or testing. Each task contributes roughly 5,000 training images and is trained for 25 communication rounds of 4 local epochs, so a run spans 250 rounds. 

Federation and fleet. Ten clients participate in every round. Within each task’s ten classes, we split samples across clients with a Dirichlet( α=0 .5) label skew, redrawn fresh for every task rather than fixed once for the whole run, so a client over-represented in one class this task need not stay that way next task. Each client holds on the order of 500 images of the current task. Capacity tiers are assigned round-robin across clients (4/3/3 per tier) once at the start of each run and remain fixed throughout, reflecting persistent device-level hardware heterogeneity. The server aggregates client updates coordinate-by-coordinate, weighted by sample count; a coordinate that only the wider tiers instantiate is averaged over just those clients that hold it. 

Model and shared budget. The backbone is a ResNet-18 with a CIFAR stem, trained from scratch, using Group-Norm in place of BatchNorm throughout. We adopt GroupNorm [Wu and He, 2018] following Hsieh et al. [2020], who isolate normalization as a primary cause of the accuracy that decentralized learning loses under skewed label partitions 6. At full capacity the model holds 11 .22 M parameters and costs 1.11 GFLOPs per inference. Every method (variant) we compare places its three tiers on one shared budget ladder: 1.000 /0.251 /0.064 ratios of the full ResNet18 model’s parameters or FLOPs. 7

# 5 Conclusion 

We studied federated continual learning when clients differ not only in their data streams but also in the model capacity they can support. DROD extends Ordered Dropout by separating the shortcut width from the residual-branch width and sampling nested submodels along both dimensions. On our shared budget ladder, DROD achieves the highest mean among the compared heterogeneous FL strategies, benefits every capacity tier, and remains strongest across the tested replay budgets and continual-learning objectives, except in the EWC regime where all geometries collapse. These findings show that how a residual network spends a fixed capacity budget matters: maintaining the carried representation while compressing the residual transform is a promising design principle for heterogeneous federated learning under drift.

# A Discussion 

Our results challenge the usual identification of a client’s capacity with a single width multiplier. A residual block contains two qualitatively different resources: the shortcut carries the representation forward, while the residual branch transforms it. Narrowing both by the same factor saves compute, but also constricts the representation available to every subsequent block. DROD can instead preserve a wider carried representation and spend the remaining budget on a lower-rank transform. The fact that an interior split ( θ=0 .50 ), rather than either endpoint, has the highest mean suggests that neither resource should be preserved at the complete expense of the other. A full-width shortcut with too narrow a branch limits how much new structure each block can learn, whereas ordinary width slicing gives the branch more hidden channels only by narrowing the stream on which it operates. Thus, the relevant design question is not simply how large a submodel is, but where in a residual block its limited capacity is placed. This allocation also changes which parameters receive useful federated updates. Under prefix-sliced aggregation, low-index coordinates are shared by more client tiers and are consequently trained more often. With one width ratio, reducing a tier simultaneously removes coordinates from the carried stream and from the branch. DROD separates these effects: smaller clients can continue to update a comparatively broad shared stream while contributing a narrower residual transform. This provides a plausible explanation for why the benefit is not confined to the small models. All three tiers improve, including the full-capacity tier whose own inference architecture is not compressed, indicating that the geometry changes the quality of the shared training signal rather than merely producing a better isolated small model. The particularly weak performance of fixed width slices and FedRolex, relative to the Ordered-Dropout variants, further suggests that allocating coordinates well and exposing nested submodels repeatedly during local training are complementary rather than interchangeable choices. The continual-learning experiments help delimit this interpretation. DROD has the highest mean with ER, DER++, and BiC, and it remains ahead as the replay buffer changes, so its advantage is unlikely to be an artifact of one replay loss or one memory size. At the same time, the absolute accuracy of every geometry depends strongly on the continual-learning mechanism: under EWC all three collapse to about 0.06 class-IL accuracy, and the differences between them are negligible. Reduction geometry therefore cannot compensate for an objective that fails to retain old classes in this single-head regime. DROD should be understood as complementary to continual learning: it determines how heterogeneous clients represent and aggregate what they learn, while the local objective still determines whether past knowledge survives at all. There is also a systems tradeoff behind the accuracy gain. Matching parameters and inference FLOPs does not make two submodels equally deployable. At a fixed budget, DROD’s wider shortcut produces wider activations than ordinary width slicing, increasing peak activation memory; Ordered Dropout also makes realized training cost depend on the submodels sampled during local optimization. Consequently, θ is best viewed as a hardware-aware allocation knob, not as a universally optimal constant. Devices constrained mainly by model storage, communication, or inference compute may benefit from moving capacity toward the shortcut, whereas devices constrained by working memory may prefer a smaller θ. Measuring this Pareto frontier on physical devices would be more informative than comparing parameter counts alone. More broadly, the study points to residual structure as an underused source of flexibility in heterogeneous learning. Existing schemes largely choose among width, depth, or unstructured masks; the DROD result shows that even within a single block, input/output width and transformation rank need not be coupled. The same principle may extend to bottleneck residual networks and transformer feed-forward blocks, although the best allocation is likely to depend on the architecture, normalization, and task stream. The present evidence supports the narrower claim that, for the tested ResNet-18 FCL setting, jointly ordering the shortcut and branch is a more effective use of a matched budget than ordering either structural axis alone. 

# B Limitations 

Our evidence is limited to Split-CIFAR-100, one ResNet-18 backbone, ten clients, synchronous full participation, and three seeds. The comparison therefore does not establish that the same ordering holds for larger datasets, non-residual architectures, partial participation, asynchronous training, or more severe system and data heterogeneity. Although parameters and inference FLOPs are matched, peak activation memory is not; retaining a wider shortcut can require more activation memory than width slicing at the same nominal budget. Normalization is a scope choice we did not ablate. Every arm uses GroupNorm, adopted on Hsieh et al.’s evidence rather than on a GroupNorm-vs-BatchNorm comparison in our own setting, and they themselves note that more study is needed before GroupNorm can be assumed to replace BatchNorm across models and applications. Extending the comparison to BatchNorm is the natural next step. Most of the grid also uses experience replay with uniformly allocated local buffers. Finally, DROD’s lead over 9DROD: Dual-Ratio Ordered Dropout its closest rival, Width × FjORD, depends on the buffer: it holds on all three seeds at our main buffer of 6,400 but narrows to 0.021 and two of three seeds at 12,800. Larger-scale experiments, additional seeds, and measurements on physical heterogeneous devices are needed to determine the robustness and practical cost of the observed gains. 

# C Related work 

Model-heterogeneous federated learning. Partial-training methods let clients of unequal capacity train subsets of a single global model and average each parameter over exactly those clients whose submodel contained it. Federated Dropout [Caldas et al., 2018] introduced the idea with randomly selected subnetworks; subsequent work makes the selection structured, so that a smaller client’s model nests inside a larger one. HeteroFL [Diao et al., 2021] assigns each client a fixed contiguous band of channels in every layer. FedRolex [Alam et al., 2022] rotates that band across channel indices between rounds, so that every parameter is eventually trained by every tier and no region of the global model is updated only by the largest clients. FjORD [Horvath et al., 2021] instead resamples a width rate at each local iteration, capped by the client’s device ceiling: low-index channels are exercised by every draw and high-index channels only by the widest, which induces an importance ordering on channels — Ordered Dropout — and wider submodels are distilled into narrower ones. Slimmable and universally slimmable networks [Yu et al., 2019, Yu and Huang, 2019] develop the same width-switchable training centrally, and Split-Mix [Hong et al., 2022] trains a set of small base subnetworks that are recombined on demand at inference time. Depth is the natural alternative axis. DepthFL [Kim et al., 2023] prunes the deepest layers, attaches a classifier at each retained depth, and mutually distils among them, reporting that depth-scaled local models build a better global model than width-scaled ones on stationary CIFAR-100. FeDepth [Zhang et al., 2023] decomposes the network into blocks trained sequentially to fit a client’s memory budget, targeting activation memory rather than the aggregation rule. ScaleFL [Ilhan et al., 2023] divides the budget along width and depth simultaneously, holding the two ratios equal, and ties early exits to the final classifier through self-distillation. InclusiveFL [Liu et al., 2022] allocates transformers of differing depth and transfers knowledge from powerful to weak clients by momentum distillation. FIARSE [Wu et al., 2024] abandons fixed structural slices altogether, selecting each client’s submodel by parameter magnitude and updating that choice as training proceeds; because the resulting mask is unstructured, it reduces parameter and communication cost but not computation, since a dense kernel does identical work on a masked tensor. Throughout this literature, evaluation is at a single stationary data distribution. 

Federated continual learning. Work on clients whose data arrive as a non-stationary stream has largely assumed one common architecture. FedWeIT [Yoon et al., 2021] decomposes each client’s parameters into a dense shared base and sparse task-adaptive components, so that knowledge transfers selectively between clients rather than through a single average. FedTA [Yu et al., 2025b] mixes trainable tail anchors with the frozen features of a pretrained backbone to counter forgetting both between clients and between tasks, and DOLFIN [Moussadek et al., 2025] equips a shared vision transformer with low-rank adapters constrained by dual gradient projection memory. STAMP [Nguyen et al., 2025], notwithstanding “heterogeneous” in its title, addresses task and data heterogeneity: clients draw unrelated or even conflicting tasks from a large pool, while every client trains the same network. In all of these the capacity available to a client is uniform, and heterogeneity resides in the data. 

Heterogeneous architectures under drift. Few works vary architecture and data stream together, and those that do share a design. FedDCL [Zhang et al., 2025] trains ResNet-18/32/50 clients that never exchange weights, send-ing synthetic prototypes drawn from a frozen pretrained diffusion model to the server instead; on its CIFAR-100 benchmark — 30 classes over three tasks, five clients — the server is a pretrained vision transformer of which only the classifier head is fine-tuned, and its from-scratch configuration is confined to shallow convolutional networks on grayscale MNIST-family data. Fed-LSCL [Yu et al., 2025a] pairs five different CNN families with one shared frozen transformer through a per-client linear generator that emits a LoRA adapter, fused on the server by distillation over buffered features, across five tasks of eight classes and likewise five clients. Both freeze a shared pretrained model and exchange only distilled or generated signal, which removes the aggregation-shape problem rather than solving it and leaves the representation-bearing model untrained under federation. Both are also evaluated on short streams covering a fraction of the label space. SpaPFCIL [Liu and Huang, 2025] is closer in spirit, learning a personalized sparse class-incremental model per client, but every client shares one backbone and one global sparsity target and differs only in which weights its mask selects, so capacity is uniform by construction. We instead train a ResNet-18 fleet from scratch, with clients of genuinely different capacity contributing into a single shared weight space, over ten tasks spanning all one hundred classes, with ten clients and no pretrained model anywhere in the system. 

Continual-learning objectives. We hold the federated geometry fixed and vary the objective clients optimise locally, drawing on the standard families. Regularisation methods constrain movement in parameter space: EWC [Kirkpatrick 10 DROD: Dual-Ratio Ordered Dropout et al., 2017] penalises deviation from the previous task’s solution in proportion to the diagonal Fisher information, and LwF [Li and Hoiem, 2016] distils the previous model’s outputs on new-task data without storing any examples. Rehearsal methods keep a bounded exemplar memory: experience replay [Chaudhry et al., 2019] interleaves sampled exemplars with the incoming batch, and iCaRL [Rebuffi et al., 2017] combines herded exemplars with a nearest-mean classifier. DER++ [Buzzega et al., 2020] strengthens replay by also matching the logits recorded when an exemplar was stored, and BiC [Wu et al., 2019] targets the classifier’s bias toward recently seen classes directly, correcting it with a two-parameter linear rescaling fitted on a balanced held-out split. We report the class-incremental scenario throughout [van de Ven and Tolias, 2019], in which no task identifier is available at test time. GDumb [Prabhu et al., 2020] is a standing caution for this family: a learner that merely stores a class-balanced memory and retrains from scratch at test time is competitive with much of it, so the replay budget must be held fixed across compared systems, as it is here. 

Ordered representations and residual scaling. DROD composes two lines of work that have not previously been brought together under federated aggregation with drift. The first is ordered, nested subnetwork sampling. Nested dropout [Rippel et al., 2014] stochastically removes coherent nested sets of units, which orders the representation by importance and, for a semi-linear autoencoder, recovers PCA exactly; Matryoshka representation learning [Kusupati et al., 2022] and MatFormer [Devvrit et al., 2024] carry the same coarse-to-fine nesting into embeddings and into transformer feed-forward blocks, and FjORD’s Ordered Dropout is its federated instance. The second is the treatment of a residual block as an interpolation between the identity and a full transform. Fixup, SkipInit, ReZero and Lay-erScale [Zhang et al., 2019, De and Smith, 2020, Bachlechner et al., 2021, Touvron et al., 2021] each bias a block toward the identity by attenuating its residual branch with a small or zero-initialised scale, while stochastic depth and LayerDrop [Huang et al., 2016, Fan et al., 2020] drop whole blocks at random, consistent with the view of a residual network as an ensemble of paths of varying length [Veit et al., 2016]. DROD performs this interpolation structurally rather than multiplicatively: the branch ratio narrows the residual branch’s hidden width instead of scaling its output, so approaching the identity releases parameters and FLOPs rather than leaving them in place, and the shortcut ratio independently sets the width of the carried stream. 11 DROD: Dual-Ratio Ordered Dropout

Choice of buffer totals. The totals are of the form 200 · 2k. 200 is the smallest standard buffer of DER++ [Buzzega et al., 2020], and 6,400 is the total closest to its largest, 5,120; 3,200 and 12,800 bracket it by a factor of two. DER++’s buffers are centralized. Federated class-incremental replay methods give each client more on CIFAR-100: 1,000 exemplars in Re-Fed [Li et al., 2024] and 2,000 in GLFC [Dong et al., 2022], against our 640 (6,400 over ten clients). 

Why GroupNorm. BatchNorm normalizes by minibatch statistics, so its estimates diverge across clients holding different label distributions, and averaging the resulting parameters is not a meaningful operation. GroupNorm nor-malizes per sample and is therefore independent of a client’s label distribution. The trade is asymmetric in our favor: on CIFAR-10 under FedAvg, replacing BatchNorm with GroupNorm costs 0.6 points when clients are IID (80 .3% → 79 .7% ) and recovers 19 .8 points when they are not ( 50 .2% → 70 .0% ), and the same work shows the effect does not require extreme skew — a partially non-IID partition already costs 1.5–3.0 points. (Their numbers come from a BN-LeNet with a tuned group size of 2; we fix the group count at 8 and did not tune it. Where a layer’s channel count is not divisible by 8, as in some DROD tiers, the count is halved until it divides.) Our clients hold a Dirichlet( α=0 .5)label skew redrawn every task, so we take the IID-side cost and keep the non-IID robustness. This is a scope choice, not a validated ablation (Section B). 

What changes across seeds. The three seeds (0, 1, 2) differ only in the draws that are a function of the run seed; every other row of Table 3 is identical across seeds. Concretely, a different seed redraws: • the per-task client partition – the Dirichlet split ( α=0 .5) is redrawn independently for every task, so both which samples and how many samples each client holds change with the seed, task by task; 12 DROD: Dual-Ratio Ordered Dropout • local training order – minibatch order and augmentation for every (task, round, client) triple; • Ordered-Dropout width sampling , for the FjORD-style arms – which sub-model width each client draws at each local step; • reservoir buffer contents – which exemplars survive eviction once a client’s buffer is full; • the initial weights , via one global seed drawn at the start of the run. Class order is not on this list: every run in this paper sets shuffle_classes=false (Table 3), so all three seeds see the identical class-to-task assignment; the Dirichlet redraw above changes each client’s per-task sample composition, not the task boundaries themselves. The learning rate, architecture, budget ladder, buffer totals, and tier assignment are likewise fixed hyperparameters, not seed draws. 

What the budget match does and does not fix. The shared ladder fixes parameters and inference FLOPs , and those two only. Three further cost axes move freely. • Peak activation memory is not matched, by construction. On the DROD axis the peak intermediate tensor is the stem output, which the branch ratio never touches, so activation memory is governed by the shortcut ratio alone. An interior θ therefore exceeds a width-sliced tier of the same parameter budget on activation memory, up to the full-width value at θ=1 . This is a genuine cost of the method, not a measurement artifact as DROD buys its accuracy at equal parameters and equal inference FLOPs and pays for it in activation footprint. • Training FLOPs are not matched either, and this is inherited from Ordered Dropout rather than intro-duced by DROD. Sampling a sub-model per iteration means the realized per-iteration training cost of every Ordered-Dropout arm sits above its own parameter/inference-FLOP label; we measure it separately rather than folding it into the match, and it is common to DROD and to the FjORD baselines it is compared against. • Replay enters the local batch by concatenation. For every arm trained under Ordered Dropout, buffer samples are appended to the batch before the forward pass rather than contributing a separately weighted loss term. Once the buffer is populated this means such a step performs a forward/backward pass over up to twice as many samples as a replay-free step. Step counts are matched across arms; per-step compute is not. 

A consequence of splitting the buffer across clients. At the 200-exemplar total, each client holds 20 exemplars while the replay batch is 32, so every local step replays that client’s entire buffer rather than a random draw from it. Splitting one fleet-wide exemplar budget across ten clients necessarily lowers per-step replay diversity relative to a centralized buffer of the same total size, and no setting of the replay batch recovers it without un-matching the budget. We disclose this rather than compensating for it, since it is a property of federated replay at a matched budget and applies identically to every arm compared here. 13 DROD: Dual-Ratio Ordered Dropout
