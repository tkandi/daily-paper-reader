## ABSTRACT 

Adam is widely used for training large language models (LLMs) owing to its strong optimization performance and training stability, but its element-wise es-timation for the first and second moments incur substantial memory overhead. Although numerous memory-efficient optimizers have been proposed, many in-cur additional computational overhead or require additional tuning of hyperpa-rameters. Consequently, Adam remains a dominant and reliable choice for LLM pre-training. We propose AdamPM (Adam with Pooled Moments), a simple and memory-efficient variant of Adam that groups adjacent moment entries along the row or column dimension and represents each group by a single shared mean. AdamPM can well inherit Adam’s hyperparameter settings and is readily inte-grated into existing Adam optimizer implementations, with less memory over-head. We systematically investigate pooling of both first and second moment esti-mates and identify second moment pooling as the effective design. With a group size of s, AdamPM reduces second moment memory to 1/s of the original require-ment. Extensive experiments on language model pretraining, fine-tuning and mul-timodal learning demonstrate that AdamPM maintains model performance and training speed while substantially reducing optimizer-state memory. Our code is available at https://github.com/jixiaowei95/AdamPM .

## 1 INTRODUCTION 

Adam (Kingma & Ba, 2015) and its decoupled weight-decay variant AdamW (Loshchilov & Hutter, 2019), which combine momentum with adaptive learning rates, have become the de facto standard for LLM training due to their strong convergence and stability. However, these advantages come at a substantial memory cost: Adam maintains first and second moment estimates for each parameter, requiring storage for two additional optimizer states per parameter. As LLMs scale to trillions of parameters, this overhead becomes increasingly prohibitive. Against this backdrop, memory-efficient optimizers have emerged as an important research direction, aiming to reduce training memory consumption through optimizer design while preserving the performance of conventional adaptive methods. Representative approaches achieve this goal by projecting gradi-ents into low-rank subspaces (Zhu et al., 2025; Robert et al., 2025), factorizing the second moment state (Luo et al., 2023), sharing the moment statistics (Zhang et al., 2025; Wen et al., 2026b), and employing orthogonalization (Liu et al., 2025) or sign-based update rules (Chen et al., 2023) to eliminate the second moment state. However, a recent survey from Stanford University (Wen et al., 2026a) notes that, despite numerous optimizers claiming faster training or lower memory consumption, Adam remains the robust default for LLM pre-training, with its primary limitation being the memory overhead of optimizer states. This raises a natural question: 

Before an optimizer consistently surpasses Adam in both performance and robustness, would a memory-efficient variant with only minimal modifications to Adam be a better practical choice?

Our main contributions are summarized as follows: • We propose AdamPM, a memory-efficient optimizer for LLM training that reduces optimizer-state memory without sacrificing model performance or increasing training time. We find that the first moment contains substantially more information and is highly sensitive to pooling, explaining why most memory-efficient optimizers primarily compress the second moment. We introduce cosine similarity and relative error to quantify devia-tions from vanilla Adam, and show that AdamPM’s preconditioners and parameter updates become more similar to those of Adam as model scale grows, suggesting that AdamPM offers greater memory-efficiency benefits over Adam for larger models. 

## 2 RELATED WORK 

As LLMs scale to trillions of parameters, memory-efficient training has become a research priority. Existing memory-efficient optimizers reduce memory overhead primarily through matrix factoriza-tion, low-rank projection, constraint-based transformations, and parameter sharing. 

Matrix factorization. These methods address reduction of memory by storing the factorized matri-ces, and the main differences involve to the optimization targets. For example, Adafactor (Shazeer & Stern, 2018) and SMMF (Park & Lee, 2025) factorize optimizer states, the LoRA (Hu et al., 2022) family factorizes weight updates, and LORO (Mo et al., 2025) factorizes weight matrices. Al-though these methods improve memory efficiency, some are unsuitable for pre-training and limited to fine-tuning (e.g., LoRA), or have not yet been validated as extensively as Adam on LLMs with bil-lions of parameters for pre-training (e.g., Adafactor and SMMF). Our AdamPM, like Adam, works well for both pre-training and fine-tuning, and is validated by experiments on large-scale LLMs and vision-language models. 

Low-rank projection. These methods aim at reducing memory by maintaining optimizer states in lower-dimensional spaces. They achieve this goal by random projections in APOLLO (Zhu et al., 2025), or by gradient projection based on either SVD in GaLore (Zhao et al., 2024) and Fira (Chen et al., 2025), or online PCA (Liang et al., 2024). Although these methods reduce memory con-sumption, they also incur additional computational overhead, such as periodic SVD computations in GaLore (Zhao et al., 2024), which increase training time. Besides, they often introduce new hyper-parameters that are difficult to tune, such as the frequency of periodic subspace updates in Fira (Chen et al., 2025). Our proposed AdamPM makes only minimal changes to Adam and preserves its desir-able optimization properties, while its parameter updates become increasingly similar to Adam’s as model size grows, as demonstrated in Figure 4 (d). 

Constraint-based transformations. These methods aim to eliminate the need to store certain op-timizer states by introducing constraint-based transformations, e.g., imposing orthogonality con-straints on optimizer states in Muon (Liu et al., 2025) and SUMO (Refael et al., 2025), normalizing gradients along the output feature dimension in SCALE (Glentis et al., 2025), and using sign-based momentum updates in Lion (Chen et al., 2023) and OLion (Wang et al., 2026). These optimizers substantially modify Adam, preventing direct reuse of its hyperparameters and requiring additional tuning. AdamPM makes only minimal changes to Adam by using moment pooling, and can directly inherit Adam’s hyperparameters, including the learning rate and decay rates. 

Parameter sharing. Adam-mini (Zhang et al., 2025) and FOAM (Wen et al., 2026b) also reduce optimizer-state memory by sharing moment estimates. However, compared with Adam-mini (Zhang et al., 2025), AdamPM offers a more general grouping scheme, pooling adjacent second moment entries along rows or columns with a configurable group size s. Moreover, AdamPM is model-agnostic, whereas Adam-mini relies on model-specific information such as the hidden dimension and attention-head count. Adam-mini (Zhang et al., 2025) also supports this view by sharing a single second moment statistic within each parameter block while retaining competitive performance. To assess local similarity during LLaMA 130M pretraining, we compute the coefficient of variation (CV) for the first moment, second moment, and diagonal preconditioner. For entries X , CV is de-fined as CV( X ) = σX /|μX |, where μX and σX are their mean and standard deviation, respectively. Figure 1 (b) compares the CVs of the first and second moments for value and the MLP gate projection matrix gate in the fifth Transformer block, while Figure 1 (c) reports the preconditioner CVs for gate . We observe that the first moment exhibits substantially higher CVs because it contains both positive and negative values and carries richer directional information, whereas the second moment has much lower CVs. Moreover, parameter updates depend directly on the diagonal preconditioner 1√bVt+ϵ , whose inverse-square-root transformation further reduces this variation. The preconditioner exhibits consistently low CVs throughout training, indicating strong local similarity among neighboring elements, with even lower variation in smaller group sizes. 3.3 ADAM PM O PTIMIZER 

These observations motivate AdamPM, which reduces optimizer-state memory by applying group-wise moment pooling to Adam’s second moment. AdamPM pools every s adjacent second moment entries within rows or columns, where s denotes the group size. By default, AdamPM performs pooling within each row, with rows corresponding to output features. In particular, given a weight matrix W ∈ Rm×n, each row is partitioned into q = n/s groups if n is divisible by s. When n is not divisible by s, the final group of squared gradients is padded with its mean.

4The complete procedure is presented in Algorithm 1. AdamPM performs moment pooling as fol-lows. It first computes the element-wise squared gradient and reshapes it into m × q groups of s

adjacent elements. Then AdamPM computes the group means St and applies an EMA to obtain the compressed second moment eVt. After bias correction, each group statistic is temporarily broadcast to its corresponding s elements before the parameter update. AdamPM pools second moments for all weight matrices, including embed , lm head , attention, and MLP weights. One-dimensional parameters, such as RMSNorm weights, are pooled as single-row or single-column matrices. Note that, unlike FOAM (Wen et al., 2026b), AdamPM does not pool the first moment and follows Adam’s element-wise update, since pooling the first moment naively leads to substantial performance degradation (Section 4.2). The order of squared-gradient computation, pooling, and EMA accumulation is crucial for memory efficiency, as it is the EMA that incurs persistent optimizer-state storage overhead. By averaging squared gradients within each group before applying the EMA, AdamPM maintains only the com-pressed second-moment state eVt ∈ Rm×q throughout training, reducing second-moment memory usage to 1/s of that required by Adam. It therefore stores mn first moment and mn/s second moment elements, totaling mn (1 + 1 /s ) optimizer-state elements, compared with 2mn for Adam. 3.4 CONVERGENCE ANALYSIS

Experimental setup. We evaluate AdamPM by fine-tuning a pretrained RoBERTa-base (Liu et al., 2019) model on the GLUE benchmark (Wang et al., 2019), using full fine-tuning with Adam and Adam-mini as baselines. For each task, we select Adam’s learning rate by grid search and reuse it for AdamPM and Adam-mini without further search. We vary the group size s ∈ { 2, 4, 8, 16 }. All methods adopt the same hyperparameter strategy to ensure fair comparisons. Detailed experimental settings are provided in Appendix C.2. 

Result analysis. As shown in Table 3, AdamPM maintains average scores close to full fine-tuning with Adam across all evaluated group sizes, while memory usage decreases as group size s increases. At s = 8 , its average score even exceeds the baseline, indicating that more extensive second-moment sharing can offer a favorable memory–performance trade-off. ## 5 CONCLUSION 

We introduced AdamPM, a simple memory-efficient Adam variant that only pools second moment estimates. AdamPM reuses Adam’s hyperparameters, remains model-agnostic, and incurs negligible training overhead. Our analysis shows that its preconditioners and parameter updates remain highly similar to Adam’s, particularly at larger model scales. Experiments on LLM pre-training, fine-tuning, and multimodal models demonstrate substantial memory savings while preserving Adam-level performance and training efficiency. These results establish second moment pooling used in AdamPM as a simple and practical approach to memory-efficient LLM training. 

Limitations. Due to computational constraints, we have not conducted full training runs on mod-els with 7B or more parameters. Our study considers pooling only along a single direction, either row-wise or column-wise. Pooling over two-dimensional groups and selective pooling that retains elementwise second moments in certain layers or modules, along with their effects on optimization, remain unexplored. 9AI USE STATEMENT

Training Efficiency. The implementation of AdamPM performs group-wise second moment pool-ing entirely through matrix-level tensor operations, including reshaping, averaging, and broadcast-ing, enabling efficient vectorized computation without element-wise Python for loops. Its compu-tational cost is therefore nearly identical to that of vanilla Adam. In the pre-training of LLaMA 1.3B 18 with a batch size of 32 and 4 × NVIDIA A100 40GB GPUs, vanilla Adam and AdamPM require 116.2 and 116.4 hours, respectively, confirming that AdamPM introduces negligible training-time overhead. Moreover, the reduced memory footprint may allow a larger batch size under the same hardware constraints, improving hardware utilization and further accelerating training.
