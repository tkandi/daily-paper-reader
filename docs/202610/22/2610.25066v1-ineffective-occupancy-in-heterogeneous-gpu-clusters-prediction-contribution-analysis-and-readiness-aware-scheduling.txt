Abstract 

In heterogeneous GPU clusters, GPUs are costly and scarce, yet placing a job on a machine does not mean that the job becomes ready to run. A large amount of allocated capacity can remain occu-pied for long periods without producing useful computation, further aggravating the waste of compute resources. On the Alibaba GPU cluster trace we find that, even when GPU capacity is sufficient and placement is complete, 52.70% of execution records never became ready. We term this phenomenon ineffective occupancy. Building on this diagnosis, and keeping the placement policy fixed, we pro-pose Readiness-Aware Scheduling (RAS): it uses only nine attributes observable at admission time to predict readiness probability and reorders admission accordingly. Under a five-fold split grouped by machine, the gradient-boosted model reaches an ROC-AUC of 0.8187. A feature-group contribution analysis shows that the model’s score is driven mainly by job profile, duration, and GPU model, with the request-size group contributing least, followed by the scheduling and concurrency group. In GPU terms, RAS reduces the ineffective-occupancy ratio (the share of cluster GPUs held under ineffective occupancy) from 43.64% to 27.15% and raises the effective coverage of large requests (eight GPUs or more) from 46.11% to 63.18%, without sacrificing capacity utilization. Relative to the upper bound obtained by ordering with ground-truth labels, RAS captures 65.1% of the attainable improvement, well above the interpretable rules built only from the contribution-analysis conclusions; the gain there-fore comes mainly from predicting with all nine attributes jointly rather than from combining rules built on any single attribute group. These results indicate that readiness status is a measurable, pre-dictable, and optimizable dimension of admission decisions in heterogeneous GPU clusters. 

Keywords: heterogeneous GPU clusters; ineffective occupancy; readiness prediction; admission order-ing; interpretable machine learning; scheduling optimization 

## 1 Introduction 

Heterogeneous GPU clusters have become the mainstream infrastructure for large-scale AI training and in-ference. Unlike homogeneous clusters, heterogeneous clusters deploy multiple GPU models at the same time, and jobs (such as large language model training and online inference services) differ in their re-quirements for GPU model, GPUs per node, and network topology. This difference changes the basic resource-allocation problem: a homogeneous cluster asks whether any GPU is still available, whereas a heterogeneous cluster must answer whether the available GPUs can satisfy a specific job’s requirements— that is, the match between job demand and GPU supply [1, 2].

1arXiv:2610.25066v1 [cs.DC] 18 Oct 2026 In practice, however, this match works poorly. A Cast AI survey of more than 23,000 Kubernetes clusters reports an average GPU utilization of only 5% in enterprise environments [3]; an ACM Comput-ing Surveys review likewise reports that GPU utilization in production clusters at Alibaba, SenseTime, Microsoft, and other organizations typically ranges from 25% to 50% [4]. The problem is not limited to a mismatch between supply and demand. Even when GPUs have been allocated, a substantial fraction of jobs never become ready. The Cast AI report lists “allocated but per-sistently near-zero utilization” as one of the main types of GPU waste [3]; Alibaba’s production traces record the same phenomenon [1]. Taken together, these two lines of evidence show that successful allo-cation does not imply that a job becomes ready: the problem extends from “GPUs cannot be allocated” to “GPUs have been allocated but the job never becomes ready.” The coexistence of compute scarcity and compute waste has become a prominent obstacle to improving the efficiency of AI infrastructure. Characterization studies of production clusters further reveal how this contradiction manifests. Astudy of Alibaba production traces [1] points out that high GPU demand does not translate into high ef-fective utilization: idle GPUs cannot be allocated promptly because of missing matched CPUs, network-locality constraints, user over-reservation, and whole GPUs idling across machines. Another characteri-zation study of MLaaS clusters [2] likewise documents systematic mismatches between the form of GPU supply and job demand. These works, however, focus mainly on cluster-level allocation and fragmentation rates; we focus on a more basic question: can a placed job actually become ready? We answer this question at the job level. On our trace, placement is usually not the bottleneck: only one record requests more GPUs than the machine it was placed on can hold (Section 3.3). The real loss is not “cannot fit” but the substantial share of placed jobs that never become ready. We call this phenomenon ineffective occupancy: GPUs are occupied by a job, but the job never truly runs. Among the 40,129,894 labeled execution records we use, this phenomenon accounts for 52.70%. Its existence implies that scheduling optimization should not pursue placement counts alone; it should also consider whether a placed job can actually run. To address this problem, we propose Readiness-Aware Scheduling (RAS). Our contributions are three-fold: 

(1) Identifying and measuring ineffective occupancy: We focus on the phenomenon that a sub-stantial share of placed jobs never become ready, and we measure its scale and distribution at the job level. 

(2) Building a readiness predictor and analyzing feature contributions: Using only nine attributes observable at admission time, we build a readiness predictor and verify its ranking ability under a machine-grouped split. A contribution analysis of feature groups shows that the model’s score is driven mainly by job profile, duration, and GPU model rather than being uniformly distributed. 

(3) Proposing and validating a readiness-aware scheduling algorithm: With the placement rule fixed, we propose an admission-ordering algorithm based on predicted readiness probability and verify its effect in suppressing ineffective occupancy. The remainder of this paper is organized as follows. Section 2 reviews related work, Section 3 defines the problem and the data, Section 4 presents the diagnostic analysis, Section 5 develops and evaluates readiness-aware scheduling, and Section 6 discusses the results and concludes. 

## 2 Related Work 

This section reviews existing work along three lines: cluster characterization and effective utilization, placement optimization and admission ordering, and the mapping from prediction to scheduling. Sec-tion 2.4 further contrasts our work with these lines. 

2.1 Cluster Characterization and Effective Utilization 

Cluster characterization studies focus on GPU utilization, resource allocation, and fragmentation [1, 2, 5]. Early analyses of Alibaba production traces [5], together with measurements of heterogeneous MLaaS 2clusters [2], document a persistent gap between requested size and actual usage. A study of Alibaba production traces [1] further attributes this gap mainly to supply-side factors: idle GPUs are not scarce in aggregate but are scattered across machines, lack matched CPUs, violate network-locality constraints, or are held by user reservations; the same study notes that fractional-GPU fragmentation has become relatively minor, while the idling of whole GPUs across machines has become the main problem. Industry reports and reviews estimate utilization at the 5% and 25%–50% levels, respectively [3, 4]; scheduling studies give broad estimates of 5%–30% [6] or below 50% [7]. These works take the cluster as the unit of analysis and focus on allocation rate, fragmentation, or overall utilization. For example, the defragmentation algorithm of the Alibaba trace study [1] reduces the number of machines with idle resources. But a higher cluster-level allocation rate does not necessarily im-ply higher effective output at the job level. Work on ghost occupancy [8] observes a related phenomenon: Kubernetes clusters contain GPUs that are allocated yet produce no useful work, and such allocations are detected and reclaimed. Overall, these works lay the foundation for understanding GPU utilization, but their unit of analysis remains the cluster and does not extend to the job level. 

2.2 Placement Optimization and Admission Ordering 

(1) Placement optimization: Placement optimization is one of the main lines of GPU cluster schedul-ing and has evolved from generic bin-packing rules to GPU-specific schedulers. Early one-dimensional bin-packing rules (First-Fit, Best-Fit, Worst-Fit) target packing efficiency and struggle to capture the het-erogeneity of GPU jobs. FGD [9] moves the objective from “can the job be placed” to “is the remaining space still usable after placement”: the quality of a placement decision depends not only on whether the current job fits but also on whether the remaining space will remain usable in the future. Later GPU-specific schedulers incorporate job characteristics: Tiresias [10] orders jobs by least at-tained service; Gavel [11] solves a linear program over a speed matrix; Pollux [12] chooses the number of GPUs by goodput; Sia [13] mixes coarse- and fine-grained resource allocation; and Synergy [14] allo-cates according to resource sensitivity. These works cover the two core questions of placement: where to place a job and how many GPUs to allocate to it. Industrial schedulers typically abstract placement as a pluggable scoring module; for example, Borg (Google’s cluster management system) [15] and Kubernetes [16] implement placement rules as configurable scoring items. 

(2) Admission ordering: Admission ordering has recently attracted attention as a problem in its own right. Work in queueing theory [6] proves that under multi-dimensional resource demands the optimal admission-ordering problem is NP-hard, and points out that the greedy policy of Kueue (a Kubernetes job queueing controller) lacks theoretical guarantees in the waiting-time dimension. In contrast to this theoretical analysis, industry has made admission a separate controller: Runway (an AI video company) uses Kueue as its admission controller and raises GPU utilization by more than 20 percentage points [17]. In that architecture, Kueue decides whether a job starts, and kube-scheduler (the default Kuber-netes scheduler) [16] decides where admitted pods run. Another line starts from scheduling as a whole: a multi-cluster compute scheduling algorithm based on deep deterministic policy gradient (DDPG) re-inforcement learning [18] solves scheduling as an integrated optimization problem and does not treat admission ordering separately. 

2.3 From Prediction to Scheduling 

By prediction target and scheduling interface, existing work falls into three categories—duration predic-tion, resource prediction, and readiness/startup-delay prediction; learning-based scheduling can serve as a separate decision interface. 

(1) Duration prediction and learning-based scheduling: Duration prediction is the most mature direction: it answers “how long will the job run”, outputs a scalar duration, and serves placement or backfilling decisions [19]. Another line is learning-based scheduling: replacing hand-written heuristics with policy networks and embedding predictors into the decision policy [20]. GPARS [21] uses a graph 3attention network to predict job duration and reduces waiting time by 10.29% and improves resource uti-lization by 7.47% on the Alibaba and Philly traces; its prediction target is still duration, and the prediction is used for GPU-type assignment. 

(2) Resource prediction: On GPU clusters, prediction-driven scheduling has made several advances in recent years. PRISM [22] uses an LSTM to predict job arrival patterns and resource demands, reducing mean job completion time (JCT) by 25%–40%. Wind [23] uses historical execution patterns to predict task demands and completion times and outperforms the default Kubernetes scheduler on a 139-machine cluster. GFS [24] uses a lightweight prediction model to anticipate GPU demand across tenants, reducing the eviction rate by 33.0% and queueing delay by 44.1%. 

(3) Readiness prediction and startup-delay estimation: A study of the Alibaba GPU trace [25] predicts instance readiness status, scheduling delay, and capacity risk for a recommendation-service sce-nario, using a large language model (LLM) augmentation approach. On container startup delay, ADAPT [26] tracks container cold-start duration with an online exponentially weighted moving average (EWMA) estimator and feeds it as a dynamic variable into a model-predictive controller to optimize the replica count and planning horizon; CLUEAI [27] combines machine learning with statistical methods to predict CPU allocation during container startup, reducing average application startup time by 19.9%.

Taken together, the main differences between this paper and the four lines above can be summarized in two respects. (1) Different problem framing. Cluster characterization remains at the cluster level and focuses on allocation rate, fragmentation, or overall utilization; we move the unit of analysis down to individual jobs and reveal ineffective occupancy—“GPU capacity is sufficient but the job never becomes ready.” Accordingly, we propose RAS, which uses predicted readiness probability as the admission score and admits jobs in descending order, thereby reducing the extent to which GPUs are held by jobs that remain unready for long periods. (2) Different decision variables and prediction targets. Fragmentation scheduling and GPU-specific schedulers optimize the placement choice; prediction-driven scheduling predicts duration, arrival patterns, or resource demand and embeds the predictor in the placement algorithm. We fix the placement rule, adjust only the admission order, and predict the job-level binary readiness probability. Our predictor is therefore not embedded in the placement decision but used directly for admission ordering. The evaluation 4target differs accordingly: existing work asks whether placement choices are sound and whether utilization improves, whereas we ask whether GPUs are actually put to use. 

## 3 Problem Definition and Data 

Resource decisions are a counting problem in homogeneous clusters and a matching problem in heteroge-neous clusters: the former asks “is there any GPU left?”, whereas the latter asks “do the available GPUs satisfy the job’s demand?” This section turns ineffective occupancy into a measurable, interpretable ob-ject: we first give the definitions, then state three problems and fix the notation, and then introduce the data and the evaluation protocol.

3.3 Evaluation Protocol and Leakage Check 

If a field’s value is produced only after the admission decision is complete, we exclude it from the feature matrix. All models in Section 4 exclude these fields. The execution-summary table has no time column, so we use a single-point observation and do not analyze temporal drift or seasonality.

Column Single-feature ROC-AUC Treatment

The two fields in Table 5 are excluded because of when their values arise: both are produced only after the admission decision is complete and therefore cannot enter the feature matrix. schedule_status is the scheduler’s outcome flag for the execution. Its relation to the label is almost degenerate in one direction: its conditional probability of being 1 is 1.0000 on ready records and 0.9890 on non-ready records; but as a single predictor it carries no information, with a single-feature ROC-AUC of only 0.5055 after aligning its direction with the label. ready_delay_sec is the decisive case: it is measured only after a job becomes ready, its single-feature ROC-AUC is 0.9644, and a model that includes it would directly leak the label. By comparison, the single-feature ROC-AUC of the nine observable attributes ranges from 0.5056 to 0.5786. One further fact concerns the data convention. The execution-summary table contains only execution spans already placed on machines with sufficient GPU capacity: among the 40,129,894 labeled records, only one has gpu_request exceeding the GPU capacity of the machine it was placed on. Hence the 52.70% non-ready share is not due to insufficient capacity but to ineffective occupancy. This distinction separates our work from cluster-level utilization analysis; Section 4.4 analyzes its structure. We split by whole machine: if records from the same machine appeared in both the training and test sets, the model could memorize whether that machine’s jobs are usually ready, whereas a new machine at deployment provides no such information. Grouping by machine puts records from the same machine 8in the same fold, so the evaluation measures generalization to new machines; a random split is optimistic, whereas a machine-grouped split is closer to deployment. We use the machine-grouped split as the pri-mary protocol. Evaluation relies mainly on a threshold-independent ranking metric (ROC-AUC) because the classes are nearly balanced and accuracy is uninformative; intervals come from bootstrap resampling of held-out records. 

## 4 Diagnosis: Readiness Is Predictable and Decomposable 

This section answers the three questions. The method is given in Sections 4.1 and 4.2, the contribution analysis in Section 4.3, and the structural evidence in Section 4.4. The output is the readiness probability

## 5 Readiness-Aware Scheduling 

Section 4 narrows the adjustable variable to a single place: the admission order. This section answers Problem 3, turns this restriction into a deployable, comparable rule, and measures it with a different evaluation quantity. Section 5.1 explains why the evaluation quantity must change, Section 5.2 gives the algorithm, complexity, and policy set, Section 5.3 gives the experimental setup and main results, and Section 5.4 performs ablation and sensitivity analyses. 

5.1 From Placement Metrics to Effective Output 

We begin with a minimal example. A cluster has one idle machine and two jobs: one can become ready and one cannot, and the idle GPUs suffice for only one of them. First-come, first-served (FCFS) queues them by arrival; if the job that never becomes ready arrives first, the GPUs are held by a job that cannot become ready. Under a different order, the same GPUs can serve a job that actually runs. In both orders the number of machines and GPUs is unchanged, yet the GPUs actually put to use differ. This example shows that placement metrics (match rate, allocation rate, fragmentation) measure whether GPUs are occupied and do not answer whether they are used effectively. We focus on the latter: the measurements in Section 4 show that on our trace almost every request that a machine can hold is placed, so placement feasibility is not the bottleneck; the real loss is that a substantial fraction of placed jobs never become ready. The evaluation quantity in this chapter is therefore not how many jobs are placed but the effective output: the share of cluster GPUs occupied effectively, and the job-level metrics derived from it. Definition 2 in Section 3 already defines this quantity as 𝑊 (𝑃 ), and this chapter uses it directly as the primary objective; the remaining metrics are computed on the same placed set 𝑃 under fixed conventions. To make comparisons fair, this chapter fixes the placement rule of all policies to best-fit (the machine with the smallest idle GPU count that can still hold the job); policies differ only in admission order. The resulting differences therefore come only from admission order, not from the placement algorithm— which is also the only adjustable variable identified by the contribution analysis in Section 4. 13 Assumption 1 (Replay assumption) . The admission replay experiment re-simulates admission on histor-ical jobs in a new order, keeps the placement rule fixed, and changes only the admission order; readiness labels are therefore treated as attributes of a job under the current machine supply and enter the compar-ison. 

The boundary of this assumption is discussed in Section 6.2.

Thus “three groups dominate” explains which kinds of attributes the problem concentrates in, but turning it into a usable admission rule requires predicting with all nine attributes jointly, not just the top groups.

## 6 Discussion and Conclusion 

6.1 Summary 

This paper formalizes ineffective occupancy as a measurable object, measures it at the job level, and pro-poses a readiness-aware scheduling algorithm on that basis. The main findings are as follows. Ineffective occupancy is predictable and has a clear structure. Under the machine-grouped five-fold protocol, readi-ness prediction reaches an ROC-AUC of 0.8187, better than the majority-class baseline (0.4960) and the 19 lookup baselines. The contribution analysis shows that the model’s score is driven mainly by job profile, duration, and GPU model, with request size contributing least. Ineffective occupancy is spatially concen-trated: measured by the variance ratio 𝐷 of Eq. (3), the observed variance of per-machine readiness rates is far larger than the variance expected from a binomial distribution at the same sample size (values of 𝐷 

in Figure 7). Readiness-aware scheduling substantially reduces ineffective occupancy. With placement fixed and only the admission order adjusted, RAS lowers the ineffective-occupancy ratio from 43.64% un-der FCFS to 27.15% and raises large-request effective coverage from 46.11% to 63.18%, without harming capacity utilization. The gain comes mainly from the predictor’s discriminative power rather than from a simple order perturbation: compared with the theoretical optimum when every job’s true readiness label is known, RAS achieves 65.1% of the improvement potential, whereas the two interpretable rules derived from the contribution analysis achieve only 38.5% and 35.1%. The work can be summarized in three respects. 

(1) Existing work focuses on cluster-level allocation and fragmentation rates: We move the unit of analysis down to individual jobs, reveal the phenomenon of “GPU capacity is sufficient but the job never becomes ready”, and measure its scale and distribution at the job level. 

(2) Using only the nine attributes observable at admission time: We build a readiness predictor and achieve good ranking performance under a machine-grouped five-fold split. The contribution analysis shows that ineffective occupancy is not uniformly distributed random noise but a structural phenomenon organized along job-side attributes such as job profile, duration, and GPU model; it can therefore serve as a stable basis for admission scoring. 

(3) Given that the capacity constraint on the placement side is not tight and job attributes cannot be changed: We narrow the adjustable variable to the admission order and propose an admission-ordering rule based on predicted readiness probability. Experiments show that the rule stably reduces ineffec-tive occupancy across clusters and loads, and the allocation rate stays essentially unchanged (98.52% vs 98.53%). In summary, readiness status should become an explicit consideration for heterogeneous GPU cluster schedulers, just like placement success rate. 

6.2 Limitations and Future Work 

The applicability boundaries of our conclusions are as follows. The readiness label is an observable indicator of readiness status. The release notes define the field as “whether the pod has ever been ready”, and we use it accordingly as an observable indicator of whether a job can enter the ready state. We also exclude a known alternative explanation: ASI’s “Standby” mechanism allows users to voluntarily yield compute, a state recorded by the state_public field of the pod-hour table, which is not in the execution-summary table we use; hence “not ready” as defined here does not include voluntary yielding. Extrapolation requires checking the supply and demand structures. Our conclusions come from ob-servations of a single production cluster. Before extrapolating to other heterogeneous clusters, one should check whether the supply structure (GPUs-per-machine distribution, rack homogeneity) and the demand structure (request-size distribution) are similar. CPU is not examined separately at the mechanism level. We treat CPU cores per GPU as supply-side structural background (its range is shown in Figure 2) and do not include CPU requests in the model. The replay evaluation assumes that readiness outcomes do not change with admission order. In reality, image-cache state (warm vs. cold), concurrent startup contention on the same machine, and network congestion can all cause the same job to obtain different readiness out-comes under different admission orders; end-to-end effects require online deployment tests. Future work can proceed in several directions: first, reproduce our conclusions on other heterogeneous clusters with similar supply and demand structures to test extrapolation; second, include CPU requests in the model and examine the effect of joint CPU–GPU matching on readiness; third, explore finer-grained admission-ordering policies, for example, jointly optimizing predicted readiness, job waiting time, and priority in a multi-objective formulation. 20 References
