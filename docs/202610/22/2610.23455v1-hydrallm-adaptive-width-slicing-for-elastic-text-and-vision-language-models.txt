Abstract 

LLMs and LVLMs are increasingly deployed across heterogeneous hardware, ranging from data-center GPUs to resource-constrained edge devices. Their fixed-size architectures limit the ability to reuse a single model across de-ployment settings with different compute, la-tency, and model-size targets. We introduce HydraLLM 1, an elastic MQA-based architec-ture. Its number of active attention heads deter-mines the effective widths of the embeddings, attention projections, feed-forward layers, and the output head, forming a nested family of subnetworks within a single parameter set. We introduce a static-to-elastic conversion pipeline that, starting from a pre-trained model check-point, includes importance-driven weight re-ordering and stochastic subnetwork training with knowledge distillation. We extend this principle to multimodal inference through an elastic projection module whose output width follows the active text width. HydraLLM cov-ers a wider range of active parameter configura-tions than FFN-only elastic architectures. With 75% of heads active, HydraLLM achieves 2.2 percentage points higher TriviaQA accuracy than a state-of-the-art nested baseline configura-tion which has 17.25% more parameters. With 50% of heads active, our multimodal model achieves a CLIPScore comparable to that of the 7× larger Gemma 3 4B model. With only 25% of heads active, HydraLLM increases token-generation throughput by 75.3% over the full-width configuration.

2 Related Work 

We discuss related work on nested representations and elastic architectures, transformer compression and submodel extraction, and projection-based mul-timodal models. We focus on how these approaches differ from HydraLLM’s end-to-end runtime slic-ing of a pre-trained LLM and its extension to an elastic multimodal projection. Kusupati et al. (2024) introduce multi-granularity, coarse-to-fine representations for embedding models. The same matryoshka nesting principle reappears in later work introducing elasticity within other transformer components. Cai et al. (2020) propose the Once-For-All (OFA) methodology, decoupling architecture search from training by progressively shrinking the convolutional neural network (CNN) kernel size, channel width, and model depth. OFA targets the search and extraction of specialized CNN architectures for different deployment constraints. In contrast, HydraLLM does not perform architec-ture search or progressive shrinking: it converts a pre-trained LLM into one elastic checkpoint whose embedding, attention, FFN, and output widths can be scaled jointly at runtime through the active head count. Devvrit et al. (2024) propose MatFormer, a natively elastic transformer that introduces a Matryoshka-like nested structure specifically only inside the FFN block. Haberer et al. (2024) target embeddings, attention heads, and FFN layers for their nested ViTs. Training employs stochastic subnetwork sampling by drawing one head count k per step and updating only the corresponding subnetwork, which biases optimization toward earlier heads, implicitly ordering them by importance (Haberer et al., 2024). We apply the same training and architectural approach to LLMs and extend it to the vision domain. Cai et al. (2024) and Taghibakhshi et al. (2025) extend the nested approach in the attention and FFN components with routers in their FLEXTRON and Nemotron Elastic models. Both approaches are text-only. FLEXTRON slices only attention heads and FFN, and Nemotron Elastic targets an architecture distinct from our multi-query attention (MQA) (Shazeer, 2019) transformer. Muralidharan et al. (2024) design a static submodel extraction approach by applying iterative structured pruning on a pre-trained model, creating MINITRON. They obtain separate models that are not suitable for elastic inference, like ours. ViTs (Dosovitskiy et al., 2021) adapt the trans-former encoder to images by splitting an image into non-overlapping patches, flattening each patch, and projecting it into the model’s embedding space to obtain patch embeddings. For multimodal models, Liu et al. (2023) connect a pre-trained CLIP (Rad-ford et al., 2021) vision encoder to an open-source LLM via a projection layer that maps visual fea-tures into the LLM’s text embedding space, pro-ducing so-called soft tokens. They merge the soft tokens with text tokens and process them jointly through self-attention. Deitke et al. (2024) build on these concepts with the Molmo family, while Marafioti et al. (2025) propose SmolVLM as a series of compact multimodal models. Gemma Team et al. (2025) release Gemma 3 with a tai-lored SigLIP (Zhai et al., 2023) vision encoder. We follow the training and architectural principles of these multimodal models and utilize insights from their training approaches. However, we employ the elastic LLM and multimodal projection. 

3 HydraLLM: Architecture and Pipeline 

This section introduces HydraLLM’s design and the pipeline for converting a static pre-trained LLM into an elastic text and multimodal model. Specifi-cally, we describe the text architecture and width-control in Section 3.1, the static-to-elastic conver-sion procedure including reordering and the opti-mization objective details in Section 3.2, and the multimodal assembly in Section 3.3. Our pipeline starts from a static pre-trained text checkpoint and converts it into an elastic decoder whose active width is controlled at runtime; see Figure 2. We then extend the same elasticity principle to the mul-timodal setting with an elastic projection module. 

3.1 Runtime Width-Control 

HydraLLM adapts HydraViT’s (Haberer et al., 2024) head-stacking principle to decoder-only lan-guage models by using the number of active query heads k as a single runtime width-control parame-ter; see Figure 1. A subnetwork keeps the first k

query heads together with the corresponding pre-fixes of the embedding channels, output projection, and FFN neurons. Each sliced dimension scales lin-early with the number of heads k in relation to the total number of query heads Hq: the active embed-ding width is dE, active = k/H q ×dE , with the FFN dimensions following the same proportion. This produces a strict nesting of subnetworks that share parameters across configurations. At inference, se-lecting k computes only the corresponding prefix, so one checkpoint serves all width budgets. We tar-get MQA (Shazeer, 2019) in our design, in which all query heads share a single key-value head. 

3.2 Static-to-Elastic Conversion 

A static pre-trained checkpoint is not immedi-ately suitable for prefix slicing. In an unmodified checkpoint, units of a component are usually not sorted by their importance. Since prefix slicing al-ways retains the first units and discards later units, naïve slicing can remove important parameters and severely degrade smaller subnetworks. HydraLLM employs a two-stage procedure to convert static models into elastic ones. First, the pipeline re-orders the checkpoint offline, moving important units toward the beginning of each sliced dimension so that prefix slicing retains the most informative parameters. Second, it optimizes the checkpoint using stochastic subnetwork training: during each training step, the pipeline samples a single k and updates only the corresponding active subnetwork. Since earlier units are active for more sampled val-ues of k, they receive proportionally more gradient updates, reinforcing the importance ordering and adapting the shared parameters to all configured widths (Taghibakhshi et al., 2025; Muralidharan et al., 2024; Cai et al., 2024; Haberer et al., 2024; Devvrit et al., 2024). 3.2.1 Importance Scoring and Reordering 

The reordering step occurs before any training and requires the definition of importance. We com-pare three importance measures: Activation magni-tude (Muralidharan et al., 2024), variance-weighted consumer strength (VxC) adapted from Weinstein and Belinkov (2022), and CETT (Zhang et al., 2024), which scores units by their normalized con-tribution to the component output and therefore aligns well with prefix slicing. We estimate unit im-portance from activation statistics collected during a forward pass over a calibration text corpus. Af-ter computing the importance scores, we permute the corresponding tensors so that important units appear earlier along the sliceable dimensions: For embedding-width reordering, we apply one global permutation to the token embedding channels and propagate it consistently through the RMSNorm weights, the input columns of the Q/K/V projec-tions, the output rows of the attention output pro-jection, the input columns of the FFN gate and up projections, the output rows of the FFN down pro-jection, the final normalization layer, and, if untied, the language-model head. For attention reordering, we permute the query heads and the corresponding attention-output input blocks. For FFN reordering, we permute FFN neurons by moving the rows of the gate and up projections and the corresponding columns of the down projection. This reordering step preserves the function of the full model. 

3.2.2 Optimization Objective 

After reordering, we train the model. The training objective we employ is a combination of autoregres-sive language modeling, logit regularization, and teacher supervision. The primary task loss, LCE , is standard next-token cross-entropy. We additionally add a z-loss term Lz , weighted by λz (Chowdhery et al., 2022), and logit-based knowledge distilla-tion (Hinton et al., 2015). 3.3 Elastic Multimodal Assembly 

We construct the multimodal variant of HydraLLM by combining the language decoder with a static pre-trained SigLIP-based vision encoder (Gemma Team et al., 2025; Zhai et al., 2023). The key differ-ence from a conventional projection-based LVLM is that HydraLLM also makes the projection elas-tic. Let dP denote the projection output width. We enforce dP, active = dE, active , so the same runtime parameter k controls both the active text decoder width and the projection output width; see Figure 3. This creates a single elasticity axis across modal-ities: selecting a smaller text subnetwork also se-lects a correspondingly smaller visual projection output.

5.3 Multimodal Pipeline Results 

The first stage in the multimodal training pipeline is aligning the projection with the text embedding space. The projection-only block of Table 4 shows that the untrained projection already produces a non-trivial CLIPScore (Hessel et al., 2021; Rad-ford et al., 2021) across all head configurations, indicating that it inherits useful structure from the Gemma 3 4B vision tower. Training the projec-tion in isolation yields only modest improvements over the untrained baseline. During training, we observe a plateau in the loss after 300 to 350 steps, depending on the head configuration; see more de-tails in Appendix C.2. The rapid plateau indicates a lack of capacity in the projection layer, caused by the much smaller projection due to the lower text embedding dimension of the Gemma 3 270M model compared to Gemma 3 4B. The projection has 25% of the parameters of the Gemma 3 4B model and therefore possesses much less represen-tational power (Gemma Team et al., 2025). Joint training of the projection with the text model substantially improves the CLIPScore; see the projection-plus-text-model block of Table 4. The models trained from an already projection-pre-aligned checkpoint perform best across all head configurations, especially at H2, implying that aligning the projection before joint training of the projection and text model is beneficial. When training all model parameters together, the all-parameters block shows that results are similar across different starting points, indicating that joint optimization can compensate for an inferior start-ing projection. The elastic projection establishes feasibil-ity, but the vision-to-language interface remains a suspected bottleneck and does not yet yield robust caption quality. 

6 Conclusion 

We introduce HydraLLM, an MQA-based (Shazeer, 2019) elastic architecture that employs stochastic head stacking and end-to-end width slicing. A sin-gle runtime parameter k controls the active width of the decoder end-to-end, from token embeddings through attention and FFN projections to the output head, producing nested subnetworks from a sin-gle parameter set. We extended the elasticity axis to LVLMs through an elastic projection module whose output width follows the active text width. On text, magnitude-based reordering of embed-dings combined with CETT-based (Zhang et al., 2024) reordering of heads and FFN units yields the strongest subnetworks, reducing perplexity by up to a factor of approximately 4,500 compared to no reordering. However, the effect is reduced to at most 14% after training. Knowledge distilla-tion (Hinton et al., 2015) is crucial during training and improves TriviaQA exact-match accuracy at 75% of active heads by 97.3%. HydraLLM covers a substantially wider parameter range at competitive accuracy compared to MatFormer-style (Devvrit et al., 2024) FFN slicing. It achieves larger token-generation speed-ups of up to 75.3%, but only mod-est prompt-processing improvements. On the multimodal side, scaled training matches or exceeds the Gemma 3 4B CLIPScore (Hessel et al., 2021; Radford et al., 2021) of 65.3 by 2.5%, 5.1% and 6.6% with two, three and four active heads respectively, confirming that the elastic text backbone transfers to the vision-language setting. However, the model remains near random accu-racy on MMMU, and qualitative analysis reveals hallucinations and weak object grounding. These limitations point to clear avenues for future re-search: (i) replacing the constrained small single-layer projector with a larger or a multi-layer FFN projection, which would better align vision fea-tures with the elastic text backbone; (ii) longer and more diverse multimodal training schedules to improve grounding beyond captioning tasks; and (iii) combining HydraLLM’s end-to-end slic-ing with MatFormer-style per-layer granularity for finer accuracy-compute control. We leave these directions for future work. Acknowledgments

C Additional Multimodal Results 

This section provides additional results on text re-covery after multimodal training and the optimiza-tion behavior of the elastic projection during align-ment. 

C.1 Text Recovery after Multimodal Training 

Table 12 lists TriviaQA accuracies after text-only continued pre-training and after subsequent multi-modal or text-only fine-tuning stages. The compar-ison illustrates how teacher choice and continued fine-tuning affect recovery of factual recall after the multimodal stage. The text-only baseline is trained for one million steps. We compare it with the default multimodal run and with two text fine-tuning variants that use either Gemma 3 270M or Gemma 3 4B as the teacher. The Gemma 3 4B teacher consistently outper-forms the 270M teacher at the four-head configu-ration, suggesting that the larger teacher transfers more factual knowledge to the student. The im-proved accuracy relative to the long-run text base-line is likely due to the shift from packed context-window training to training on individual padded samples, together with the lower learning rate and larger batch size used in this stage.
