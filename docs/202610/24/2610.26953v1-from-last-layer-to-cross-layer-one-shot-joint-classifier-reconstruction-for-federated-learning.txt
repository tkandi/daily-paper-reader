Abstract 

Federated learning (FL) enables collaborative model training while preserving data privacy. However, non-independent and identically distributed (non-IID) data inevitably introduces classifier bias, leading to significant performance degradation. Existing studies primarily focus on calibrating or reconstruct-ing the classifier after FL training to alleviate this bias. We find that the last-layer representations of the global model are often suboptimal for establishing a global decision bound-ary. Instead, classifiers constructed based on intermediate-layer representations can achieve superior performance. This finding provides a critical insight into overcoming the perfor-mance bottleneck inherent in existing paradigms. Neverthe-less, identifying the optimal layer without auxiliary datasets or prior knowledge remains a formidable challenge. In this paper, we propose FLARE ( Federated Cross-Layer Joint Cl Assifier 

RE construction) which rethinks classifier reconstruction from a cross-layer perspective and generalizes ridge regression to jointly exploit cross-layer representations, thereby surpassing the performance of any individual layer. Specifically, after FL training, clients perform only a single forward pass to extract class-conditional statistics from predefined layers. The server aggregates these statistics to construct a joint cross-layer ridge classifier, where an adaptive coupling coefficient regulates cross-layer correlations, thereby facilitating the integration of complementary discriminative information. Extensive exper-iments across 11 datasets, 8 model architectures, and diverse data heterogeneity scenarios demonstrate that FLARE, as a plug-and-play post-processing method, improves accuracy by 11.35% with only one additional communication round.

Existing classifier reconstruction approaches in FL can be categorized into two strategies, both of which build upon the global models obtained after FL training or pretrained back-bones. The first strategy reconstructs the classifier by leverag-ing feature statistics uploaded by clients, such as class means, covariance matrices, or regression statistics (Goswami et al. 2025; Legate et al. 2023; Fanì et al. 2024; Guan, Zhou, and Gu 2025). The second synthesizes pseudo-features that approxi-mate the global distribution to fine-tune the classifier (Beitol-lahi et al. 2025; Chen, Wu, and Ji 2024; Tang et al. 2024; Pan et al. 2024; Huang et al. 2024). Despite their promise in mitigating classifier bias, these methods are fundamentally limited, as they focus exclusively on last-layer representa-tions. This paradigm implicitly assumes that deeper repre-arXiv:2610.26953v1 [cs.DC] 18 Oct 2026 sentations exhibit superior linear separability and semantic discriminability, making them optimal for establishing global decision boundaries(Alain and Bengio 2017; Cohen et al. 2020). However, under non-IID FL conditions, deep features are sensitive to local data distributions, which exacerbates representation divergence across clients and increases insta-bility during aggregation(Li et al. 2023a; Wang et al. 2024; Shi et al. 2023). Consequently, relying solely on last-layer representations undermines the reliability and creates a crit-ical bottleneck for existing reconstruction paradigms. To quantify the impact of representation depth on model performance, we conduct a preliminary study. We first train a global model via FedAvg and freeze its backbone. Then, we attach linear classifiers to intermediate layers and fine-tune all classifiers. As illustrated in Figure 1, classifiers derived from certain intermediate-layer representations outperform those on the last-layer. This reveals a critical limitation in existing paradigms. For classifier reconstruction, the optimal choice is not strictly limited to the last layer. Instead, it varies across model architectures, datasets, and other factors. However, identifying the optimal layer remains a formidable challenge in FL, where privacy constraints limit access to auxiliary data and prior knowledge(Wu et al. 2024; Zhu et al. 2023). Building upon these findings, we propose FLARE (Federated Cross-Layer Joint Cl Assifier RE construction) designed to overcome the last-layer decision bottleneck and surpass the optimal-layer performance. FLARE introduces a novel cross-layer perspective for federated classifier recon-struction and further generalizes ridge regression over cross-layer representations, thereby integrating complementary in-formation into a unified decision space. To suppress unreli-able cross-layer correlations, FLARE introduces an adaptive coupling coefficient to regulate inter-layer interactions. Con-sequently, FLARE transcends the limitations of last-layer reconstruction inherent in existing methods, yielding signif-icant improvements in the global model’s performance. Ex-tensive experiments demonstrate that FLARE consistently outperforms state-of-the-art (SOTA) baselines across vari-ous settings. Our contributions are summarized as follows: 

• We transcend mainstream FL classifier reconstruction paradigms that focus exclusively on the last layer, re-vealing the critical value of intermediate-layer represen-tations under data heterogeneity. We accordingly propose FLARE to integrate cross-layer discriminative informa-tion for classifier reconstruction. This insight provides a novel perspective on federated classifier reconstruction. 

• As a plug-and-play method, FLARE further generalizes ridge regression from a single representation space to a jointly optimized cross-layer space. It also introduces an adaptive coupling coefficient to suppress unreliable cor-relations, thereby constructing a superior joint classifier. 

• Extensive experiments across five image datasets with three model architectures and six text datasets with five model architectures, covering diverse FL settings, demonstrate that FLARE consistently outperforms exist-ing SOTA methods while preserving privacy. 

# Related Work 

FL under Data Heterogeneity 

Existing research on FL under data heterogeneity follows two primary paradigms: general federated learning (GFL) and Personalized Federated Learning (P-FL). GFL aims to learn a global model applicable to all clients, as exempli-fied by FedAvg(McMahan et al. 2017). However, non-IID data severely constrains the convergence and performance of the global model. Existing studies have proposed optimiza-tion strategies, including mitigating local update drift (Li et al. 2020; Gao et al. 2022), gradient correction(Hsu, Qi, and Brown 2019; Acar et al. 2021), and introducing distil-lation for knowledge preservation (Lu et al. 2023; Lee et al. 2022). In contrast, P-FL seeks to learn client-specific mod-els adapted to each client’s data distributions (Smith et al. 2017), subsequently inspiring diverse approaches including local fine-tuning of global models (Fallah, Mokhtari, and Ozdaglar 2020), personalized classifiers (Collins et al. 2021), jointly learned personalized components (T Dinh, Tran, and Nguyen 2020; Li et al. 2021), and personalized aggregation weights (Huang et al. 2021; Zhang et al. 2023). 

Classifier Reconstruction in FL 

Existing FL classifier reconstruction methods primarily fo-cus on the post-processing phase with a frozen global backbone. Specifically, CCVR(Luo et al. 2021), FedCRGP (Pan et al. 2024), and DFL-FS(Chen, Wu, and Ji 2024) adopt a generation-based approach, where clients upload class means and covariances, subsequently server gener-ates pseudo-features to reconstruct the classifier. Similarly, FedPFT(Beitollahi et al. 2025) utilizes client-uploaded Gaus-sian mixture models to synthesize features for classifier re-training. FedNCM(Legate et al. 2023) simplifies this process by requiring only weighted class means to construct a nearest class mean classifier at the server. Alternatively, Fed3R(Fanì et al. 2024) leverages a recursive regression algorithm, aggre-gating client-side regression statistics to construct the classi-fier. To enhance model reliability and robustness, APH(Chen et al. 2024) samples multiple classifiers and combines them after differentiated fine-tuning. Furthermore, FedCGS(Guan, Zhou, and Gu 2025) builds a parameter-free Gaussian Naive Bayes classifier based on local feature statistics, while Fed-COF(Goswami et al. 2025) exploits properties of the means distribution for unbiased covariance estimation. However, these methods reconstruct classifiers solely based on the last layer, limiting their performance to a single representation space. In contrast, FLARE reconstructs a joint classifier, en-abling it to integrate cross-layer discriminative information.

# Experiment 

Experimental Setup 

Datasets and Models. To comprehensively evaluate the ef-fectiveness of FLARE, we conduct extensive experiments on both image and text classification tasks. ❶ Image datasets and models. We consider five datasets covering both nat-ural and medical image domains, including SVHN (Netzer et al. 2011), CIFAR-10 (Krizhevsky, Nair, and Hinton 2009), CIFAR-100 (Krizhevsky, Nair, and Hinton 2009), CINIC-10 (Darlow et al. 2018), and BloodMNIST (Yang et al. 2023). We employ three representative backbone models, namely ResNet-18 (He et al. 2016), VGG-11 (Simonyan and Zis-serman 2014), and MobileNetV2 (Sandler et al. 2018). ❷

Text datasets and models. We further evaluate FLARE on six widely used text datasets: AG News (Zhang, Zhao, and LeCun 2015), SST-2 (Socher et al. 2013), IMDb (Maas et al. 2011), 20 Newsgroups (Lang 1995), DBpedia-14 (Zhang, Zhao, and LeCun 2015), and Yahoo Answers Topics (Zhang, Zhao, and LeCun 2015) on five pre-trained language mod-els, including BERT (Devlin et al. 2019), DistilBERT (Sanh 2019), XLNet (Yang et al. 2019), ELECTRA (Clark et al. 2020), and MiniLM (Wang et al. 2020). For each backbone, we predefined four layers (three intermediate and the last layer). The layer selection criteria, predefined layer locations, and feature dimensions are detailed in Appendices D and E. 

Baselines. We comprehensively evaluate FLARE as a post-processing method from two perspectives. ❶ FL al-gorithms for the training phase. To obtain the global model, we consider the canonical FedAvg and advanced methods de-signed to mitigate data heterogeneity, including FedDecorr (Shi et al. 2023), FedGELA (Fan et al. 2023), and FedSOL (Lee et al. 2024). ❷ Classifier reconstruction methods for post-processing. These SOTA baselines serve as direct com-petitors to FLARE. After FL training with the above algo-rithms, we freeze the backbone and apply CCVR (Luo et al. 2021), Fed3R (Fanì et al. 2024), APH (Chen et al. 2024), FedPFT (Beitollahi et al. 2025), FedCOF (Goswami et al. 2025), and FedCGS (Guan, Zhou, and Gu 2025) to recon-struct the classifier. Although several methods assume pre-trained models, we adapt them to the same cold-start setting as our baselines for a fair comparison. Additional compar-isons with other methods can be found in Appendix G. 

Implementation Details. Following standard FL settings (Lu et al. 2023; Lee et al. 2022), we consider two chal-lenging data heterogeneity scenarios: (1) Dirichlet non-IID, where the label distribution is controlled by a parameter α

(a smaller α indicates higher skewness); and (2) Patholog-ical non-IID, where each client possesses only S classes. During the FL training phase, all 100 clients employ SGD with momentum of 0.9 and weight decay of 10 −5. The batch size is set to 128, and the initial learning rate is 0.01, which decays exponentially by a factor of 0.99 after each communi-cation round. In each round, 5% of clients participate in local updates for 5 epochs. To ensure sufficient convergence, the total rounds are set to 500. After training, all classifier recon-struction methods are executed based on the frozen global model backbone. λ is set to 10 −3 according to the sensitivity analysis provided in Appendix H. Experimental Results 

Overall Comparison. Tables 1–2 and Appendix Table 1 compare the accuracy of FLARE against existing SOTA clas-sifier reconstruction methods across three architectures, five datasets, and Dirichlet non-IID settings α ∈ { 0.05 , 0.1, 0.3}.Methods marked with "+" indicate that the reconstruction methods after FL training (e.g., FedAvg), and the reported accuracy is evaluated after this reconstruction. All results are averaged over three random seeds with small standard devia-tions detailed in Appendix I. The best results are highlighted in bold with a gray background. FLARE consistently and significantly improves the global model performance across all evaluated scenarios, achieving SOTA and competitive results. On average, FLARE yields a 10 .18% accuracy im-provement over the original global model. Compared with the strongest reconstruction baselines, FLARE further boosts ac-curacy by 5.03% , 4.06% , and 8.04% on ResNet-18, VGG-11, and MobileNetV2, respectively. Notably, under extreme het-erogeneity ( α = 0 .05 ), FLARE achieves an average accuracy gain of 23.43% , highlighting its effectiveness in mitigating severe classifier bias. We also evaluate FLARE under the Pathological non-IID setting, with detailed results provided in Appendix B. By exploiting cross-layer discriminative in-formation, FLARE overcomes the performance bottleneck of relying solely on the last layer for reconstruction, thereby achieving consistent and substantial performance gains.

Analysis 

Privacy Analysis. Uploading client-side statistics in FLARE may raise privacy concerns. However, FLARE relies only on aggregated class-conditional statistics rather than client specific instance-level information. Consequently, FLARE is naturally compatible with secure aggregation protocols (Bonawitz et al. 2017; Mai, Yan, and Pang 2024), under which the server can only access the aggregated results. This design fundamentally prevents the server from isolating any single client’s contribution and recovering private data from individual uploads. Furthermore, we consider a more challenging scenario without secure aggregation. The server merely observes class-conditional statistics. To empirically validate the privacy guarantee, we evaluate FLARE against a feature inversion attack (Mahendran and Vedaldi 2015). We simulate an adversarial setting that is highly favorable to the attacker, where the server is assumed to be the adversary and a client holds very few single-class samples, representing an extreme scenario. As shown in Figure 5, when the server has direct access to the raw features of a single sample, the cor-responding data can be reconstructed accurately. In contrast, when the attacker is restricted to the statistics uploaded by FLARE, the reconstructed images collapse to unrecognizable noise, even when the client contains only 2,4, and 8 samples for the same class. The consistently low PSNR values provide quantitative evidence of reconstruction failure.
