Abstract 

The Arcade Learning Environment (ALE) has been a cornerstone of Deep Rein-forcement Learning research, providing a standardized benchmark for evaluating general agents. However, its lack of native support for object-extraction and en-vironment modification restricts research into evaluating whether agents learn robust causal models or rely on brittle visual heuristics. Furthermore, the ALE’s dependence on CPU-based emulation creates a significant bottleneck in modern GPU-accelerated training pipelines, limiting both experimental scale and iteration frequency. In this work, we introduce JAXtari, an open-source, high-performance reimplementation of Atari 2600 environments. By leveraging massive GPU par-allelization, JAXtari allows PPO agents to finish training on 100M environment steps on a single A100-GPU in under an hour. Beyond speed, it provides object-centric observations and a flexible modification system, allowing researchers to, 

e.g. develop neuro-symbolic methods or discover novel shortcuts and subsequently train and adapt their agents rapidly, sometimes in only minutes. 

1 Introduction 

The Arcade Learning Environment (ALE) (Bellemare et al., 2013) has shaped deep reinforcement learning (RL) for over a decade (Mnih et al., 2015; Machado et al., 2018; Badia et al., 2020). As a suite of games originally designed by professional developers for human players, Atari possesses a unique advantage: it is inherently free of the "experimenter bias" that often plagues custom-built RL environments. Consequently, ALE remains the primary RL benchmark ( cf. Figure 1), particularly for studying tasks that require abstract, relational concepts and long-horizon planning, standing in contrast to continuous control benchmarks ( e.g. MuJoCo (Todorov et al., 2012), DM Control (Tunyasuvunakool et al., 2020), D4RL (Fu et al., 2020)), that require low-level motor actuation control. However, despite superhuman aggregate scores achieved on the diverse set of reasoning tasks, ALE is far from being solved. Recent analyses reveal that high performance often stems from shortcut learning rather than robust human-like cognition; agents overfit to highly specific visual cues and frequently fail completely when a task is trivially simplified or visually perturbed (Farebrother et al., 2018; Taiga et al., 2023; Delfosse et al., 2025). Unfortunately ALE prevents it, as it emulates closed-source binary ROMs that are semantically opaque. Both the games logic and their states are not natively interpretable, preventing straightforward implementa-tions of neurosymbolic RL methods and of controlled semantic modifications. Furthermore, the CPU-bound nature of the emulation is completely out of step with modern accelerator-centric training pipelines. The continuous host–device synchronization required to transfer data between the CPU and GPU heavily penalizes wall-clock time, acting as a bottleneck for highly parallel algorithms, population-based training, and broad hyperparameter sweeps. To resolve this dichotomy between the necessity of Atari as a reasoning benchmark and the limitations of its legacy architecture, we introduce and open source JAXtari 1, a framework of faithful, fully JAX-based reimplementations of the ALE environments. By reconstructing the game logic from the ground up rather than wrapping legacy ROMs, we preserve the human-designed, bias-free nature of Atari while making the environments accelerator-native, semantically editable, and inherently object-centric. JAXtari can expose the underlying game logic, rich object-centric (OC) states, and rendering pipeline directly to the user, bridging the gap between high-throughput scalability and the deep, counterfactual evaluation required to diagnose and mitigate goal misgeneralization. Our main contributions are: 

(i) Flexible Modification API: We introduce a modular plugin system for editing game semantics directly at the source-code level, turning robustness evaluations and zero-shot shortcut-learning experiments into simple, few-line changes rather than complex ROM reverse-engineering projects. 

(ii) Object-Centric by Design: Unlike the constraints of ALE, JAXtari exposes structured object states natively from every environment. This removes the need for post-hoc feature extractors and enables clean, rigorous studies of relational, compositional, and logic-augmented RL. 

(iii) ALE Fidelity and Learning Task Parity: Rather than constraining simulation to bit-level 1:1 emulation, JAXtari targets behavioral and learning-task parity, preserving the human-designed mechanics and challenge of ALE. We validate this consistency by comparing learning dynamics and performance rankings across platforms using standard CleanRL and Gymnasium-compatible baselines. 

(iv) Accelerator-Native Throughput: We provide pure-JAX, JIT-compiled, vmap -able environ-ments for high-throughput execution. By eliminating the CPU-GPU communication bottleneck, JAXtari allows population-scale evaluation. The remainder of the paper introduces the design of JAXtari, describes its object-centric and modifi-cation interfaces, evaluates fidelity against the ALE, and benchmarks accelerator-native throughput across games and training settings.

2 JAXtari: The JAX-native Arcade Framework 

This section introduces the JAXtari framework. We first provide the technical details of the JAXtari implementation, then describe the unique features it offers compared to the ALE. 

2.1 Architecture 

Implementing JAXtari requires a fundamental shift from the stateful design of the ALE to a purely functional, stateless paradigm compatible with JAX’s Just-In-Time (JIT) compilation. Unlike the ALE, that relies on the Atari binary ROMs ( e.g. via hardware emulation or automated ROM-to-source transpilation), JAXtari offers JAX-native, bottom-up reimplementations of Atari environments. Concretely, game physics, collision handling, object updates, scoring rules, and termination conditions are manually reimplemented using high-level JAX array operations. The environment transitions are placed inside a single XLA (Accelerated Linear Algebra) compiled computation graph, removing the need for a CPU-bound emulator and enabling direct execution on accelerators. We provide an overview of the JAXtari architecture in Figure 2. Let us now detail the different JAXtari components. 

States. In JAXtari, the transition function follows the signature step(state, action) →

(observation, next_state, reward, done, info) , ensuring full compatibility with the gymnax API (Lange, 2026). The returned values are either primitive types (reward, done) or defined as immutable PyTreeNode objects ( e.g. PongState ) that contain all necessary variables ( e.g. player positions, ball velocity, scores) and enable direct use within JAX transformations. This allows future algorithms to perform interventions outside the game engine, e.g. a (causal) algorithm may intervene on the symbolic state values between two subsequent calls of the step function, changing the game’s behavior drastically from the outside. Stochasticity is handled via jax.random.PRNGKey , which is defined as part of the game state and thus propagated through all steps. 

Observations. JAXtari natively supports two distinct observation modes, managed using wrappers: 

Object-Centric: The core environment state naturally exposes structured data ( e.g. positions, veloci-ties). The ObjectCentricWrapper flattens this information into a feature vector, enabling research into relational RL and agents that reason about object interactions directly, without the need for a separate vision backbone. It also only expose the observed objects (depicted on the screen), but hides internal variables ( e.g. respawning counters). 

Pixel renderings: For compatibility with standard deep RL algorithms ( e.g. DQN, PPO), we provide a JAXGameRenderer for each environment. This differentiable renderer constructs the frames from the state variables using JAX operations, allowing fast convolutional agents training on GPUs. No-tably, both observation types can be retrieved simultaneously for neuro-symbolic approaches. The 3object-centric observation is JAXtari’s native and thus most efficient instantiation, since it can directly be extracted from the state that is passed between transitions. The pixel-based observations require additional rendering functions that impact performance. Despite this, JAXtari is still significantly more efficient than ALE due to the missing necessity of emulating the hardware architecture, its ability to render game states only when necessary (typically every fourth frame is rendered) and the ability to natively downscale images ( cf. Section D.2.1). The implications of the observation mode choice is analyzed in the experimental section. 

Vectorized Accelerator Execution. One of the primary advantages of JAXtari is its ability to leverage massive parallelization. Using jax.vmap , we vectorize the step function to simultaneously run thousands of environment instances on a single GPU. This also avoids the data transfer overhead of CPU-based environment and GPU-hosted agent present in ALE workflows. 

2.2 The Modification System 

A key goal of JAXtari is its modular modification system, designed to test the agent alignment and generalization capabilities. Unlike HackAtari (Delfosse et al., 2024a), which requires complex RAM manipulation, JAXtari’s codebase supports direct, programmatic modifications via a plugin system, making modifications authoring accessible. Furthermore, HackAtari can only modify RAM accessible parts of the state, mostly canceling environments’ dynamics. For example, in Kangaroo, HackAtari e.g. cancels the spawning of the monkeys. While JAXtari also implements such simple modifications, it additionally allows switching the ladders positions (fixed in ALE and not stored in the game’s RAM), changing the sprites, altering the reward system ( e.g. punishing the agent for violent actions such as punching), or modifying the environment dynamics ( e.g. turning bonuses into penalties). To support this flexibility, JAXtari includes two modification systems that intervene at different environment execution points. Internal modifications are applied before the game environment is built, allowing researchers to efficiently alter core rules and assets—like gravity, enemy speeds, or reward structures—by deeply integrating them into the environment at construction time. Conversely, post-step modifications adjust the environment’s state immediately after each game step. This approach is ideal for making direct interventions that don’t require rewriting the underlying game physics, such as instantly relocating an object, hiding specific variables, or forcing a game to end. Modifications can be freely combined and grouped into reusable collections. 

2.3 Using JAXtari for Benchmarking 

Having introduced JAXtari as a flexible framework, we focus on its utility as a benchmark substrate to evaluate agent robustness under semantic distribution shifts and to diagnose reliance on spurious shortcuts. Following the intervention-based protocol established by Delfosse et al. (2025), agents are trained exclusively on unmodified JAXtari environments. We subsequently evaluate their zero-shot transfer performance on controlled semantic variations that preserve the core task structure while systematically altering visual representations, object properties, game dynamics, or reward functions. Discrepancies in performance under these distribution shifts serve as a proxy for the agents’ gener-alization: identifying whether they have internalized robust, task-relevant causal structures or have instead converged on brittle heuristics tied to specific environment configurations. For each game and variation, reports should contain the following metrics (defined formally in Section C). 1. Base Performance (Pbase ). The environmental return achieved by the agent trained on the unmodified environment. 2. Modification Performance (Pmod ). The return achieved by the base-trained agent when evaluated on the modified environment zero-shot. 3. Performance Change (PC ). The shift in performance across all evaluated games, quantifying the sensitivity to game modification. 43 Experimental Evaluation

Experimental Setup 

As standard deep reinforcement learning baseline, we employ PPO (Schulman et al., 2017), a widely used policy-gradient method. For comparability with the ALE baselines, we employ the training and network configuration from the CleanRL PPO baseline (Huang et al., 2022). Unless stated otherwise, we follow standard Atari training conventions, including frame skipping, frame stacking, and preprocessing to 84 × 84 grayscale observations. We evaluate both raw environmental performance, reported as episodic returns, and performance change, which we analyze using the Interquartile Mean (IQM) with bootstrap confidence intervals. Throughput is measured in total steps per second (SPS). JAXtari experiments were run on a single NVIDIA A100 GPU with 40GB of memory, ALE ones on two AMD EPYC 7742 CPUs (128 cores, 256 threads). Unless specified, results are aggregated over 3 random seeds, using 10 M steps per environment. See Appendix D for more details regarding training and environment setup. 

The JAXtari-15 Benchmark. While JAXtari covers many ALE games (45+, cf. Appendix F), we evaluate on a representative subset of 15 environments selected to span six core real-time control categories: Shooters & Space (Asteroids , Beam Rider , Gravitar , Phoenix , Seaquest ), Platformers & Action (Freeway , Frostbite , Kangaroo ), Maze & Pathfinding (Ms. Pac-Man ), Paddle & Ball 

(Breakout , Pong ), Sports & Racing (Enduro , Skiing , Tennis ), and Sparse Reward exploration ( Mon-tezuma’s Revenge ). This selection isolates distinct RL challenges—from reactive motor control (Pong , Breakout ) and delayed credit assignment ( Skiing ) to sparse multi-room exploration ( Mon-tezuma’s Revenge )—while intentionally omitting turn-based or static categories (board and gambling games) to focus on dynamic continuous-time interactions. Each environment is validated against the corresponding ALE version to ensure behavioral consistency. Next to task-fidelity to the original environments and scalability, we position JAXtari as a benchmark for agent robustness. Thus, each game supports a set of pre-implemented modifications, which range from simple visual changes, such as altered colors or background elements, to more substantial gameplay variations. This allows evaluating not only base-task performance, but also the robustness of trained agents under controlled distribution shifts. Details of games and modifications are provided in Section F.3. 

3.1 Experimental Results JAXtari games achieve learning task parity with ALE (RQ1). A central design principle of JAXtari is prioritizing accelerator-native parallel throughput and transparent, source-level modification over bit-level, 1:1 ROM emulation. Exact cycle-level emulation of legacy Atari 2600 hardware (such as TIA register timings and 6502 assembly constraints) imposes rigid execution bottlenecks on GPUs and precludes clean Pythonic interventions on game semantics. Furthermore, deep RL policies are notoriously brittle; minor low-level implementation details or differing pseudo-random number generation prevent direct zero-shot policy transfer between independent simulators. Consequently, JAXtari is designed not as a drop-in emulator replacement, but to achieve behavioral and learning task parity with the ALE. Under this criterion, environments must present equivalent learning dynamics and exploration challenges: tasks solved rapidly in ALE ( e.g. Pong , Freeway ) are mastered similarly in JAXtari, while sparse-reward exploration bottlenecks ( e.g. Montezuma’s Revenge , Skiing ) remain equally challenging under standard algorithms. Moreover, agents discover and exploit identical behavioral shortcuts across both suites ( e.g. in Kangaroo and Bankheist ). As depicted in Figure 3, pixel-based PPO runs (orange vs. green curves) confirm this alignment: learning curves follow comparable trajectories with asymptotic returns in the same order of magnitude. JAXtari achieves significantly higher throughput, reducing the needed training time (RQ2). 

The scalability properties of the JAXtari-15 suite are validated against standard Gym-ALE, En-vPool (Weng et al., 2022) and ALE-VectorEnv, a recent EnvPool variation within the ALE. JAXtari-15 environments reach at least a throughput of 1M steps-per-second compared to a maximum of 360 kfor ALE-VectorEnv and a maximum of 280 k for EnvPool (see Figure 4 b). The JAXtari environments significantly outscale CPU-based ALE with a single GPU. Some lower-complexity games, e.g. ,pong or freeway, reach numbers exceeding 100 M steps per second when using OC observations (cf. Figure 4a). The observed variations in throughput across the JAXtari suite are attributable to heterogeneous visual and gameplay complexities, alongside implementation-specific nuances arising from our ground-up reimplementation of each game. In general, OC observations exhibit significantly higher scalability and throughput, which we attribute to the ability to execute the environment logic exclusively without rendering, significantly reducing memory usage and computational complexity. This is an advantage over ALE, whose emulator does not allow to skip rendering. Additionally, the smaller per-step memory footprint fits roughly 4× as many parallel environments on GPU as in the pixel-based setting. We also validate the utility of scaled-up JAXtari runs by performing PPO experiments with a large number of parallel environments (8192 OC, 2048 RGB, cf. Section E.1). For this, we follow the proposed benchmark detailed in Section 2.3 and perform zero-shot adaptation evaluation on each modification. The results, depicted in Figure 5, suggest that agents converged on the original games systematically lose performance under modifications, even on simplifications. Furthermore, JAXtari’s native object-centric state representations offer the structural scaffolding required to develop and evaluate neuro-symbolic methods—such as NUDGE (Delfosse et al., 2023), BlendRL (Shindo et al., 2025), and Kintsugi (Cao et al., 2026)—which explicitly ground policy learning in relational concepts and allow repairing brittle behavioral shortcuts. 4 Related Work 

JAXtari builds on three lines of RL research: Atari benchmarks, accelerator-native simulators, and generalization benchmarks. Prior work has improved Atari along individual axes such as speed, stochasticity, editability, or state access, but these capabilities have typically remained separate. 

The Arcade Learning Environment and its extensions. The ALE (Bellemare et al., 2013) remains the canonical Atari benchmark, and many extensions address specific limitations of the original setup. Sticky actions and action noise reduce determinism (Machado et al., 2018; Koch et al., 2021), while MinAtar (Young & Tian, 2019) provides simplified Atari-like games for faster experimentation. Other extensions preserve the Atari setting but target different bottlenecks: HackAtari (Delfosse et al., 2024a) enables semantic interventions through RAM manipulation, CuLE (Dalton & Frosio, 2020) and EnvPool (Weng et al., 2022) accelerate environment execution, and AtariARI (Anand et al., 2019) and OCAtari (Delfosse et al., 2024b) expose structured state on top of ALE. These systems expand Atari’s utility, but typically improve one axis at a time. JAXtari instead reimplements Atari-style games directly in JAX, making the environments simultaneously accelerator-native, semantically editable, pixel-compatible, and object-centric by design. Accelerator-native RL environments. Recent RL benchmarks increasingly move environment execution into JAX or GPU-native backends to reduce simulation bottlenecks and support large-scale vectorized training. Examples include 

Brax (Freeman et al., 2021) for continuous con-trol, Gymnax (Lange, 2026) for classic control and lightweight environments, Jumanji (Bonnet et al., 2024) for structured decision-making, Pgx (Koya-mada et al., 2023) for board games, and JAX-MARL (Rutherford et al., 2024) for multi-agent RL. Game-based suites such as Craftax (Matthews et al., 2024) and Octax (Radji et al., 2026) further show the value of scalable JAX-native simulation. These frameworks share JAXtari’s systems moti-vation, but target domains other than ALE-style Atari gameplay or simplified arcade settings. Atari remains a milestone benchmark because it com-bines visual input, long-horizon control, diverse game mechanics, and a long history of compara-ble baselines. Bringing this benchmark class into an accelerator-native, editable, and object-centric setting is therefore a necessary step for testing modern RL agents at Atari-like complexity. 

Generalization and Robustness in RL. Generalization remains a central challenge in RL: agents can achieve high returns in fixed environments while failing under small distribution shifts (Moos 8et al., 2022). Procedural benchmarks such as CoinRun (Cobbe et al., 2019) and Procgen (Cobbe et al., 2020) expose agents to diverse generated levels, while XLand-MiniGrid (Nikulin et al., 2023) and Craftax (Matthews et al., 2024) provide scalable task diversity. Another line improves ro-bustness through visual augmentation or representation learning, including RAD (Laskin et al., 2020), DrQ (Yarats et al., 2021), and the combination of pixel-based and object-centered representa-tions (Blüml et al., 2026) or environment perturbations (Gu et al., 2025). These approaches broadly improve or evaluate robustness, but do not isolate which semantic assumptions an agent has learned. Targeted semantic interventions address this diagnostic question more directly. HackAtari (Delfosse et al., 2024a) introduced controlled modifications to ALE games through RAM manipulation, and subsequent work showed that deep RL agents can fail even when task variants become easier for humans (Delfosse et al., 2025). JAXtari builds on this intervention-based view, but moves it from opaque RAM manipulation in a CPU-bound emulator to source-level modifications of JAX game logic. This makes counterfactual evaluation easier to implement, compositional across modifications, and scalable to large experimental sweeps. 

Object-centric representations and neuro-symbolic RL. Learning structured, object-level rep-resentations is an established paradigm for compositional generalization and sample efficiency in RL (Diuk et al., 2008; Garnelo et al., 2016). While prior Atari tools such as AtariARI (Anand et al., 2019) and OCAtari (Delfosse et al., 2024b) extracted object states post-hoc from emulator RAM, they remained constrained by CPU-bound stepping and lacked native integration with GPU training loops. Natively exposing object states unlocks methods that incorporate relational induc-tive biases and neuro-symbolic reasoning. Frameworks such as NUDGE (Delfosse et al., 2023) induce differentiable first-order logic rules over extracted entities, BlendRL (Shindo et al., 2025) unifies neural and symbolic policy learning, and Kintsugi (Cao et al., 2026) repairs misaligned or brittle policies by directly editing executable knowledge bases. Furthermore, structured object representations bridge the semantic gap to foundation models, enabling multimodal large language models and code-as-policy systems ( e.g. Atari-GPT (Waytowich et al., 2024), RL-GPT (Liu et al., 2024)) to interpret Atari scenes with human-like commonsense priors. JAXtari provides the first accelerator-native environment suite where such object-centric and neuro-symbolic architectures can be trained, verified, and stress-tested at modern GPU throughput. 

Positioning of JAXtari. While prior research has independently established the necessity of Atari as a reasoning benchmark, the scalability of accelerator-native simulation, and the diagnostic power of object-centric states and controlled interventions, these capabilities have largely remained fragmented across distinct frameworks. JAXtari resolves this fragmentation by fundamentally reconstructing Atari-style game logic directly in JAX, bypassing the severe bottlenecks of legacy CPU-bound emulation. Through this purely functional paradigm, agents can be trained from standard pixel renderings or evaluated using natively exposed object-centric features, effectively bridging the gap between deep and relational reinforcement learning. Furthermore, the framework leverages massive GPU parallelization for highly efficient scaling, and its modular API allows agents to be tested under direct, source-level semantic modifications—such as shifting dynamics, rules, or visual properties—without relying on complex and opaque RAM manipulation. Ultimately, this unification makes JAXtari a comprehensive benchmark substrate for studying not only how well agents perform, but precisely what causal structures or brittle heuristics they rely on when they succeed. 

5 Limitations and Future Work 

JAXtari is intended as a scalable and editable benchmark substrate for Atari-style RL, but it has limitations. First, reimplementing Atari games from scratch is substantially more labor-intensive than wrapping an existing emulator. As a result, the current release covers a subset of the full ALE suite rather than the typically used 57 games. We prioritize commonly used games and provide validation for the environments included in the benchmark, but the suite should not yet be interpreted as a complete replacement for ALE. Furthermore, JAXtari does not cover the ALE mode and difficulty switches of the original ROMs which alter core game parameters but instead relies on an independent modification system to introduce similar task-variance. Expanding coverage across the full Atari benchmark remains an important direction for future work. Second, JAXtari aims to reproduce ALE-style gameplay, but it is not an emulator and therefore cannot guarantee bit-level equivalence with the original Atari ROMs. Small divergences in timing, collision 9handling, object behavior, or rare edge cases may remain, in part due to differences in random number generation. We mitigate this by using visual comparisons, per-game divergence measurements, and downstream learning-curve comparisons, but users should interpret JAXtari as a high-fidelity reimplementation designed for scalable experimentation and controlled intervention, rather than a drop-in substitute for exact emulator-based evaluation. Third, the flexibility of the modification system introduces a benchmark-design challenge. Not every modification defines a meaningful generalization test: some preserve the task structure, while others may change the objective, difficulty, or optimal policy in ways that make comparisons ambiguous. For this reason, benchmark results should distinguish between visual shifts, dynamics shifts, reward shifts, simplifications, and more difficult out-of-distribution variants. Each existing modification is categorized by its intended semantic category and the expected effect. Next to increasing the number of available environments, future releases will include a larger curated modification library. Future work will focus on three directions. First, we plan to expand the number of supported games and broaden coverage of the ALE benchmark. Second, we will extend the library of validated semantic variations and integrate them with benchmark protocols for robustness, shortcut learning, controlled generalization and continual RL. Third, JAXtari’s explicit JAX state and source-level transition func-tions open opportunities for model-based RL, planning, world modeling, and curriculum generation. Although Atari’s discrete actions and collision-heavy dynamics limit straightforward gradient-based policy optimization, the JAX-native implementation makes it easier to inspect, transform, batch, and potentially differentiate through selected components of the environment. 

6 Conclusion 

We introduced JAXtari, a JAX-native framework and benchmark substrate for scalable, editable Atari-style reinforcement learning. By reimplementing game logic directly in JAX, JAXtari removes the CPU-bound emulator bottleneck of standard ALE workflows, enables accelerator-native vectorized execution, and exposes native object-centric state alongside pixel observations. Its source-level modification system further enables controlled semantic variations, allowing agents trained on standard Atari-style tasks to be evaluated under targeted visual, dynamical, reward-level, and object-level shifts. Our results show that JAXtari preserves ALE-style learning behavior while providing substantially higher throughput and making counterfactual evaluation practical at scale. Together, these properties make JAXtari a benchmark substrate for studying not only how well RL agents perform, but also whether their behavior reflects robust task understanding or brittle shortcut reliance. By reducing experiments that previously required many hours of emulator-bound execution to substantially shorter accelerator-native runs, JAXtari makes systematic robustness evaluation over many agents, seeds, and environment variants substantially more accessible. 

Broader Impact. JAXtari is an open-source benchmark substrate for academic reinforcement learning research. Its primary positive impact is democratizing large-scale rl experimentation: by reducing the compute required to run meaningful experiments from many CPU-hours to short GPU-native runs, it lowers the barrier for researchers. The modification system additionally makes systematic robustness and shortcut-learning evaluation accessible, which we expect to benefit the broader community studying agent generalization. We do not foresee significant negative societal impacts; JAXtari releases no pretrained agents, collects no user data, and involves no human subjects.

Reimplementing Atari 2600 game mechanics from scratch in a pure-JAX functional paradigm requires rigorous quality control to ensure learning task parity with the original Arcade Learning Environment (ALE) (Bellemare et al., 2013). Because Atari ROMs were originally written in 6502 assembly with tight hardware constraints (Television Interface Adapter / TIA clock timings), subtle gameplay mechanics and edge cases can easily be missed in high-level reimplementations. To systematically prevent behavioral discrepancies, we established a four-stage Human + Agentic validation pipeline combining autonomous specification extraction, automated code auditing, human-in-the-loop verification, and empirical policy stress-testing. 

B.1 The Four-Stage Validation Protocol 

1. Stage 1: Autonomous Specification Extraction (Agentic). For every environment, an LLM agent extracts an exhaustive ground-truth operational specification by reading official docu-mentation from the Farama Foundation ( ale.farama.org ) and canonical AtariAge manuals (atariage.com ). This structured specification document formalizes: • Action Space: The active subset of the 18 discrete Atari actions, identifying which inputs induce positional movement, fire projectiles, or trigger special mechanics. • Game Mechanics: Complete entity lifecycle, including spawn conditions, directional velocity, screen boundaries, multi-level progression, and collision consequences. • Reward Structure and Termination: Explicit scoring criteria (points per entity hit or col-lectable picked up), milestone bonuses, life counters, and game-over triggers. 2. Stage 2: Code Mapping and Gap Auditing (Agentic). A secondary auditing LLM agent parses the JAX implementation against the generated specification: • Recall Audit: Maps each specified game mechanic and scoring rule to exact line numbers and function definitions in the Python/JAX source code, ensuring that no specified rule was omitted. • Precision Audit: Examines all unmapped code paths to identify undocumented logic, hard-coded constants, or custom mechanics not found in official manuals. 3. Stage 3: Dual Ground-Truth Verification (Human-in-the-Loop). Human expert verifiers evaluate the audit outputs along two complementary dimensions: • Recall Verification: Experts review the mapped code blocks to confirm that the mathematical dynamics and coordinate transforms accurately match Atari mechanics. • Precision Verification: For any undocumented code behavior flagged in the precision audit, human verifiers play the original ALE ROM side-by-side with the JAXtari environment. This step confirms whether the logic reflects an authentic assembly-level edge case of the original game or represents an unintended implementation artifact. 4. Stage 4: Empirical Policy Stress-Testing. Finally, standard RL agents (CleanRL PPO) are trained on both JAXtari and ALE under identical hyperparameters. Learning curve trajectories, asymptotic returns, and exploration rates are compared to guarantee that the JAX environment presents an equivalent learning challenge and does not permit unintended shortcut strategies.

We assess agent performance and framework efficiency using common metrics designed to provide statistically rigorous insights into learning dynamics and hardware utilization. 

Reliable Performance Aggregation. To mitigate the impact of outliers and account for the high variance inherent in RL training, we adopt the statistical framework proposed by rliable (Agarwal et al., 2021). We aggregate final performance using the Interquartile Mean (IQM), which computes the mean over the middle 50% of the data distribution (i.e., the 25th to 75th percentiles). This provides a more robust estimate of central tendency than the grand mean or median. Furthermore, we report 95% stratified bootstrap confidence intervals to quantify uncertainty. For longitudinal analysis, learning curves represent the mean in-game rewards accompanied by the standard deviation across 3 independent seeds. 

Performance Change ( PC ). To quantify the impact of our environmental perturbations on agent proficiency, we define the Performance Change metric Delfosse et al. (2025). This metric assesses the relative shift in performance when an agent trained on a standard environment is evaluated on a modified variant. It is formulated as: 

PC = (Smod − Rmod ) − (Sorg − Rorg )

|Sorg − Rorg | (1) where Smod is the score in the modified environment, Sorg is the score in the original environment, and R the random baselines. A value of PC = 0 indicates perfect performance invariance, while PC < 0 signifies a collapse in the perturbation. 

Throughput and Hardware Efficiency. The computational utility of JAXtari is quantified via environmental throughput, defined as the total number of frames processed per second across all parallel environments. A primary architectural objective of our JAX-native implementation is to maximize hardware efficiency, targeting 100% GPU utilization by eliminating CPU-GPU communication bottlenecks that typically constrain traditional emulators. To provide a transparent account of the computational costs associated with our benchmarks, we track and report total GPU hours. Also, all experiments were done on a single A100 GPU, while scaling with multiple GPUs is possible. 19 D Agent Architectures and Training Setup 

We evaluate the JAXtari-15 environments on PPO (Proximal Policy Optimization) with both pixel-based and object-centric observation types. Whenever possible the implementations are JAX-based for maximum performance (jitted and scanned stepping through the environment). Note that JAXtari can also be employed with non JAX implementations, e.g., pytorch or tensorflow (using DLPack 2

for zero-copy tensor transfer). We also provide a gym-wrapper for maximum compatibility. The network architecture and hyperparameters are adapted from the same sources as the baselines we compare JAXtari’s environments against to ensure that discrepancies in agent performance can, with high certainty, be attributed to environment differences and not the training setup. Therefore PPO is adapted from CleanRL (Huang et al., 2022). 

D.1 Baseline environments: ALE, EnvPool and ALE-VectorEnv 

We evaluate our environments against the ALE (Bellemare et al., 2013) which serves as the standard CPU-based framework for running Atari 2600 games as RL environments. However, the ALE executes environments sequentially, incurring throughput bottlenecks. Since our environments are highly parallelizable, we also evaluate throughput against EnvPool (Weng et al., 2022) and ALE-VectorEnv. Both are high-performance, native C++ multi-threaded implementations for running Atari2600 games. Unfortunately, CuLE (Dalton & Frosio, 2020) is currently unmaintained. We made extensive efforts to compile and evaluate the framework locally; however, following the official installation instructions did not result in usable states. Reproducing the environment on modern hardware proved unfeasible due to three well-documented technical blockers: (1) strict dependencies on deeply deprecated software stacks, specifically CUDA 10.0 and PyTorch 1.2 3, (2) fatal compilation failures when building the C++ source with modern GCC compilers or the provided Docker containers 4, and (3) unresolved assertion crashes during initialization on multi-GPU systems for users who manage to bypass the build errors 5. Because we could not establish a reliable, non-deprecated baseline for this hardware-accelerated framework, we focus our throughput evaluations on the ALE, EnvPool and ALE-VectorEnv.

D.2.1 Native Downscaling 

The aforementioned native downscaling is a feature of JAXtari’s rendering approach. Since the rendering is not directly tied to the game logic, but a separate computational step that constructs the image from a given state some preprocessing operations can be performed directly inside the renderer instead of afterwards on the finished image. Concretely, rather than rendering the full 210x160 pixel image, the renderer scales down all sprites at initialization and construct the image at the target resolution. This reduces memory consumption and increases throughput significantly, as operating on smaller images lowers the computational cost. 

D.3 Network Architecture D.3.1 Pixel-Based Architectures 

For pixel-based observations, PPO uses a standard DQN-like convolutional neural network (Mnih et al., 2015) consisting of three convolutional layers followed by a fully connected layer: • Conv1: 32 filters, 8 × 8 kernel, stride 4, ReLU • Conv2: 64 filters, 4 × 4 kernel, stride 2, ReLU • Conv3: 64 filters, 3 × 3 kernel, stride 1, ReLU • FC: 512 units, ReLU 

D.3.2 Object-Centric Architecture 

For object-centric observations the agents use a fully-connected architecture instead of convolutional layers used for pixel-based observations. For PPO, we use 461 nodes in the first layer and 512 in the second. The first layer size is specifically chosen to match the convolutional layer’s parameter count, enabling a fair comparison between the two input variants. Separate linear actor and critic heads are applied to the output. 21 D.4 Algorithm Details D.4.1 PPO

Does More Training Time Yield Better Perfor-mance? Whether extended training budgets translate into improved agent performance is not a question with a universal answer. On a sub-stantial fraction of ALE titles, learning curves saturate well before the 100 M-step mark, and additional environment interactions yield dimin-ishing, or even negligible, returns. However, this picture changes markedly for the subset of games that are notoriously difficult to solve or converge upon, most prominently Seaquest and 

Montezuma’s Revenge . These environments are characterized by sparse, delayed, or hierarchi-cally structured rewards, which require the agent to execute long sequences of coordinated actions before any learning signal is produced. In such regimes, a longer training horizon directly en-larges the agent’s effective exploration budget: it increases the probability of stumbling upon rare reward-bearing trajectories, allows replay buffers to accumulate a more diverse set of tran-sitions, and gives policy- and value-estimates additional time to escape local optima. Empiri-cally, this manifests as higher final scores and qualitatively richer behaviors that simply do not emerge under shorter budgets. Our results in Figure 10 show that, with a training budget of 100M (or just 43min), we can achieve very high scores. The throughput characterized in the preceding section opens a concrete avenue for future investigation. Because JAXtari renders 100 M-step (and beyond) training runs tractable on a single GPU, it becomes feasible to systematically revisit the hard-exploration subset of the ALE under substantially extended compute budgets, 200 M, 500 M, or even 1B steps, without resorting to large-scale distributed infras-tructure. We see several promising directions enabled by this regime, which can be found in our future work section.

F.2 Creating new Modifications 

JAXtari features a modular modification system that allows users to edit game semantics directly at the source-code level (as described in Section 2). Modifications are built around the JaxtariInternalModPlugin and 

JaxtariPostStepModPlugin . When creating a new mod, you can intervene at two distinct points in the environment’s lifecycle: • Internal Modifications: These interventions are applied before Just-In-Time (JIT) compi-lation. Because they become part of the compiled transition function, they execute highly efficiently. Use this method to override core game constants, collision rules, environment methods, enemy speeds, or object spawning behaviors. You can also use the declarative asset system here to swap visual sprites by overriding values in the environment’s original 

ASSET_CONFIG .• Post-Step Modifications: These interventions operate on the environment state after the original transition function has evaluated. They are ideal for changes that do not require rewriting the core game dynamics, such as relocating an object, masking a state variable, changing a reward, or triggering an alternative episode termination. Once your modification is defined, you can inject it directly during environment initialization by pass-ing it to the mods argument: env = jaxtari.make("game_name", mods=["custom_mod"]) .
