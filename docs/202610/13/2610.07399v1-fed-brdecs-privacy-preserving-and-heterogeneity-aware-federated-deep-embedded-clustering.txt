Abstract 

Federated deep clustering seeks to learn clustering-friendly representations from decentral-ized unlabeled data while preserving client privacy. However, Deep Embedded Clustering (DEC)-style objectives depend on global soft-assignment statistics that require clients to reveal their sensitive information. We propose Fed-BRDECS, a privacy-preserving and heterogeneity-aware federated deep embedded clustering framework. Fed-BRDECS replaces the globally normalized clustering objective with a locally computable sample-stability loss, avoiding the transmission of local soft-assignment distributions. To tackle non-IID client distributions, we introduce prediction-balanced sampling, which oversamples locally rare predicted clusters with-out requiring ground-truth labels, and centroid-level restarting, which periodically refreshes biased or inactive centroids. Experiments on image and text clustering benchmarks show that Fed-BRDECS consistently outperforms representative federated clustering and deep clustering baselines under both IID and non-IID partitions. We further demonstrate its applicability to federated time-series anomaly detection, where it improves reconstruction-based detectors with-out adding inference-time cost. 

# 1 INTRODUCTION 

Clustering is a fundamental unsupervised learning task, but in many applications data are dis-tributed across institutions or devices and cannot be centrally collected due to privacy and owner-ship constraints (Jain, 2010; Rieke et al., 2020; Nguyen et al., 2021; Pfeifer et al., 2024; Presotto et al., 2022). Federated learning (FL) provides a natural paradigm for this setting by enabling multiple clients to collaboratively train a model while keeping raw data local (McMahan et al., 2017). Extending clustering to the federated setting is therefore important for privacy-preserving unsupervised representation learning, where labels are unavailable and data heterogeneity is often severe (Dennis et al., 2021; Garst and Reinders, 2025). However, directly applying centralized deep clustering methods to FL is nontrivial. DEC (Xie et al., 2016) and its improved variant IDEC (Guo et al., 2017) are the dominant modern approaches to deep clustering, especially in the centralized regime. After autoencoder pre-training, they jointly refine latent representations and cluster assignments using soft assignments and an auxiliary target distribution. In FL, this target requires dataset-level assignment frequen-cies, and reproducing it requires either exchanging local cluster statistics, which exposes client composition, or constructing targets from incomplete local views, which can bias optimization under heterogeneity. 1arXiv:2610.07399v1 [cs.LG] 5 Oct 2026 Heterogeneity can further lead to biased centroid updates and inactive clusters (Dennis et al., 2021; Pan et al., 2023). For example, cluster-frequency-based objectives may over-emphasize locally dominant clusters and under-train rare clusters. Federated clustering can also suffer from cluster collapse and early saturation. Once the encoder and centroids become aligned with an imperfect early partition, subsequent training may reinforce the same assignments rather than correct them. Recent work identifies this phenomenon as a reclustering barrier (Miklautz et al., 2025). In FL, this early saturation can be further amplified because client updates are computed from partial, heterogeneous views and then averaged at the server. We propose Fed-BRDECS (Federated Balanced-Sampling and Restarted Deep Embedded Clus-tering with Sample Stability), a federated deep clustering framework with three components. These enhancements address the privacy issues inherent in directly applying DEC to FL, while simulta-neously tackling the core challenges of federated deep clustering. First, it replaces the globally normalized IDEC clustering objective with a locally computable sample-stability loss inspired by DECS (Cheng et al., 2024). Since this objective is computed using only local samples and current centroids, clients do not need to transmit sensitive soft-assignment distributions. Second, to mitigate local imbalance under heterogeneous FL, it uses a prediction-based balanced sampler that oversamples each client’s locally rare predicted clusters using the model’s own assignments. This acts as an unsupervised form of class-balanced importance sampling. Third, periodic federated restarting recomputes centroids in the current embedding space to recover collapsed clusters. DEC has several applications, with outlier detection being one of the most common. We use Fed-BRDECS as the building block for federated outlier detection and validate on time-series data from both public benchmarks and a proprietary sensor dataset. Since many detectors are built on deep embedded clustering structures (Zong et al., 2018; Obied et al., 2023), our framework can be easily applied to DEC-based or AE-based federated time-series anomaly detection algorithms. This demonstrates that the proposed framework is not limited to standard clustering benchmarks, but can also serve as a privacy-preserving representation-learning backbone for downstream unsuper-vised tasks. Our contributions are summarized as follows. 1. We propose Fed-BRDECS , a federated deep embedded clustering framework for hetero-geneous unsupervised federated learning. Unlike prior federated or distributed clustering methods that exchange local clustering information, Fed-BRDECS enables IDEC-style joint representation and clustering refinement using a locally computable sample-stability loss with-out transmitting local assignment statistics. 2. We introduce two mechanisms to improve federated deep clustering under heterogeneous client distributions: a prediction-based balanced sampler that mitigates local cluster imbalance using pseudo-labels, and an iterative restarting strategy that refreshes centroids to reduce early commitment and cluster collapse. 3. We demonstrate consistent empirical improvements over representative federated clustering and deep clustering baselines, with ablation studies validating the contribution of stability loss, balanced sampling, and restarting. 4. We evaluate Fed-BRDECS on federated time-series anomaly detection as a practical stress test for unsupervised federated representation learning, showing that the proposed framework can improve reconstruction-based detectors under heterogeneous client time-series distributions without adding inference-time cost. 2Table 1: Comparison with representative federated clustering methods. # 2 RELATED WORK 

Deep clustering. DEC (Xie et al., 2016) is a pioneering work showing that a representation can be learned directly from a clustering objective. IDEC (Guo et al., 2017) adds a reconstruc-tion term to preserve local structure, and DCN (Yang et al., 2017) similarly connects represen-tation learning with k-means style clustering. A common limitation of DEC-based methods is their reliance on manually constructed pseudo-targets, which may reinforce early assignment errors and depend on dataset-level assignment statistics. DECS (Cheng et al., 2024) replaces pseudo-targets with sample-stability optimization, which we adopt to eliminate global assignment statistics. BRB (Miklautz et al., 2025) addresses early saturation through reclustering and soft parameter re-sets. Fed-BRDECS adapts these ideas to heterogeneous FL using local stability optimization and centroid-level restarting without parameter resets. 

Federated clustering. Federated clustering has mostly focused on centroid-style algorithms over fixed features rather than deep representation learning. k-FED (Dennis et al., 2021) is a one-shot method in which each client uploads its local cluster centers only once. FKM (Garst and Reinders, 2025) extends this paradigm to multiple communication rounds. Other methods address specific limitations of federated k-means: DWF k-Means (Holzer et al., 2023) introduces dynamic weighting, Fork (Bravo and Dalleiger, 2026) improves robustness, and FedDP k-Means (Scott et al., 2025) incorporates differential privacy. In contrast to these hard-assignment approaches, FFCM (Stallmann and Wilbik, 2022) adapts fuzzy c-means to federated learning, allowing samples to have soft membership across multiple clusters. However, these methods still perform centroid-based clustering on raw or precomputed features rather than jointly learning deep representations, and they also exchange explicit local cluster statistics. More recently, efforts have emerged to apply deep clustering to FL. F-DEC (Mashhadi et al., 2021) directly applies DEC to FL by optimizing a KL-based clustering loss across clients, and FDEC (Xu et al., 2025) attempts to achieve robustness through hybrid federated averaging. CCFC (Liu et al., 2024) incorporates a cluster-contrastive loss to learn clustering-friendly representations. Still, these deep frameworks do not explicitly resolve core FL challenges such as centroid collapse, data heterogeneity, and privacy leakage tied to transmitting local soft assignment statistics. Table 1 summarizes these differences. 

Imbalance in federated and unsupervised learning. Heterogeneity and imbalance are cen-tral challenges in FL. Supervised FL methods commonly address them through local data rebal-ancing, loss reweighting, or aggregation adjustment (Wang et al., 2021; Seol and Kim, 2023; Chung and Lee, 2025; Park et al., 2026). However, these methods rely on labels or label-distribution infor-3mation, which are unavailable in unsupervised federated clustering. In unlabeled settings, related works—particularly in centralized environments—utilize inferred groups or pseudo-clusters to mit-igate imbalance. For instance, some approaches balance unlabeled datasets using cluster IDs as pseudo-classes, while others partition majority classes into subclusters for imbalanced classification (Suayama and Nakamura, 2025; Lim, 2024). We adapt this concept to federated clustering. How-ever, rather than treating predicted cluster assignments as supervised targets, we use them strictly to define a local sampling distribution paired with an oversampling strategy.

3.4 Theoretical Analysis 

A privacy-aware alternative to the globally normalized DEC target is to let each client reconstruct the target using only its local soft cluster frequencies. Under heterogeneous client cluster propor-tions, however, this local reconstruction is not equivalent to the centralized DEC target and can even reverse the relative preference between two clusters. Appendix A.2 formalizes this effect and bounds both the target distortion and the resulting clustering-gradient bias. Thus, replacing the global DEC target with a locally computable stability objective avoids this source of heterogeneity-induced target mismatch. The local stability objective avoids this dependence on global frequencies fj in (3), but it con-tains no explicit cluster-frequency balancing term. Consequently, centroids may become inactive, as formalized in Appendix A.3. Federated partitioning. All experiments are conducted with K = 10 clients. For each dataset, we evaluate two federation settings. In the IID setting, samples are uniformly distributed across clients. In the non-IID setting, client data distributions are generated using a Dirichlet partition with concentration parameter α = 0 .5, as described in Hsu et al. (2019). The non-IID setting tests whether each method can learn meaningful global clusters when clients observe different local cluster proportions. 

Baselines. We compare Fed-BRDECS with three groups of baselines. The first group consists of federated centroid clustering methods over fixed features: k-FED (Dennis et al., 2021), FKM (Garst and Reinders, 2025), and DWF k-Means (Holzer et al., 2023). These methods do not learn deep representations. The second group consists of deep federated clustering baselines. Fed-AE first trains an au-toencoder in a federated manner and then applies k-FED to the learned embeddings, isolating the effect of representation pretraining without joint clustering refinement. F-DEC (Mashhadi et al., 2021) is a direct federated implementation of IDEC, where clients optimize IDEC reconstruction and the KL clustering objective under FedAvg. This baseline uses the same autoencoder backbone but retains the DEC/IDEC target distribution objective and, therefore, requires exchange of local clustering information to construct the global target distribution. CCFC (Liu et al., 2024) is a recent contrastive federated deep clustering method. The third group consists of our ablation variants. Fed-DECS uses only the local sample-stability loss, corresponding to the stability component (denoted as S). Fed-BDECS adds prediction-balanced sampling ( S+B). Fed-BRDECS is the full method with sample stability, prediction-balanced sampling, and centroid-level restarting ( S+B+R). We also report Fed-BRIDEC, which applies balanced sampling and restarting to the IDEC-style objective, to separate the effect of our stability objective from the effect of the balancing and restarting mechanisms. 4.2 Clustering Results 

In Figure 1, results are shown as percentage-point improvements over the worst-performing method for each dataset, partition, and metric. Fed-BRDECS attains the highest ACC in all eight settings. The corresponding absolute ACC and NMI values are reported in Appendix C.3. Overall, Fed-BRDECS achieves the strongest and most consistent clustering performance across datasets and partition settings. Its advantage is particularly evident under non-IID distributions, indicating improved robustness to heterogeneous client data. 

Effect of representation learning. The fixed-feature federated k-means methods are consis-tently weaker than deep clustering methods on most benchmarks (Figure 1). For example, on MNIST-IID, the best fixed-feature baseline obtains 0 .4914, whereas Fed-AE reaches 0 .8155. This confirms that learning a clustering-friendly representation remains crucial in federated clustering. However, Fed-AE is still weaker than Fed-BRDECS, showing that autoencoder pretraining alone is insufficient without clustering-aware refinement. In several settings, particularly under non-IID partitions, F-DEC also underperforms Fed-AE, indicating that DEC-style refinement can degrade the pretrained representation. 

Effectiveness of proposed components. F-DEC is a direct federated implementation of the IDEC objective. Fed-BRDECS improves over F-DEC by 6.47 percentage points in average ACC across the eight settings, with especially large gains under non-IID partitions. Further, the ablation 10 ladder Fed-DECS → Fed-BDECS → Fed-BRDECS shows the contribution of each component. Fed-DECS, which uses only the stability loss, already performs competitively with F-DEC while avoiding the transmission of local cluster-frequency statistics. Adding prediction-balanced sampling and centroid-level restarting further improves ACC, indicating that our B+R components help correct biased or inactive centroids during federated refinement. 

Robustness to heterogeneity. Fed-BRDECS is particularly effective under non-IID partitions. Averaged over the four non-IID settings, Fed-BRDECS obtains 0 .6889 ACC, outperforming other al-gorithms. This suggests that the combination of prediction-balanced sampling and centroid restart-ing improves robustness to heterogeneous local cluster proportions. 

Embedding visualization. Figure 2 visualizes the learned MNIST embeddings using t-SNE. Compared with F-DEC, Fed-BRDECS mitigates cluster collapse and cluster mix-up. The im-provement is especially visible under the non-IID partition, where the baseline exhibits stronger cluster mixing, whereas Fed-BRDECS progressively separates clusters through prediction-balanced refinement and centroid-level restarting.

4.3 Application to Federated Time-Series Anomaly Detection 

Beyond standard clustering benchmarks, we evaluate Fed-BRDECS in federated time-series anomaly detection (TSAD), which provides a practical stress test for unsupervised federated representation learning. Many TSAD methods are reconstruction based. A model is trained to reconstruct normal time series, and anomalous time series are identified by a large reconstruction error (Malhotra et al., 2016; Audibert et al., 2020; Tuli et al., 2022). In federated TSAD, different clients may correspond to different machines or operating environments, so normal patterns can be highly heterogeneous. As a result, a single reconstruction model trained by FedAvg may underrepresent rare normal regimes and produce false alarms. 

Applying Fed-BRDECS to reconstruction-based TSAD. Fed-BRDECS can be applied to reconstruction-based TSAD backbones as a training-time module. If the detector already in-cludes a latent clustering component, we directly replace its clustering objective with our sample-stability loss and add prediction-balanced sampling and centroid restarting. If the detector is purely reconstruction-based, we attach a temporary projection head and centroids to the latent represen-tation, optimize the sample-stability objective jointly with reconstruction, and use the resulting pseudo-clusters for balanced sampling. We instantiate this mechanism on LSTM-AE (Garg et al., 2022) and TranAD (Tuli et al., 2022) for regularly sampled public benchmarks, attaching the clustering head to the encoder final hidden state and to the latent representation used by the reconstruction module, respectively. The pro-prietary sensor data are irregularly sampled multivariate time series, so we use CoFormer-AE (Wei et al., 2023), which replaces the LSTM encoder with CoFormer; TranAD and other architecture-specific TSAD variants are not directly compatible with this backbone. # 5 CONCLUSION 

We presented Fed-BRDECS, a federated deep embedded clustering framework that replaces the globally normalized IDEC target with a locally computable sample-stability loss, so that clients do not transmit soft-assignment or cluster-frequency statistics during refinement, and that coun-ters local imbalance and centroid collapse through prediction-balanced sampling and centroid-level restarting. Fed-BRDECS attains the highest ACC in all eight federated clustering settings, and it also serves as a training-time module for downstream unsupervised monitoring, improving reconstruction-based anomaly detectors without changing their inference procedure. It does not provide formal differential privacy, since model updates and k-FED centroid means are still com-municated (Appendix B.2); adding formal privacy mechanisms for these centroids is a natural next step.

B.1 k-FED Centroid Initialization and Restarting 

Fed-BRDECS uses k-FED (Dennis et al., 2021) for both initial centroid construction and periodic centroid restarting. We use k-FED because it constructs global centroids from uploaded local cluster means without requiring clients to transmit local soft assignment statistics or cluster-count statistics, which may reveal sensitive information about client-specific data composition. After federated autoencoder pretraining, each client embeds its local data using the current encoder and solves a local clustering subproblem in the latent space. Following k-FED, the client sends only the resulting local cluster means to the server without sending cluster assignment statis-tics. The server then applies the k-FED server-side aggregation procedure to the collection of uploaded local means to obtain the global centroids. This procedure replaces the centralized k-means initialization used in DEC/IDEC with a one-shot federated centroid construction step. The same procedure is used during restarting. At the beginning of each restart iteration, the server does not reset or perturb the encoder or decoder parameters as in BRB (Miklautz et al., 2025). Instead, clients recompute local cluster means from their current embeddings, and the server reruns the k-FED aggregation step to refresh the global centroids. The model parameters are kept unchanged across restarts, while the centroids are relocated according to the current federated embedding geometry. This design allows Fed-BRDECS to correct biased or inactive centroids induced by heterogeneous client updates without disrupting the learned representation. Fed-BRDECS is privacy-preserving in the specific sense of reducing local assignment-statistic disclo-sure. In DEC/IDEC-style federated refinement, local soft-assignment matrices, soft-count vectors, or cluster-frequency statistics directly encode client-specific cluster composition. Such composition information can be sensitive in practical applications, for example when local clusters correspond to defect types, disease subpopulations, or machine operating regimes. Fed-BRDECS does not provide formal differential privacy. Like other prototype-based federated clustering methods, it still communicates model updates and local centroid means during k-FED initialization and restarting. However, during clustering refinement, Fed-BRDECS avoids repeated transmission of local soft assignments, soft-count vectors, and predicted-cluster histograms. Ex-isting work (Scott et al., 2025) applies differential privacy to mitigate this leakage, but DP-noised cluster counts may still reveal client-level composition. Appendix B.3 further discusses why adding DP noise to cluster counts may fail to hide local composition unless the noise magnitude is large enough to substantially degrade the utility of the statistics.

Image datasets. For MNIST (Deng, 2012), Fashion-MNIST (Xiao et al., 2017), and KMNIST (Clanuwat et al., 2018), we use the official test split of each dataset. C.2 Implementation Details 

For centroid-based baselines, including k-FED, FKM, and DWF k-Means, we apply the algorithms directly to the raw input features, following the evaluation setting of the original federated clus-tering papers. Thus, for image datasets, these methods operate on flattened raw pixels, while for REUTERS-10K they operate on TF–IDF features. Deep clustering methods instead learn an autoencoder representation before or during clustering refinement. All federated deep clustering methods use FedAvg as the federated optimization framework. For methods incorporating the sam-ple stability loss or the balanced sampler, each client updates the sampling weights or calculates the Otsu threshold τk once per epoch across its entire local dataset. 

Selection of loss weights. We examine the sensitivity of Fed-BRDECS to the clustering-loss weight λstab on MNIST and Fashion-MNIST. As shown in Figure 3, the preferred weight differs between the IID and non-IID settings. We use λstab = 1 .0 for IID partitions, which provides near-optimal performance on both datasets, and λstab = 0 .5 for non-IID partitions, which achieves the highest ACC on both datasets. These values are used for the corresponding partition settings in all main experiments. We set λvar = 0 .8, following Cheng et al. (2024). 

Figure 3: Sensitivity of Fed-BRDECS to the clustering-loss weight λstab on Fashion-MNIST and MNIST. 

Optimization details. Local client optimization uses SGD with momentum. We use a cosine annealing learning-rate scheduler for both the pretraining and refinement stages. The scheduler de-cays the learning rate from the selected initial value to 10 −3 times the initial value by the final round. For refinement with multiple restarts, the scheduler is applied over the entire refinement process 26 rather than being reset at each restart. We tune weight decay over {10 −4, 10 −5, 0} and batch size over {64 , 128 , 256 }. The initial learning rate is selected by grid search from {1.0, 0.2, 0.1, 0.02 , 0.01 }.For local training, each client performs 20 local optimization steps per communication round. All experiments are implemented in PyTorch 2.1.0 with CUDA 12.6. Jobs are executed on four NVIDIA GeForce RTX 2080 Ti GPUs.

# D ADDITIONAL DETAILS FOR FEDERATED TIME-SERIES ANOMALY DETECTION 

D.1 Related Work 

Centralized TSAD spans deep one-class methods (Deep SVDD (Ruff et al., 2018)), reconstruction and forecasting methods (LSTM-AE (Malhotra et al., 2016), OmniAnomaly (Su et al., 2019), USAD (Audibert et al., 2020)), graph-based methods (GDN (Deng and Hooi, 2021)), and transformer-based detectors (TranAD (Tuli et al., 2022)). A growing thread also builds detectors on deep embedded clustering itself, including DTC (Madiraju et al., 2018) and DCLOP (Obied et al., 2023). Such DEC-based detectors are common in centralized learning but scarce in federated learning, where FedTADBench (Liu et al., 2022) evaluates representative TSAD algorithms under federated learning. Fed-BRDECS fills this gap as a federated deep clustering backbone for unsupervised outlier detection, whose mechanism can be easily plugged into existing federated TSAD algorithms.
