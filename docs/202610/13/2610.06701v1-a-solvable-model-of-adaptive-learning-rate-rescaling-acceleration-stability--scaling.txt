## A Solvable Model of Adaptive Learning Rate Rescaling: Acceleration, Stability & Scaling

A recurring design principle in modern optimizers is to decouple update magnitude from the raw gradient norm, yet its consequences for learning-curve and resource scaling remain unclear. We isolate this mechanism by studying normalized SGD in a random-feature model with power-law teacher and data covariance. Fixed-norm updates induce an effective learning rate that grows as gradients shrink. We derive a dynamical mean-field theory (DMFT) describing the joint dependence of the loss on training time, model width and batch size. Normalization initially accelerates SGD, mapping the power-law exponent rSGD < 1 to 2 rSGD /(1 − rSGD ), with exponential convergence at 

rSGD = 1 and formal finite-time convergence for rSGD > 1. At finite step size, however, the same feedback ultimately breaks the acceleration and leads to marginal stability. The late-time theory yields width-limited, edge-of-stochastic-stability (EoSS), and deterministic edge-of-stability (EoS) regimes. These phases determine when larger batches or wider models reduce serial training time at comparable compute. We quantify in which of these phases increased batch size or width can compensate the excess compute use per step by fewer optimization steps to target loss. Linearized ResNet experiments on CIFAR-5M support the predicted acceleration, breakdown, and resource-scaling trends. Together, these results connect normalization-induced acceleration, EoS effects, and width–batch allocation within a solvable theory. 

I. INTRODUCTION 

Optimization governs how efficiently neural networks convert training compute into improved performance, yet its role in shaping neural scaling laws remains incompletely understood. These laws describe how performance improves with training time, model size, and data, providing a quantitative basis for allocating compute across these resources [1, 2]. Several theoretical studies connect these laws to the spectral structure of data and tasks: learning proceeds progressively through the data spectrum, while finite training time, model size, and data impose different spectral cutoffs [3–7]. Only recently have studies begun to examine how these spectral learning dynamics interact with optimizer design [8, 9], and many aspects of this interaction remain poorly understood. Modern adaptive optimizers such as RMSProp, Adam, SOAP, and Muon induce trajectory-dependent effective learning rates through gradient-dependent preconditioning, even when the base learning rate is fixed [10–13]. This rescaling changes both update direction and magnitude; grafting makes their distinct roles explicit by combining the direction of one optimizer with the magnitude of another [14]. We isolate the feedback between update magnitude and learning dynamics by studying normalized SGD, which preserves the stochastic-gradient direction while fixing the update norm. With the normalization used below, this is equivalent to SGD with an endogenous scalar learning rate ηeff (t) = η / |g(t)∥2, providing a tractable setting for studying how adaptive rescaling affects convergence and stability. Gradient normalization creates a tension between acceleration and stability. As progressively weaker spectral modes are learned, the gradient norm decays and the effective learning rate grows, potentially accelerating progress through the spectral tail. At finite step size, however, the same growth eventually brings the leading spectral mode to the discrete-time stability threshold. The deterministic edge-of-stability (EoS) corresponds to marginal stability of the leading Hessian mode [15], while the edge-of-stochastic-stability (EoSS) is associated with a stability limit imposed by minibatch fluctuations [16]. The spectral tail therefore controls the transient acceleration, while the spectral edge controls its breakdown. These stability limits also shape the computational value of increasing training resources. A larger batch can support a larger effective learning rate, but each update is more expensive [17–20]; increasing width changes both approximation capacity and the spectrum that controls stability. Our main contributions are: 

• We derive a dynamical mean field theory (DMFT) for normalized SGD in a high-dimensional power-law random-feature model, characterizing the joint scaling of the loss with training time, model width, batch size, and finite step size. 

• We characterize source- and capacity-dependent transient scaling of norm-SGD: normalization accelerates the SGD exponent rSGD to 2 rSGD /(1 − rSGD ) for rSGD < 1, while rSGD = 1 and rSGD > 1 yield exponential and finite-time convergence, respectively. 

• DMFT yields a spectrum-dependent width-batch phase diagram: in overparameterized models, a width-dependent critical batch separates stochastic edge-of-stability (EoSS) from deterministic edge-of-stability (EoS), whereas underparameterized models are limited by finite-width error. 

• We characterize compute–speed trade-offs across batch size and width: in the EoSS phase, a nearly linear batch-scaling regime reduces the steps to a target loss at almost unchanged compute. In the width-limited phase, we identify spectral regimes where the stationary effective learning rate grows superlinearly with width, giving rise to potential width speedups. 

• We test the theory using linearized ResNets on CIFAR-5M. Measured kernel and target exponents yield predictions consistent with the accelerated loss decay and its crossover toward SGD-like scaling, while batch and width sweeps support the predicted efficiency trends in the explored regime. We use norm-SGD to isolate how the feedback between gradient normalization and learning dynamics shapes convergence and stability. Our aim is to understand this mechanism rather than advocate norm-SGD as a practical optimizer.  

> A. Related Work

a. Theories of Neural Scaling Laws. Empirical scaling laws have been documented across several do-mains [22], quantifying the dependence of loss on model size, data, and compute [1, 2]. Theoretical explanations derive power-law exponents from kernel spectra and target alignment, or power-law random feature mod-els [3–5, 23, 24] or hierarchal data model [25]; with recent works extending the spectral framework to training dynamics, compute-optimal regimes and feature learning [6, 7, 26]. 

b. Optimizers and Scaling. Whether optimizer gains persist with model size and compute has itself become a scaling question. Comparisons among AdamW, Shampoo, SOAP and Muon show that reported gains depend on tuning, model scale, and training horizon [27–32]. Time dependent batch-size and learning rate schedules further shape finite-time scaling through update scale and gradient noise, batch-size schedules, and coupled schedules [17, 19, 33–37]. High-dimensional theory further resolves optimizer-dependent preconditioning and batch effects [8, 9]. Grafting and norm-based views separate update direction from magnitude [14, 38]. Following this decomposition, we study gradient normalization to isolate magnitude control while leaving the SGD direction unchanged. 

c. Critical Batch Size and Compute Efficiency. Increasing batch size trades more computation per update for fewer serial updates, with diminishing returns beyond a problem-dependent “critical batch size” [17, 18]. In LLM pretraining, prior work showed that critical batch size scales primarily with training data size rather than model size [19]. Batch-size schedules can also replace learning-rate decay while preserving similar learning curves [33]; Seesaw develops this connection theoretically for noisy linear regression and extends it to normalized SGD under a variance-dominated approximation [36]. Spectral analyses provide complementary perspective: high-dimensional theory identifies a spectrum-dependent batch threshold beyond which the convergence rate of SGD with momentum saturates [39]. d. Training Stability and large step size effects. Large learning rates can lead to faster optimization, while driving training toward the boundary of discrete-time stability. Because curvature determines the stable learning-rate range, its scaling with model size is also central to optimal learning-rate transfer. The μP framework establishes such transfer across width and depth [48–54], with recent work seeking mechanistic explanations for it through width-consistent sharpness and top spectral modes [55–57].

> V. BATCH SIZE AND WIDTH SPEEDUPS

While compute efficiency in power-law random-feature models has been studied extensively (for the case of SGD see [6, 7]), another aspect is how to reduce the number of optimization steps needed to reach a target loss without substantially increasing compute, which we will refer to as speed or speedup. The three phases identified above yield distinct regimes: 

• In Phase 1, increasing batch size speeds up optimization, but the reduction in steps does not offset the higher compute per step. Increasing width, however, can reduce the required steps enough to reach some target losses faster at roughly the equal total compute. 

• In Phase 2, increasing batch size can result in speedup with roughly equal compute, but increasing width cannot. 

• In Phase 3, neither larger batch size nor wider networks can lead to speedup with approximately equal compute if the model is over-parameterized ( ν ≥ 1) or the total parameter count in the model is larger than the batch size ( ν ≥ α). We quantify these results using Qα(α) := η∞ 

> eff

/α (or Qν (ν) := η∞ 

> eff

/ν ) which measure whether the potential steps saving due to the increase η∞ 

> eff

can compensate the increase in compute due to α (ν resp.). In all phases above these quantities would depend on the data and task through a, b , enabling speed up in some scenarios an forbidding it in others. The absence of approximately equal-compute speedup follows from the scaling of the effective learning rate. In the small-step Phase-1 regime, increasing batch size yields only square-root gains; in Phase 2, increasing width yields gains slower than square-root. These gains cannot offset the linear increase in per-step compute. In Phase 3, increased batch size provides no effective-step gain, while the fractional gain from increasing ν

scales at most as √ν leading to a significant compute inefficiency. The derivations are given in Appendix F 4.

VI. EXPERIMENT WITH REAL DATA AND NETWORKS 

In this section, we experiment with training real ResNet in the lazy (kernel) regime on the CIFAR-5M binary classification task of classifying animate vs inanimate while training with normalized SGD. To match the setting to our theory, we extract a, b from the infinite-width deterministic NTK and treat the linearized neural networks eNTK as random features drawn from the NTK. In our experiments, we measured b ≃ 1.8and a ≃ − 0.325, close to the measurements of [6] for the same architecture. In Fig. 5, right panel, we show the results when varying the width in the range w = 4 ( N = 1 , 120 random features) to w = 256 ( N = 4 , 135 , 936) with fixed B = 1 , 000 for a single pass with no repetitions. On the left panel we show the results for the same setting when fixing w = 128 and varying the batch size from B = 10 to 

B = 10 , 000. Since the NTK is infinite-dimensional, while any network has a finite parameter count and batch size, we adopt our theory to this dimension-free regime ( d → ∞ while N, B are finite) in Appendix H. This also means that ν < 1 and phase 2 (EoSS) is inaccessible. As discussed in the previous section, this implies that the smallest batch size B is always compute optimal, which we confirm in the left panel. Additionally, based on results from the previous section, we expect that as long as the model is time-constrained rather than parameter-constrained, narrow networks will be more compute-efficient for our measured exponents a, b ,which the experiments in the right panel confirm. Additionally, we add to the figures rulers showing the normalized gradient flow accelerated exponent, and intersecting rulers showing the breakdown of acceleration and the change to the standard SGD exponent when the effective learning rate reaches marginal stability. Throughout, our deterministic DMFT dynamics track the stochastic dynamics well, even though the DMFT assumes Gaussian features and only uses the NTK spectrum, the NTK target coefficients, and N, B , with no information on the eNTK or data, architecture, 9and task beyond those four quantities.  

> VII. OUTLOOK

Normalized SGD provides a solvable setting in which acceleration, stability, and resource allocation can be studied together. In continuous time, normalization preserves the gradient-flow trajectory while accelerating its clock, yielding accelerated power-law, exponential, or formal finite-time convergence depending on the teacher exponent. However, in discrete dynamics, the growing effective learning rate ultimately breaks this acceleration down through one of three mechanisms: width-limited noise and approximation error, a noise-driven EoSS, or deterministic EoS dynamics. We relate these phases to compute efficiency and speedup. We find a critical batch ratio αc that marks the phase transition between EoSS and EoS, and distinguish it from αδ , the maximal batch ratio that allows approximately linear scaling of learning rate and batch size – which allows speedup with equal compute. We quantify the dependence of these scales on width and on data exponents. Experiments with linearized ResNets on CIFAR-5M support the predicted acceleration and its breakdown, and connect the spectral description to observed batch- and width-dependent compute trends. Extending this connection beyond the kernel regime requires accounting for the evolution of features, target alignment, and curvature during training. Further directions include determining how learning-rate and batch-size schedules alter the trade-off, and relating effective-time efficiency to wall-clock performance under realistic hardware costs. Additionally, developing similar trade-off of compute–parameter count instead of compute-speedup could accelerate inference at the cost of increased compute cost in the training phase. Finally, extending the analysis to optimizers that also modify update direction would clarify which conclusions persist beyond global gradient normalization.

This transformation diagonalizes operators that have a shift symmetry, or as they are more commonly called time translation invariant (TTI) operators. Practically, the transform turns shifts and convolutions into algebraic manipulations.
