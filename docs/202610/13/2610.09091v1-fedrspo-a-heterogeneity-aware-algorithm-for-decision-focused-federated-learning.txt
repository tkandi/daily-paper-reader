## Abstract 

Decision-focused learning (DFL) trains predictive models for downstream op-timization, but existing methods largely assume centralized data. In cross-silo settings, federated learning is a natural alternative, yet standard federated pipelines optimize prediction quality rather than decision quality and do not account for client heterogeneity in downstream objectives or feasible sets. Heterogeneity is especially problematic for DFL methods: under heterogeneous polyhedral decision problems, small perturbations can cause discontinuous changes in optimal deci-sions, leading to unstable client updates and aggregation. We propose FedRSPO+ ,a heterogeneity-aware framework for decision-focused federated learning. Our approach is built on RSPO+ , a regularized predict-then-optimize surrogate that smooths the decision map through projection, enabling stable optimization even when clients face different objectives and constraints. We show that RSPO+ upper bounds downstream decision error and regret of the regularized decision and, under exact regularization and consistent LP solution selection, the original LP decision. We also demonstrate that FedRSPO+ has cross-client heterogeneity bounds that (i) 

scale with both objective and feasible-set heterogeneity, (ii) vanish at homogeneity, and (iii) do not require strong convexity. We develop an annealed and modular training procedure that is compatible with standard federated personalization and aggregation methods. We evaluate FedRSPO+ on three experimental settings: controlled synthetic knapsack instances that isolate multiple axes of heterogeneity, a shortest path DFL benchmark, and an energy pricing case study based on real data. Across these experiments, we compare against prediction-only federated learning and DFL frameworks under varying heterogeneity and communication budgets. Together, our results suggest that smoothing is a useful ingredient for stable collaborative decision learning and provide a heterogeneity-aware foundation for federated DFL 1.

## 1 Introduction 

Decision-focused learning (DFL) has emerged as a common approach to tackle data-driven contextual optimization [1, 2, 3, 4]. Among DFL methods, surrogate loss functions allow practitioners to take advantage of deep learning innovations in operational contexts, with the Smart Predict-then-Optimize (SPO) framework [5] being widely adopted. Its convex surrogate loss, SPO+, provides a tractable upper bound on the downstream decision loss and has seen broad use [6, 7, 8, 9]. However, cross-silo applications of DFL face a key constraint: collaborative analytics are often prohibited due to regulatory, privacy, and competitive considerations.

Federated learning (FL), which enables collaborative model training without centralizing data, offers a natural training procedure for addressing these constraints [10, 11, 12]. In cross-silo FL, a global model is trained across multiple organizations without centralizing data: each client keeps its local dataset and shares only model updates (or related statistics), which are aggregated to update a global model [13]. There exists an extensive FL litera-ture on mitigating data, model, system, and task heterogeneity [14, 15, 16, 17, 18, 19, 20].

In the DFL setting, though, when organizations collabo-ratively train DFL models while keeping data local, het-erogeneity in client objectives and constraints creates a fundamental challenge: standard federated aggregation becomes brittle because the optimal solution map can be discontinuous across clients. Let client i have covariates 

x ∼ X i, a client-specific true objective map ci : Xi → Rd,and a shared federated predictor g ∈ H used in the down-stream optimization model min w∈Si g(x)T w. Here, the objective map ci(·) and feasible region Si can differ across clients, which induces objective and constraint heterogene-ity that direct naive implementation of a decision-focused federated learning (DFFL) does not address (Fig. 1). For example, SPO+, the most prominent DFL approach, re-lies on an optimal solution map that can be discontinuous when clients have different linear objectives over poly-hedral feasible sets. Near boundaries where the optimal basis changes, arbitrarily small perturbations can switch the optimizer to a different extreme point, producing a 

O(diam( Si)) change in the optimal decision. We propose RSPO+, a smoothed surrogate that replaces the discontinuous optimal solution map with a projection-based decision, and FedRSPO+, a federated training framework built on RSPO+ with per-client calibration and co-annealed scheduling. Our contributions are summarized as follows: • We introduce RSPO+ and FedRSPO+ . RSPO+ is a regularized surrogate loss that smooths the decision map while retaining a provable connection to the original downstream regret. FedR-SPO+ builds on RSPO+ to provide a modular federated algorithm compatible with standard FL personalization and aggregation methods [14, 16, 21, 22]. • We formally show why naive federation of SPO+ can fail and how FedRSPO+ avoids the same issues, developing point-wise cross-client loss bounds that degrade gracefully with heterogeneity, vanish at homogeneity, and require no strong convexity. • We evaluate the approach on three tasks [23, 24, 25]: synthetic knapsack, shortest path, and PJM energy pricing. We compare against FedAvg, naive SPO+ federation and perturbation methods across communication budgets and controlled heterogeneity, demonstrating strong improvements in convex resource-allocation settings and competitive performance under structural mismatch. 

## 2 Related Work 

Decision-Focused Learning and Contextual Optimization Decision-focused federated learning is largely unexplored. The closest prior work is [26], which analyzed federating SPO+ under client-level objective and feasible-set heterogeneity. Their results showed that naive SPO+-based federation can be brittle in heterogeneous polyhedral problems: optimizer instability induces point-wise heterogeneity bounds with an irreducible diameter term, while stronger geometric assumptions such as strong convexity yield tighter stability guarantees. They also proposed Interp-DFFL, which interpolates between local and federated models using client validation data, which is complementary to our method, and we compare against it in Section 4. We build on this foundation by introducing a smoothed surrogate, RSPO+, and a modular federated training procedure that replaces discontinuous optimizer jumps with stable projection-based decisions, thereby removing the diameter penalty and yielding bounds that vanish at homogeneity. More broadly, DFL is a sub-topic of the broader area of contextual optimization [2, 3]. Other decision-aware surrogates include perturbation-based gradient losses [29] and Fenchel–Young losses [30], while black-box and implicit differentiation methods differentiate through the solver directly rather than introducing an explicit surrogate [24, 31, 32, 33]. Recent work has also begun to study DFL across multiple downstream tasks and changing objectives, suggesting a growing interest in DFL beyond the single-problem setting, though federated DFL still remains underexplored [34, 35, 36]. 

Federated Learning under Heterogeneity FL, in turn, includes extensive literature on heterogene-ity, including proximal penalties [14], control variates [37], localized or partially personalized models [38, 39, 40, 41, 42, 43], and regularized bilevel formulations [21]. However, these methods are de-signed primarily for heterogeneity in data distributions, model heterogeneity, or predictive objectives, not for heterogeneity in downstream optimization structure. This distinction is especially important in DFL, where small perturbations can induce large changes in the optimal decision. Notably, [21] use Moreau envelopes as a personalization regularizer, whereas we use Moreau smoothing on the decision-focused surrogate itself to stabilize the decision map. More generally, Bregman divergences are widely used in machine learning and optimization as geometry-aware discrepancy measures, supporting methods such as mirror descent, clustering, and structured prediction [44, 45, 46]. We leverage this to construct an adaptive surrogate aligned with each feasible set. Taken together, prior work addresses either decision-aware learning in centralized settings or heterogeneity-aware federated learning for predictive objectives, but not federated DFL with heterogeneous downstream feasible sets.

## 4 Experiment 

With each experiment, we test a different aspect of FedRSPO+. Our synthetic fractional knapsack experiment uses controlled heterogeneity across all three axes (feasible set, objective, and data) to test Theorem 3 empirically and the performance of our method against the state-of-the-art. Our PJM experiment uses real price dynamics and heterogeneous feasible sets to test our method on real datasets. Finally, our Warcraft experiment has heterogeneous starts and destinations to vary feasible sets and datasets across clients to evaluate how FedRSPO+ performs when the optimization task relies on the structure of the unregularized objective, and how RSPO+’s regularization affects performance. For all experiments, we use a federated warm-start followed by local personalization. Our model is a shallow neural network with 64 hidden units for the fractional knapsack and PJM tasks, and a truncated ResNet-18 for the Warcraft task following prior work [1]. We report relative regret in Table 3 and the optimality gap in Table 6. While FedRSPO+ is trained on a regularized objective, we report on the actual downstream regret. We also compare with popular perturbation DFL methods, perturbed Fenchel-Young Loss (PFYL, [30]) and Differentiable Perturbation Optimizers (DPO, [52]). We also compare with Interp-DFFL [26] and an OptNet-style baseline [48]. Implementation details, hyperparameters, and solver configurations are provided in the Appendix C, and additional centralized and local-only baselines in Appendix Section C.5. 

4.1 Fractional Knapsack 

To test FedRSPO+ against different axes of heterogeneity in the client dataset, we implement a fractional knapsack experiment, using a synthetic dataset for feature size p = 8 , cost vector size d =50 , and 20 clients. We test 3 different levels of heterogeneity, represented by (ηo, η c, η d) in Table 3: no heterogeneity, moderate heterogeneity, and extreme heterogeneity. Overall, RSPO+ has the lowest relative regret across the board. The largest gap is during federation, meaning RSPO+ succeeds in developing an effective model for all clients despite heterogeneity. Across all methods, data heterogeneity was most detrimental to model performance, with constraint heterogeneity moderately affecting regret and objective heterogeneity minimally. RSPO+ was found resilient to all forms of heterogeneity compared to the baseline methods. We also test additional baselines. 4.2 PJM Case Study 

We evaluate our approach on hourly day-ahead electricity prices from the PJM Interconnection for the year 2025 [25]. 4.3 Warcraft Shortest Path 

To characterize RSPO+’s behavior under structural mismatch, we evaluate on the 12x12 Warcraft shortest-path benchmark [24] with 10,000 training maps. We define 44 heterogeneous clients by assigning each border cell as an origin and its 180°-rotated opposite border cell as the destination, with additional terrain-cost perturbations. We use Dijkstra’s algorithm [55, 56] as the linear oracle. We explicitly chose shortest path because regularization alters the downstream optimization problem. Importantly, adding the quadratic term destroys the shortest path’s total unimodularity and allows fractional flows across the grid. RSPO+ still achieves a 2.571% optimality gap (Table 6) and outperforms perturbation methods and MSE, losing out marginally to SPO+. Annealing narrows the gap between the regularized flow objective and the true shortest-path objective, yielding a ∼66% improvement during personalization, the largest personalization gain across all three experiments. 4.4 Computational overhead 

While FedRSPO+ has no additional commu-nication costs compared with FedAvg, it has additional computational overhead, since solv-ing QLPs is more expensive than LP or short-est path problems. We observed 1.35 ×–1.77 ×

slower solves than LPs with Gurobi and up to 

∼50 × slower solves than Dijkstra. However, oracle-only timings overstate the end-to-end cost. In the Warcraft experiment, MSE and SPO+ both took about 67 seconds per round, whereas RSPO+ took 133 seconds, i.e., a 2.0 × increase in training time. The difference is mainly due to solving the QLP and computing the freezing criterion. The remaining model training is shared across methods and dominates most of the runtime. Moreover, 9the additional QLP oracle calls are parallelizable across clients and samples, suggesting that further engineering could reduce the observed wall-clock overhead. 

## 5 Conclusions 

In this paper, we connected the fields of FL and DFL by addressing the brittleness of current methods under optimization heterogeneity through our proposed method, FedRSPO+. We showed how smoothing via Moreau envelopes replaces discontinuous vertex-switching with √δ-stable projected decisions and achieves heterogeneity bounds that vanish at homogeneity without strong convexity. Our principled per-client calibration and co-annealed scheduling make the method practical without expensive tuning, and we demonstrated compatibility with existing FL methods such as FedProx and Ditto. Across our synthetic knapsack and PJM energy pricing experiments, RSPO+ consistently outperformed both prediction-only FL and naive SPO+ federation, with the largest gains appearing during the federation phase. It also outperformed Interp-DFFL and an OptNet-style baseline under objective and feasible-set heterogeneity. Even under the mismatched downstream objective in the Warcraft experiment, FedRSPO+ achieved close to optimal performance for clients, with significant gains over MSE and popular DFL methods. Together, these results position projection-based smoothing as a key ingredient for stable collaborative decision learning. 

Limitations. Our goal is to introduce a heterogeneity-aware surrogate and training methodology for DFFL, and to evaluate it against representative prediction-only FL, SPO+, personalization and aggregation variants, and standard DFL baselines where they are most applicable. A broader benchmark spanning additional FL optimizers, privacy mechanisms, combinatorial DFL methods, and how they fit in the field of DFFL is an important direction for future work. Our regret and decision-error guarantees are for the regularized decision, and apply to the LP decision only under exact regularization, which our stopping criterion does not enforce. Furthermore, our convergence analysis applies to linear estimators, while our main experiments use neural networks. We examine FedRSPO+ performance with linear estimators in the Appendix. RSPO+ replaces each LP solve with a QP of the same dimension. Specialized QP solvers can match simplex-based solvers on this structure [57, 58], and warm-starting can further reduce overhead. Furthermore, RSPO+ introduces hyperparameters α, κ LR , κ λ beyond SPO+ and MSE. While we provide principled defaults (Eq. (11)), tuning for new domains may add overhead. Our convexity assumptions (Assumptions 1 to 3) are standard in DFL but exclude mixed-integer programs. In Section 4.3 we demonstrate that FedRSPO+ is not appropriate for all downstream objectives, and extending FedRSPO+ to other domains and problem types via relaxations or decompositions is a natural direction. 

Broader Impact. FedRSPO+ enables privacy-preserving collaboration for data-driven decision-making in settings where data sharing is restricted by regulation or competition, potentially lowering barriers for smaller organizations that lack the data scale to train effective models independently. However, participants with higher-quality data or more favorable constraint structures may benefit disproportionately from federation, and model updates can still leak information about local objec-tives, particularly in small-coalition settings. Practitioners should consider pairing FedRSPO+ with differential privacy or secure aggregation mechanisms in sensitive domains.

Remark 4 (Scope and practical interpretation) . Theorem 4 is stated for the convex linear-model setting, whereas Proposition 5 gives a post-freeze stationary-point analogue for the nonconvex neural predictors used in our experiments. The additional constants J and ρ in Assumption 4 control, respectively, the sensitivity of the predictor to parameter perturbations and the variation of this sensitivity along the training trajectory. For smooth activations with bounded derivatives, these constants are finite on compact parameter regions. For ReLU networks, the Jacobian is only piecewise constant and may jump at activation-boundary crossings, so Assumption 4 only provides a trajectory-level regularity condition. The resulting smoothness bound LNN  

> f

= 4 J2/λ f + 2 Dρ can be conservative when J or ρ is large. Therefore, the nonconvex result only adapts a standard FedAvg guarantee to the frozen RSPO+ objective, and the experiments provide empirical support for the same bias–stability mechanism beyond the convex setting. When ρ = 0 , as in the linear case, the co-annealed condition reduces to controlling the invariant ηtLt = O(αJ 2), matching the role of the convex co-annealing condition.

## C Experimental Details 

This section provides the full experimental details of the setup described in Section 4. Experiments were conducted on AMD EPYC Milan CPU nodes with 128 cores per node and 528 GB RAM. The total wall-clock runtime for the reported experiments was approximately 250 hours. For the Warcraft case study, each seed-method run took approximately 4 hours for MSE and SPO+ and approximately 6 hours for RSPO+ and the other DFL methods. For the synthetic knapsack experiments, one full heterogeneity-design run covering the three main methods (MSE, SPO+, RSPO+) and three heterogeneity levels across three axes and five seeds took approximately 90 minutes. The PJM experiments took approximately 2 minutes for five seeds. 

C.1 Shared Configuration 

For all experiments and baselines, we use a federated warm-start then local personalization. We use a shallow neural network with 64 neurons for the fractional knapsack and PJM experiments. For the Warcraft experiment, we use a truncated ResNet-18 [62], as per the literature [24]. We implement our method and baselines using the InferOpt.jl framework [56] in Julia [63] with the Lux.jl [64] deep learning package. We use the Gurobi [65] through the JuMP framework [66]. While FedRSPO+ uses the per-client λ and learning rates defined in Eq. (11), we use a learning rate of 10 −3 for the baselines. We use κLR = 0 .1, κ λ = 1 , and α = 0 .01 for the fractional knapsack experiments, and α = 10 −3 for the PJM and Warcraft experiments, selected to set initial learning rates within the same magnitude as the baseline learning rate 10 −3. We use a stopping threshold 

τ = 1% . We report absolute regret R = zLP (c, ˆw) − zLP (c, w ⋆) and relative regret R/z Avg LP (c, w ⋆)

as metrics, where zAvg LP is the per-client average ground-truth objective functions. 

Optimizer. All experiments use Adam [67] with gradient clipping at norm 1.0 and no weight decay. Tabular experiments (knapsack, PJM) use Float64 precision; the Warcraft experiment uses 

Float32 .

Freeze mechanism. The annealing schedules for λ and η in Eq. (11) use a scheduler: once the stopping criterion (Eq. (6)) is satisfied, the schedule freezes and training continues. For SPO+ and MSE baselines, all schedules are held constant ( κλ = κLR = 0 ). We also tested early stopping a few rounds after freezing, and found that while it did help mitigate computational overhead, it did not significantly influence training performance, due to the already annealed learning rate slowing training in later rounds. We implement an early stopping condition 3 rounds after the schedule freezing criterion is met.

Client heterogeneity. Rather than 4 corner-defined clients, we use a border-antipodes construction on the 12 × 12 grid. Since the grid border contains 4 · 12 − 4 = 44 cells, this yields 44 clients. Predicted cost grids are decoded into hard paths using the benchmark shortest-path solver (Dijkstra). Float32 precision is used throughout.

C.4 PJM Energy Pricing 

We use hourly day-ahead locational marginal pricing (LMP) data from the PJM Interconnection, a regional transmission organization spanning 13 U.S. states [25]. We use the following nodes as clients: DOM, PEPCO, PPL, DUQ, BGE, AECO, ATSI, RECO, PSEG, DPL, DAY, METED, JCPL, APS, DEOK, AEP, COMED, PECO, and PENELEC. Prices are retrieved for the year 2025 and assigned to clients, with variation arising from localized congestion effects and are in dollars per MWh [68]. Energy markets provide a natural setting for decision-focused federated learning, as operational data are often siloed across entities and subject to regulatory protections such as NERC CIP-011 [69]. 

Data processing. The 8 weather covariates are: mean/min/max temperature ( ◦C), mean humidity, mean wind speed, mean/max solar irradiance, and day of week. The 24 target dimensions are

Optimization problem. The downstream task is to minimize daily electricity cost subject to daily demand. This can be formulated as a capacity-constrained fractional knapsack problem, as described in Section 4.1. Specifically, the feasible set is defined as S = {w ∈ [0 , 1] 24 : 1T w = Dj }, where Dj

Data splits. The data is split chronologically with no shuffling: 50% train, 10% validation, 40% test, allocated by date index, with 19 total clients.

Discussion. Together, these baselines reinforce the interpretation of the main results. Centralized training is a useful upper-bound comparator but is not uniformly dominant: it excels when clients are statistically aligned and degrades sharply under data heterogeneity. Local-only training is a strong personalization baseline in two specific regimes (SPO+ in synthetic data-heterogeneous settings and RSPO+ on PJM) but offers no shared model and is inconsistent across heterogeneity types, due to the addition of the downstream objective. The main FedRSPO+ result is a robust middle ground that retains performance across the heterogeneity axes while supporting data privacy. The PJM observation that local-only RSPO+ is competitive with FedRSPO+ also identifies a regime where the federated machinery is nearly redundant. We view formalizing this regime, where shared training adds little beyond the surrogate itself, as a useful direction for follow-up work.
