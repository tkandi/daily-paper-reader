# BARE-AI : Bit-Flip Attack Resilience in AI Hardware through Built-in Performance Monitors 

Habibur Rahaman , Student Member, IEEE , Swastik Bhattacharya , Student Member, IEEE , Sanjay Das , Student Member, IEEE , Kanad Basu , Senior Member, IEEE Swarup Bhunia , Fellow, IEEE 

Abstract —Deep Neural Networks (DNNs) are now integral to a wide range of safety critical systems, yet they remain highly vulnerable to bit-flip attacks (BFAs), in which a small number of memory level perturbations can drastically degrade model accu-racy. Existing defenses often incur significant hardware overhead, depend on retraining, or fail against targeted adversarial flips. We propose BARE-AI , a runtime framework that detects, localizes, and mitigates BFAs during AI inference. BARE-AI introduces AI Performance Counters (APCs), lightweight hardware monitors embedded in the accelerator datapath that capture fine grained per-layer activation statistics such as sparsity, entropy, kurtosis, and spectral shift. These statistics are analyzed by the Predictive Unit for Layer Security Evaluation (PULSE) , a compact machine learning detector trained offline as an ensemble of classifiers and realized on-chip as a small neural engine. For explainability and recovery, BARE-AI introduces an Activation Shift Index (ASI) for layer level fault localization and a z-score based statistical repair that resets anomalous weights toward clean layer statistics. Across CNNs, Vision Transformers, and Large Language Models under random, targeted, adaptive, and magnitude based BFAs, 

BARE-AI achieves up to 98% detection accuracy on vision models and between 74% and 95% on language models, restores near clean accuracy for CNNs and ViTs, and provides partial recovery for LLMs. Synthesized in a 28nm technology node, the monitoring infrastructure incurs less than 3% energy overhead, less than 4% area overhead, and approximately 10% inference latency increase, with a configurable operating point that reduces latency overhead to about 6%. A key property of the design is that its cost is independent of attack strength: unlike error correcting codes, whose redundancy grows with the number of tolerated flips, the monitoring overhead of BARE-AI remains constant regardless of how many bits an adversary flips. These properties make BARE-AI attractive for resource constrained, safety critical edge applications such as autonomous systems, energy, and healthcare. 

Index Terms —DNN, AI Performance Counters, Bit-Flip At-tack, AI Safety, AI accelerators, hardware security, runtime monitoring.

Model parameters stored in DRAM or SRAM are vulnerable to diverse fault injection mechanisms, including Rowhammer induced charge leakage [7], laser injection [8], voltage glitch-ing [4], and radiation induced upsets [8, 9]. Variants such as SledgeHammer [10] and RowPress [11] demonstrate that disturbance effects persist even in DDR4 systems, highlighting the fragility of DRAM [12, 13]. The threat is exacerbated in edge devices, which lack hardware redundancy, and in cloud settings, where Rowhammer can be launched from co-located virtual machines [18]. These attacks exploit an asymmetry of memory level faults: microscopic perturbations propagate across layers into catastrophic mis-behavior, motivating lightweight runtime anomaly detection. Existing defenses against BFAs differ in methodology, merits, and limitations. Hardware methods such as ECC and redundancy mitigate random faults but fail against adversar-ial flips and incur substantial power, latency, and area cost [17, 19]. Training time defenses such as BFAT [5] improve robustness through weight corruption during retraining, but cannot detect or localize inference time faults and weaken under targeted or adaptive attacks. Software level methods, including weight randomization, memory layout obfuscation [20], and weight reconstruction [21], offer partial protection but require retraining, prior knowledge of attack locations, or system changes, limiting runtime applicability. Alternatives such as binarized networks [22] improve resilience at the cost of accuracy and generality. Most existing defenses suffer from performance, scalability, or deployment limitations [8].

In this section, we present the threat model for BFAs and review the defenses that have been proposed. 

A. Threat Model and Attack Surface 

To assess the capability of the proposed defense framework against corruption of the inference time model, we first define the class of adversarial behaviors against which the system must defend. We adopt a threat model in which adversaries compromise the integrity of deployed DNNs by inducing 3

parameter perturbations, specifically bit flips, in stored weight parameters. The model weights, encoded in FP32, FP16/BF16, or INT8 formats, may reside in off-chip DRAM or on-chip SRAM or cache. Adversaries can exploit gradient guided or heuristic search techniques such as Progressive Bit Search (PBS) [4, 6, 14] and Targeted Bit-Flip Attacks (TBFA) [5] to identify highly sensitive weight bits for maximal disrup-tion. Realistic fault injection mechanisms include disturbance induced memory corruption such as Rowhammer [7], tar-geted neural network specific campaigns such as DeepHam-mer [19, 17, 14], and broader techniques involving voltage or clock glitching, electromagnetic or laser injection, and software based stressors [16]. The resulting perturbations may manifest as isolated single bit errors, clustered flips within a codeword, or burst faults across adjacent memory locations, patterns that can evade simple error correcting codes or overwhelm hardware scrubbing. Whether such perturbations arise from malicious attacks, including parasitic trojans in the ML infrastructure [24], or from non-malicious hardware faults, they introduce critical errors into the computational flow, propagate across layers, and ultimately compromise model integrity, an unacceptable risk in safety critical applications. Our objective is to reliably detect any deviation from expected model behavior and enable timely mitigation. The security of the monitoring infrastructure itself, and the reason it does not share the attack surface of the model weights, is analyzed in Section III-A3. 

B. Defenses of Bit-Flip Attacks (BFAs) 

Existing defenses against BFAs can be categorized by the stage at which protection is applied: hardware, training time, system (runtime), and software. Hardware level approaches include error correcting codes (ECC), memory scrambling or layout randomization, and dual modular redundancy (DMR). ECC corrects random faults but incurs area and latency over-head and is ineffective against targeted adversarial flips [21]. Memory scrambling and layout randomization reduce attacker precision but can be reverse engineered via side channels, while DMR detects faults through redundant execution at the cost of doubled computation. Training time defenses aim to improve robustness during model optimization. Techniques such as Bit-Flip Adversarial Training (BFAT) inject bit flips during retraining [5], neural fingerprinting detects deviations from clean activation patterns, and activation constraining methods limit fault propagation through range restriction or boundary aware clipping [25, 26]. Fault aware training and range aware quantization further improve resilience by constraining vulnerable weights [27, 28]. Although effective, these methods depend on offline retraining, are architecture specific, and do not provide runtime fault detection or re-covery. System level monitoring using hardware performance counters (HPCs) captures execution anomalies [29], while software level techniques include robust quantized models [22], selective weight protection [30], and runtime detection and correction mechanisms such as NeuroPots [31]. These approaches remain limited by hardware dependence, static configurations, or insufficient fault localization. Meanwhile, attacks such as DeepHammer [19], impactful search [17], and quantized attacks [14] continue to grow more efficient, motivating stronger defenses. 

Positioning of this work. Individual ingredients of our approach have appeared in prior contexts: HPC based anomaly detection monitors processor level events for malware and integrity violations [29], activation fingerprinting compares runtime activations against reference patterns [5], statistical bounds on weights have been used for reconstruction based repair [21], and NeuroPots performs runtime detection with honeypot neurons [31]. BARE-AI differs from these lines of work in four respects. First, the monitored signal is a purpose built set of per-layer activation statistics captured by dedicated counters inside the accelerator datapath, rather than general purpose CPU event counters that observe the workload only in-directly. Second, detection, layer level localization, and statis-tical repair are integrated into a single runtime pipeline; prior activation fingerprinting detects anomalies without localizing or repairing them, and reconstruction based repair [21] requires retraining and prior fault identification. Third, the hardware cost of the monitor is independent of the number of adversarial flips, in contrast to ECC whose redundancy grows with the required correction strength (Section V). Fourth, the same monitoring infrastructure applies unchanged across CNNs, Vision Transformers, and LLMs, since the counters observe generic activation statistics rather than architecture specific structures. We view the contribution as the co-designed system and its hardware realization, together with the ASI localization metric and the evidence that activation level counters retain discriminative power under quantization, adaptive attacks, and cross domain transfer. To address the gaps above, we propose BARE-AI , a model agnostic runtime behavioral monitoring framework that pro-vides real time detection, localization, and recovery from bit flip induced distribution shifts. Using a lightweight hardware assisted mechanism that tracks activation statistics via APCs, 

BARE-AI delivers fine grained explainability and robust protection with low overhead across CNN and Transformer architectures. 

C. Model Quantization 

Quantization reduces the computational and memory over-head of deep neural networks, enabling deployment on re-source constrained hardware such as consumer grade GPUs or edge devices. Converting floating point weights and activations to low bit formats such as INT8 significantly lowers mem-ory and bandwidth requirements with minimal architectural changes. Well designed quantization schemes (uniform or per-channel) preserve accuracy and reduce inference latency even with aggressive bit width reductions [32, 33, 34]. Post-training quantization (PTQ) is especially practical, as it operates on pretrained models without retraining or access to training data [32, 35]. For large architectures such as LLMs, quantiza-tion methods have evolved to meet precision and stability demands. Toolkits such as BitsAndBytes enable INT8 post-training quantization of LLMs using mixed precision and vector wise strategies. The LLM.int8() method [36] applies 8-bit computation to most weights while isolating rare large magnitude outliers for 16-bit processing, allowing over 99.9% of matrix operations to run in INT8. This hybrid approach significantly reduces inference memory while preserving near full precision accuracy for large models [36, 37]. In this work, we adopt BitsAndBytes based INT8 PTQ to evaluate the performance of BARE-AI against BFAs on quantized models. 4

III. METHODOLOGY 

In this section, we present the design of BARE-AI , a lightweight runtime defense framework that leverages layer wise behavioral signals, termed AI Performance Counter (APC) metrics, to detect and recover from bit-flip attacks. The framework does not require retraining of the protected model. 

A. BARE-AI Framework 

BARE-AI is integrated with the AI accelerator as shown in Fig. 3. It is composed of four major operations: i) APC Ex-traction , which captures a rich set of activation based metrics across layers during inference; ii) the PULSE Classifier , which determines whether the model has been compromised; iii) 

Fault Localization , which identifies the layers affected by the bit-flip attack using the Activation Shift Index (ASI); and iv) Z-Score Based Restoration , which repairs compromised weights to recover clean functionality with minimal degradation. 

A.1 AI Performance Counters (APCs) : We define a be-havioral fingerprint (Fig. 3) for each DNN layer using a set of statistical metrics derived from its activation outputs. As shown in Fig. 4, the APC integrated with the accelerator collects key inference metrics (Table I). The APC block consists of a counter array, an FSM controller, and a memory block, with configuration hardware that enables dynamic counter selection for workload specific monitoring. The neuroscope, embedded within the APC, records activity per layer (e.g., sparsity, entropy, activations) in a manner conceptually similar to PyTorch hooks [38], but without interfering with execution. The FSM controller enables runtime logging and adversarial detection via PULSE without external hooks. The extracted metrics are summarized in Table I. By track-ing computational efficiency, activation sparsity, and distribu-tional statistics, the APC enables comprehensive characteriza-tion of neural network behavior. A.2 ML based Analysis Engine (PULSE) : In BARE-AI ,the data collected by the APC is analyzed by a lightweight ML engine, PULSE . It continuously monitors workloads on the AI accelerator, detects unauthorized or adversarial behavior, and provides real time feedback during inference. Beyond identifying malicious activity, it alerts the user, enabling the AI IP owner to restrict model usage or specific inference capabilities. The system is model agnostic and requires no changes to the model architecture or training pipeline. The PULSE detection function operates in two forms that we distinguish explicitly. For offline training and for all detection accuracy results reported in Section IV, PULSE is an ensemble of Random Forest, XGBoost, and LSTM classifiers trained on labeled APC feature vectors; the LSTM compo-nent captures temporal and inter-layer dynamics. For on-chip deployment and for the hardware overhead characterization in Section IV-D, the learned decision function is realized as a compact three layer neural engine with a 16-8-4 neuron configuration that operates on the selected APC features. This compact engine is the unit synthesized in the 28nm technology node. The two stage design keeps training expressive while keeping the deployed logic small enough for negligible silicon cost. 

A.3 Threat Model and Security of the Monitoring In-frastructure : Attacker capabilities. Building on the attack surface defined in Section II-A, we consider an adversary who (i) can mount disturbance based fault injection such as Rowhammer [7] and RowPress [11] against off-chip DRAM, 5

Fig. 3: AI Performance Counter (APC) in an AI accelerator, illustrating the architecture of the APC module, which consists of a Finite State Machine (FSM) and the APC memory block that records operational metrics during AI inference and transmits the data to PULSE for real-time bit-flip attack detection. including from co-located software in virtualized settings; (ii) has white box knowledge of the victim model and can use gradient guided bit search [4, 39] to select target bits; (iii) can supply arbitrary inference inputs; and (iv) may possess full knowledge of the BARE-AI design, including which APC statistics are monitored. The attacker cannot physically probe or modify the fabricated die, cannot execute code within the trusted firmware layer that configures the monitor, and cannot directly read or write on-die monitor state. We discuss relaxations of these assumptions at the end of this section. 

Why the monitor does not share the attack surface of the weights. A natural question is the following: if an adversary can flip bits in the model weights, why can the same attack not corrupt the APC counters or the PULSE parameters, thereby blinding the defense? The answer lies in a fundamental asymmetry of memory technology and capacity, not in an appeal to trust. Rowhammer and its variants are disturbance phenomena specific to high density DRAM cells, in which repeated row activations induce charge leakage in physically adjacent rows [12, 13]. Model weights must reside in off-chip DRAM because of their size, tens to hundreds of megabytes, which places them directly within this attack surface. In contrast, the entire BARE-AI state, comprising APC counter registers, FSM state, feature buffers, and the deployed PULSE parameters, occupies only a few kilobytes and is implemented exclusively in on-die SRAM and standard cell registers within the accelerator. On-die SRAM and flip-flops are not susceptible to Rowhammer class disturbance errors: they use a different bit cell technology, are not row activation addressable by user software, and are not mapped into any attacker accessible address space. The very property that makes the weights attackable, bulk storage in commodity DRAM, does not apply to the monitor. 

Low cost integrity hardening of the monitor. The small footprint of the monitor enables a complementary argument: protections that are prohibitively expensive for the model itself are essentially free for the monitor. Based on the synthesis results in Table XIII, the deployed PULSE engine occupies 0.043 mm 2; triplicating it with majority voting would add approximately 0.086 mm 2, which is below 4% of even the smallest accelerator configuration considered, and parity pro-tection of the APC counter array and feature buffers adds a single bit per word of a few kilobyte memory. Beyond parity, the monitor state can itself be protected with error correcting codes at negligible cost: a standard (72,64) SECDED code on the PULSE parameter memory adds 12.5% redundancy on a store of a few kilobytes, on the order of hundreds of bytes, and even multi-bit BCH codes correcting several flips per word remain trivial at this scale. We emphasize that this is fully consistent with our critique of ECC based weight protection in Section V: as Eq. 2 shows, ECC cost scales with both the size of the protected data and the correction strength t, and is therefore prohibitive for tens of millions of weight bits yet insignificant for a kilobyte scale monitor. The same scaling law that rules out ECC for the model makes it an ideal armor for the monitor. By comparison, applying modular redundancy to tens of millions of weight parameters is infeasible by orders of magnitude. This asymmetry, a minimal trusted computing base that is cheap to harden monitoring a large asset that is expensive to harden, is the standard rationale for hardware roots of trust, and BARE-AI follows the same principle. In addition, the deployed PULSE parameters can be periodically validated against a reference digest held in on-die non-volatile or fuse based storage; any mismatch triggers an alert and quarantines the inference output. APC configuration registers are writable only through the trusted firmware path established at secure boot, so a user space or guest level attacker cannot lower thresholds or disable counters. 

Side channel considerations. The monitoring infrastructure raises two side channel questions. First, the monitor could it-self become a leakage source: APC traces summarize per-layer activation statistics and could, if exposed, leak information about the model architecture or the inputs being processed. 

BARE-AI therefore never exports raw traces off-die. APC outputs are consumed directly by PULSE on the same silicon, and only the binary detection verdict, optionally with the flagged layer index, crosses the trust boundary. Raw counter access is restricted to the trusted firmware interface, and readouts can be quantized and temporally aggregated so that fine grained per-inference values are not observable even to privileged diagnostic software. We note that the switching activity of the counter logic is small relative to the MAC array 6

and memory subsystem, limiting its visibility in the power signature; a formal power and electromagnetic side channel evaluation of the monitor is left to future work. Second, an attacker who learns the monitored statistics or thresholds may attempt side channel assisted evasion, crafting flips that remain below the detection boundary. Our adaptive attack evaluation (Table VII) models a strong version of this adversary: gradient guided attacks that jointly optimize bit positions and inputs to suppress APC deviations, with knowledge of the feature set, are detected with at least 93.8% accuracy. Two design properties limit evasion further. The detection boundary is not a single public constant: the adaptive threshold is calibrated per device and per model from local clean run statistics, so thresholds differ across deployments. Randomized temporal sampling of APC readouts additionally denies the attacker a deterministic view of which inference windows are being scored. 

Limitations under weaker threat models. For transparency, we state the boundaries of our guarantees. If the adversary compromises the trusted firmware layer itself, for example through an exploited secure boot chain, the APC configu-ration interface is exposed and BARE-AI degrades to the protection level of the underlying platform; defending the firmware root of trust is an orthogonal problem addressed by measured and verified boot. A hardware Trojan inserted at design or fabrication time could, by construction, bypass any on-die monitor including ours; pre-silicon verification and post-silicon Trojan detection are complementary lines of defense. Invasive physical attacks such as microprobing are outside our model, as they defeat all non tamper resistant hardware equally. Within the stated model, a software level or disturbance based fault injection adversary with white box model knowledge, the monitor attack surface is physically disjoint from that of the weights, and its integrity can be maintained at negligible cost. 

B. Bit-flip Attack Detection & Mitigation 

The AI accelerator supports both training and inference. During inference, the APC block logs performance metrics (Table I) in dedicated memory and forwards them to PULSE 

for activity monitoring. On detecting anomalies, PULSE flags the incident, can halt the affected IP block, and reports to the oversight authority. Adversarial detection in BARE-AI re-quires selecting counters during an offline phase using clean and adversarial samples. Feature selection identifies the most informative APCs, which remain fixed during inference; in certain applications, data independent APC choices may also be viable. The complete runtime defense workflow of 

BARE-AI is described in Algorithm 1. It defends against BFAs by leveraging activation based APCs through a two phase process (offline and online) that is model agnostic and hardware efficient. 

BARE-AI incorporates a two stage normalization and adap-tive thresholding mechanism. Z-score normalization standard-izes each APC metric per layer during offline profiling using the mean and variance from clean executions, centering benign variation and scaling dispersion to ensure well conditioned fea-tures for the PULSE detector. The adaptive threshold τadaptive 

defines a dynamic anomaly boundary using clean signature statistics, with τadaptive = μc + λ σ c, where μc and σc denote the mean and standard deviation of clean signatures, and 

λ (empirically set to 2) controls sensitivity to benign drift. This enables PULSE to adapt detection confidence to model and dataset specific variability. Additionally, PULSE stabilizes detection and reduces false positives by aggregating Random Forest, XGBoost, and LSTM outputs through weighted voting.

B.2 PULSE Training Process : PULSE training is shown in Fig. 4. The AI accelerator runs inference on both clean and adversarial data, while the APC captures operational traces under each condition. To evaluate BARE-AI , we used a diverse set of neural net-work architectures and datasets widely adopted in adversarial robustness research. The CNN models include AlexNet, VGG-16, ResNet-18, and ResNet-50, covering lightweight and deep architectures. We further extended the evaluation to ViT-B and MobileNetV2 to capture performance across transformer and efficient convolutional architectures. The vision models were benchmarked on standard datasets such as ImageNet-1K [40]. These workloads collectively stress test the framework across heterogeneous data modalities. To simulate adversarial threats, we implemented state of the art BFAs [4], including random bit flips and Progressive Bit Search (PBS) [39]. These attacks represent both random and highly targeted strategies, enabling a comprehensive robustness evaluation. The detection and mitigation pipeline was evaluated on an NVIDIA RTX A6000 GPU platform. This platform was cho-sen for algorithmic validation of runtime detection accuracy across diverse architectures and for fair comparison with prior bit-flip defenses, which also employ GPU based evaluations; in this setting, APC feature extraction is emulated in software with hooks that mirror the hardware counter semantics. C. BARE-AI Performance Analysis 

In this section, we use the insights obtained from the APC metrics (Section IV-B) to systematically detect and localize BFAs in different DNN models, mitigate their effects, and assess the effectiveness of BARE-AI .

C.1 Detection Performance : To assess the capability of 

BARE-AI in detecting bit-flip attacks, we experimentally investigate its detection performance and robustness in detail. 

Detection Accuracy of BARE-AI : Table II compares the impact of three representative bit-flip attack strategies, Bit-flip Attack (BFA), Random Bit-Flip Attack (RBFA), and Progressive Bit Search (PBS), on ViT-B, ResNet-50, and MobileNetV2 using ImageNet-1K. Random bit flips moder-ately reduce accuracy, while sensitivity guided BFA amplifies degradation. The most destructive case is PBS, which selec-tively flips the most sensitive weight bits based on gradient information, reducing ResNet-50 accuracy from 80% to only 22%. Despite such severe degradation, BARE-AI consistently achieves high detection accuracy across all models, with aver-age detection rates of 93% for ViT-B, 92% for ResNet-50, and 91% for MobileNetV2. These results show that BARE-AI is model agnostic and effective across both convolutional and transformer architectures. 

Detecting BFAs on different bit groups: Although detection rates are generally high, Table III shows clear differences between floating point bit groups. Exponent flips (FP30 to FP23) cause large distortions and are detected with 95 to 98% accuracy and only 1 to 2% false negatives. Sign bit flips (FP31) are moderately harder, achieving 90 to 93% accuracy with up to 7% false negatives, due to changes in activation direction and statistics. Mantissa flips (FP22 to FP0) are the most subtle, reducing detection accuracy to 70 to 75% and increasing false negatives to 15 to 20%. These results characterize both the strength of BARE-AI against high impact perturbations and its current limits against low magnitude mantissa flips, which by construction cause small behavioral deviation and correspondingly small model damage. 

Cross Attack Detection Performance: Our APC feature selection is attack agnostic, relying on fundamental activation level statistics (e.g., entropy drift, sparsity ratio, cosine simi-larity shift) that capture deviations from weight perturbations independent of the attack method. These intrinsic properties generalize to unseen attacks. As shown in Table IV, training PULSE on one attack (Progressive Bit Search) and testing on another (Random Bit-Flip) results in only a 1.7% accuracy drop, demonstrating strong cross attack robustness. Moreover, PULSE supports incremental updates with new APC traces without retraining the base model, enabling continual adapta-tion to emerging attack variants. 

Detection Performance for Cross Domain Attack: PULSE generalizes beyond dataset specific biases by using architecture agnostic APC features and ensemble learning that capture intrinsic activation dynamics rather than pixel semantics. We evaluate this by training PULSE on one dataset and model pair and testing directly on different datasets without retraining. Trained across diverse architectures (ResNet, MobileNet, ViT) and datasets (CIFAR-10, ImageNet, Tiny-ImageNet), PULSE learns a high order decision boundary in the APC space using the RF, XGB, and LSTM ensemble. In cross domain tests (e.g., ImageNet to CIFAR-100 and SVHN), average accuracy reaches 94.8% (Table V). Cross architecture transfer between CNNs and ViTs incurs under a 3% accuracy drop, confirming robust generalization. 

Detection Performance for Small Random Bit-Flips: De-tecting subtle perturbations is critical for runtime robustness. To evaluate PULSE under minimal corruption, we injected random low rate bit-flip faults ( Nflip ∈ { 1, 2, 4, 8}, below 0.001% of total weights) into ResNet-18 and ViT-B across 200 randomized trials per configuration. Table VI shows that detection accuracy exceeds 91% for a single flip and rises to 97.4% for eight flips, with false alarm rates below 4%. We observe that (i) spectral and entropy features are highly sensitive, providing early fault cues; and (ii) mean and sparsity metrics tolerate small noise, showing limited sensitivity to single flips. Overall, PULSE reliably detects even minute perturbations without excess false positives; ensemble fusion mitigates metric level masking effects.

Based on this approach, the scaled area esti-mates for popular CNN models are tabulated in Table XIII as follows: AlexNet 2.27mm 2, ResNet-18 3.39mm 2, ResNet-50 4.52mm 2, and VGG-16 6.78mm 2. We note that these accelerator side figures are scaling based estimates rather than measured silicon, and that the BARE-AI overhead percentages should be read relative to this estimation methodology.

Although ECC has been the traditional approach for protect-ing memory against random soft errors, it faces fundamental limitations when applied to DNNs under bit-flip attacks. Adaptive adversaries can concentrate multiple flips within the same codeword, defeating single error correction and forcing the use of stronger multi-bit correction, which drives rapidly increasing redundancy in area, latency, and energy. Practical implementations additionally incur latency for syndrome computation and correction, along with interleaving and buffering to avoid clustered errors. In contrast, BARE-AI does not rely on storing redundant bits. It leverages layer wise APCs, such as sparsity, entropy, and cosine drift, to monitor abnormal deviations caused by bit-flip attacks. The overhead depends only on the number of layers ( L) and counters per layer ( K): Overhead BARE-AI = αLK, (3) where α is a small implementation constant representing the cost of each counter. Crucially, this cost is independent of the number of adversarial flips t. Whether the adversary flips 1 bit or 100 bits, the detection infrastructure remains constant. ECC overhead grows as O(t) with rising resiliency re-quirements, while BARE-AI incurs constant O(1) overhead regardless of attack strength. This scalability advantage makes 

BARE-AI more efficient for protecting DNNs against adaptive bit-flip attacks, which often involve clustered multi-bit flips that are especially costly for ECC to handle. The same asymmetry underlies the monitor hardening argument of Sec-tion III-A3: the small, constant size of the trusted monitoring base is what makes it cheap to protect. VI. CONCLUSION 

We presented BARE-AI , a low cost runtime defense against bit-flip attacks in DNN accelerators. By leveraging embedded AI Performance Counters and the lightweight PULSE detector, 

BARE-AI detects and localizes adversarial faults with up to 98% accuracy on vision models and between 74% and 95% on language models, while incurring modest energy (below 3%), area (below 4%), and latency (approximately 10%, reducible to about 6%) overhead. The framework cap-tures subtle runtime deviations induced by random, targeted, adaptive, and magnitude based bit flips across CNNs, Vision Transformers, and LLMs. Unlike ECC or retraining based defenses, BARE-AI is attack agnostic and integration friendly, provides actionable insight through the ASI, enables statistical repair of corrupted layers through z-score based restoration, and maintains a monitoring cost that is independent of attack strength. Recovery is near clean for CNNs and ViTs and currently partial for deep language models, where errors accu-mulate across the transformer stack. Future work will address stronger repair mechanisms for LLMs, including per-channel and per-head statistics, silicon or FPGA level validation of the APC extraction pipeline, robustness characterization under benign distribution shift, and formal side channel evaluation of the monitoring infrastructure.
