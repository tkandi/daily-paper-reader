# Byzantine-Resilient Federated Large Language Model via Hypergraph Message Passing 

Kai Li, Senior Member, IEEE , Hanlin Cai, Member, IEEE , Zihao Liu, Yichen Li, Member, IEEE ,Jose Alcaraz Calero, Senior Member, IEEE , Wei Ni, Fellow, IEEE , Falko Dressler, Fellow, IEEE , and ¨Ozg¨ ur B. Akan, Fellow, IEEE 

Abstract —Federated Large Language Models (FedLLMs) are promising to deliver privacy-preserving, collaborative intelligence. The decentralized training process is vulnerable to Byzantine attacks that corrupt semantic consistency and factual resilience through malicious model updates. This paper presents a new resilience strategy that prevents semantically corrupted knowledge from propagating during federated aggregation. A defense problem is formulated to jointly maximize global accuracy and suppress manipulated model updates, enabling trustworthy knowledge aggregation without accessing private local data. To strengthen the resilience of FedLLMs, we develop a novel Hypergraph Message Passing Graph Autoencoder (HMP-GAE) framework, where low-rank model updates are represented as nodes in a hyper-graph and high-order semantic relationships among multiple agents are exploited through iterative message passing. By leveraging historical checkpoints and cross-agent relational reasoning, HMP-GAE identifies and suppresses semantically inconsistent updates while preserving knowledge alignment among agents throughout federated training. HMP-GAE is implemented using Qwen-2.5 and Llama-3.2 as pretrained FedLLM backbones and evaluated on AG News and Yahoo! Answers datasets. Compared to existing defenses, HMP-GAE improves global accuracy by 1.21 %, reduces final-model clas-sification semantic entropy by 44 .08 %, and lowers perplexity by 13 .34 %. HMP-GAE outperforms the GAE-based defense by improving the accuracy 1.39 %, reducing the semantic entropy by 54 .5%, and lowering perplexity by 35 .5%.

By integrating federated learning (FL) with LLMs, edge nodes can perform local reasoning, decision-making, and adaptation based on their contextual data while sharing high-level knowledge or model updates rather than raw information [3]–[5]. This enables a new generation of self-organizing, privacy-preserving, and resilient Cy-berEdge networks that can support complex, real-time ap-plications, e.g., autonomous mobility, digital twin synchro-nization, smart healthcare, and disaster response. Federated LLMs (FedLLMs) act as cognitive coordinators, learning human-aligned behaviors, negotiating across agents, and dynamically optimizing communication, computation, and sensing processes under constrained resources [6]–[9]. This synergy enhances situational awareness, interoperability, and trust across heterogeneous edge environments, paving the way to autonomous, explainable, and resilient artificial intelligence (AI) infrastructures at the network edge. The nature of FedLLMs empowers each agent to integrate and reason across diverse data features, dynamically adapt its learning strategy to evolving local contexts, and coordinate with other agents based on shared semantics, trust levels, and task interdependencies [10]–[13]. Through iterative aggre-gation and local fine-tuning, FedLLMs collectively evolve into a distributed intelligence capable of robust language understanding, cross-modal reasoning, and context-aware decision-making in heterogeneous and privacy-sensitive CyberEdge environments. When these adversarial updates are aggregated, the global model’s latent graph and contextual alignments be corrupted, leading to systemic manipulations that produce confident yet false textual descriptions, visual interpretations, or sensor-based inferences. Over multiple communication rounds, these ma-nipulations can cascade through the network, undermining the collective intelligence of FedLLMs. For instance, a malicious agent in a vehicular CyberEdge network might cause an LLM to create nonexistent obstacles from camera feeds, while in healthcare or industrial IoT systems, fabri-cated insights could misguide diagnostics or trigger unsafe automated actions. Moreover, the distributed and privacy-preserving nature of FedLLMs amplifies the impact of Byzantine attacks, since each agent trains locally on private data, and central-ized verification or direct inspection of training samples is infeasible. A single compromised agent can influence others through conversational interactions, aggregation of model updates, or semantic alignment mechanisms, gradually in-ducing large-scale semantic drift in the collective model. Such drift erodes the interpretability, factual consistency, and ethical resilience of the FedLLMs. This paper presents a new Byzantine-resilient strategy to mitigate Byzantine attacks in FedLLMs that distort semantic consistency and induce factual misalignment in LLMs. The strategy introduces a self-healing mechanism within the federated aggregation process to recognize and neutralize adversarial model updates before they propagate semantic corruption across FedLLMs. A problem is cast to maximize task accuracy of the LLMs while simultaneously identifying and suppressing manipulated updates. This fil-ters for trustworthy and semantically coherent knowledge, thereby improving the learned representations. To adaptively enhance the resilience of FedLLMs against latent Byzantine-inducing perturbations, we design the 

Hypergraph Message Passing Graph Autoencoder (HMP-GAE) framework . In HMP-GAE, at the server each agent’s model update that is fine-tuned by low-rank adaptation (LoRA) is represented as a node within a hypergraph, where hyperedges capture high-order semantic and contextual relationships among multiple agent updates, as opposed to simple pairwise correlations. The server performs iterative message passing across these hyperedges to refine the embeddings of benign updates, allowing collective rea-soning that effectively identifies and suppresses corrupted or semantically inconsistent contributions. By leveraging hypergraph structures, HMP-GAE distills reliable relational knowledge from trustworthy agents and historical check-points, ensuring robust aggregation that maintains factual coherence and mitigates adversarial manipulation. II. R ELATED WORK 

This section reviews the existing studies on Byzantine attacks mitigation and detection in FL. Our contributions over the existing Byzantine-resilient models are presented. 

A. Byzantine-resilient Strategies 

The authors in [18] developed a communication-efficient and Byzantine-tolerant FL in CyberEdge networks. It com-bined sign-refined momentum updates with error com-pensation to reduce communication costs and stabilize convergence. Edge servers used cosine similarity to assess trust and suppress unreliable local updates. The cloud server combined real and dummy data projections through projection consensus for global aggregation. Layer-wise cosine similarity was used in [19] to detect malicious gradients against a gradient median benchmark. A proactive defense was formulated to constrain divergence among benign gradients, while trust scores reduce misjudgments under non-IID data. In [20], a Byzantine-resilient FL was studied for improv-ing global model consistency. An asynchronous common subset protocol was presented, where benign users can aggregate the model updates despite conflicting messages from malicious users. Dual-domain trust scoring was used to evaluate consensus behavior, similarity among model updates, and stability across rounds to limit malicious influence. Shen et al. [21] presented a Byzantine-resilient FL, which can distinguish malicious model updates through discrepancies in local models’ feature representations. A reference model trained on public data was utilized to quantify these discrepancies as feature fingerprints. These fingerprints were clustered to filter out malicious models before aggregation. Two FL schemes were developed in [22] to enhance resilience against Byzantine users while preventing eaves-droppers from learning users’ private data. Both used a small representative dataset at the server and a discrimina-tor function to suppress malicious contributions. Lagrange coded computing was combined with re-randomization to protect privacy, while communication costs were reduced through a trusted third party. Moreover, a sparsity-guided refinement scheme could be added to mitigate Byzantine attacks [23]. Sparsity was utilized to derive significance distribution vectors that expose subtle malicious changes in client model updates. These vectors are clustered to com-pute weighted similarity scores for group resilience. The resulting groups guide further refinement of raw updates before robust aggregation. Bray-Curtis dissimilarity was introduced in [24] to mea-sure element-wise differences among users’ gradients. Their method can reduce the misclassification of benign gradients against Byzantine attacks under non-IID data distributions. A lattice-based fully homomorphic encryption scheme was used to protect local gradients while enabling secure aggre-gation and Byzantine user detection. The authors in [25] formulated a Byzantine-resilient FL scheme combining homomorphic encryption with gradient dimension com-pression. The Johnson-Lindenstrauss transformation was applied to reduce gradient dimensions while preserving geometric relationships needed for Byzantine defense. Existing Byzantine-resilient aggregation rules were adapted in [26]. A shared, updated consensus dataset was introduced to provide a common benchmark for applying these rules and reaching agreement. A dual-layer privacy shielding strategy was designed to protect local models while preserving global model accuracy. A Byzantine-resilient FL framework was developed in [27], combining client-side and server-side defenses. Clients used a model-level penalty loss to limit divergence from benign models and reduce correlation with malicious model updates, which can help prevent benign users from being misclassified. The server combined manifold-based analysis with a maximum-clique strategy to strengthen Byzantine detection. Wang et al. [28] presented a Byzantine-resilient scheme to defend FL against poisoning attacks while protecting user privacy. A two-server architecture used secure two-party computation to analyze local model gradients. A security protocol was designed to normalize users’ model gradients, compute their cosine similarities, and identify malicious model updates. In [29], a two-stage defense framework was presented for Byzantine-resilient FL. Malicious users can be detected through model similarity. A validation mechanism was then used to estimate similarity among users’ local data to refine detection decisions. A weight recovery mechanism was also applied to adjust the aggre-gation weights of users identified as Byzantine. In [30], dimensionality reduction was adopted to accel-erate the private computation of complex filtering rules in Byzantine-resilient FL. An adaptive tuning strategy was formulated to limit the influence of malicious model up-dates that evade filtering in the reduced space. Moreover, the authors in [31] introduced a correction term during local training to reduce model inconsistency. A secure protocol deployed across two servers enabled Byzantine-resilient aggregation while protecting user privacy, which was expected to limit the privacy computation burden on resource-constrained users. Herath et al. [32] developed a dual-server protocol to protect local model updates without encryption or key exchange. A group-wise filtering mechanism used deviation scores and client credits to isolate Byzantine users, where a reward-penalty system can promote fair participation throughout training. To address heavy-tailed gradient noise, the authors in [33] combined distributed stochastic heavy-ball optimization with update normalization and gradient clipping. Nearest-neighbor mixing through random projec-tions was used to isolate the impact of Byzantine users. An internal auditor was built in [34] to examine gradient similarity and distribution to distinguish benign model up-dates from malicious ones. It combined a Gaussian mixture model with Mahalanobis distance for Byzantine-tolerant aggregation. A three-server computation was developed to perform Byzantine-resilient aggregation [35], where each server held two of the three shares for privacy protection. A 4

top-k protocol was invoked to measure the cosine distance and compute the scores to select benign local models. 

B. Our Contributions 

Existing Byzantine-resilient techniques primarily address Byzantine attacks in FL. Their defenses employ robust ag-gregation, model update similarity, trust evaluation, and se-cure computation to identify malicious contributions. How-ever, Byzantine resilience in FedLLMs has not been specif-ically addressed. Our work aims to investigate Byzantine-resilient FedLLMs, focusing on how malicious updates dis-rupt semantic consistency and knowledge alignment during federated aggregation. Moreover, our proposed HMP-GAE framework for Byzantine-resilient FedLLMs enables each user’s LoRA up-date to form a hypergraph node, while hyperedges capture semantic relationships among multiple users. HMP-GAE combines historical checkpoints with iterative hypergraph message passing to learn shared latent representations of these updates. HMP-GAE captures high-order relational inconsistencies that individual model update analysis may overlook. Therefore, the representations are leveraged to identify and suppress semantically inconsistent updates dur-ing aggregation, which enables Byzantine-resilient knowl-edge integration without accessing private local data. HMP and reconstruction jointly capture these cross-agent rela-tionships, while historical consistency provides a temporal reference for assessing suspicious deviations. V. P ERFORMANCE EVALUATION 

This section presents the implementation of HMP-GAE in FedLLMs under Byzantine attacks. The source code for HMP-GAE is available at: https://github.com/GuangLun2000/HMP-GAE. 

A. Experimental Implementation 

The experimental evaluation is conducted on a Linux workstation equipped with an NVIDIA RTX PRO 6000 Blackwell GPU with 96 GB of memory. The framework is implemented in Python 3.12 with PyTorch 2.10. Two representative text classification datasets [41] are adopted to evaluate the proposed HMP-GAE framework:  

> •

The first is the AG News dataset , which consists of four topical categories, namely World, Sports, Busi-ness, and Sci/Tech, containing 120,000 training sam-ples and 7,600 test samples.  

> •

The second one is the Yahoo! Answers dataset , a large-scale corpus for topic classification that includes 10 categories, with approximately 1.4 million training instances and 60,000 test samples. These datasets differ in label granularity and corpus scale, helping assess the Byzantine-resilient performance under distinct semantic distributions and learning conditions. Two pretrained backbones are deployed at the agents to help examine the effect of model capacity on Byzantine resilience.  

> •

Qwen-2.5 [42] is a model with approximately 0.5 billion parameters, pretrained on 18 trillion tokens with broad multilingual coverage and a context length of up to 32K tokens.  

> •

Llama-3.2 [43] is a model with around 1.2 billion parameters, pretrained on up to 9 trillion tokens from multilingual online data and enhanced through knowl-edge distillation from larger Llama models, with a context length of up to 128K tokens. To enable efficient adaptation of these backbones in the federated setting, we employ LoRA, where trainable low-rank matrices are injected into the attention and feedforward projections while the pretrained weights remain frozen. Consequently, each agent optimizes and uploads only the flattened low-rank update, which constitutes both the com-munication payload and the input to the proposed resilience mechanism at the server. 

B. Performance Metrics 

HMP-GAE is evaluated using three complementary per-formance metrics, namely prediction accuracy, classifica-tion semantic entropy (CSE), and perplexity (PPL), to com-prehensively assess its semantic resilience, language mod-eling capability, and task-specific prediction in FedLLMs.  

> •

Prediction accuracy is adopted to evaluate how pre-cisely a FedLLM can predict the target outputs, i.e., class labels, on unseen data after federated aggre-gation. It is computed as the ratio of correctly es-timated samples to the total number of evaluated samples. A higher estimation accuracy indicates that the aggregated FedLLM can correctly preserve task-specific knowledge learned from distributed agents and produce more reliable predictions under data hetero-geneity and potentially malicious updates.  

> •

CSE is employed to quantify the semantic uncertainty of FedLLMs, which evaluates whether the model as-signs predictive mass consistently to a single meaning. It is the mean Shannon entropy of the predictive class distribution over the evaluation set, where the class la-bels serve as semantic equivalence classes and provide a generation-free surrogate of semantic entropy. Lower CSE indicates that the FedLLM produces semantically stable and confident predictions; higher CSE suggests greater semantic dispersion, uncertainty, or potential Byzantine attacks.  

> •

PPL measures how well the aggregated global model predicts a sequence of tokens. A lower perplexity indicates that the FedLLM assigns higher probability to the correct token sequence, suggesting stronger language understanding, generalization, and more ef-fective aggregation across distributed agents. 

C. Resilience Performance 

The proposed HMP-GAE is compared with three rep-resentative robust-aggregation defenses under an identical federated configuration, where the aggregation rule at the server is the only variable.  

> •

Multi-Krum [44] scores each update by the sum of squared Euclidean distances to its nearest neighbors, and averages the several updates with the lowest scores, thereby retaining only the contributions that reside in the densest region of the parameter space.  

> •

FoolsGold [45] identifies colluding agents by the pair-wise cosine similarity of their historical updates, and attenuates the learning rate of the agents whose update directions are persistently aligned with each other.  

> •

FLTrust [46] maintains a clean root dataset at the server to compute a reference update, and weighs each agent by the cosine similarity between its update and this reference after normalizing the update magnitudes.

In this paper, we proposed HMP-GAE, a resilience framework that mitigates Byzantine-inducing updates in FedLLMs through self-healing federated aggregation. We formulated a defensive optimization problem for benign users to maximize global task accuracy while filtering out malicious, semantically manipulated contributions during aggregation. HMP-GAE represents each agent’s LoRA-fine-tuned model update as a hypergraph node, with hyper-edges capturing higher-order semantic and contextual rela-tionships among multiple updates beyond pairwise correla-tions. The server iteratively passes messages across these hyperedges to refine benign-update embeddings, enabling collective reasoning to identify and suppress Byzantine-inducing or semantically inconsistent contributions. Exper-iments on AG News and Yahoo! Answers using Qwen-2.5 and Llama-3.2 demonstrated that HMP-GAE consistently achieved higher accuracy and lower classification semantic entropy and overall perplexity than the evaluated baselines. Trust-weight analysis further showed that HMP-GAE per-sistently suppressed malicious users’ contributions while preserving benign participation, with benefits extending to benign agents’ local performance.
