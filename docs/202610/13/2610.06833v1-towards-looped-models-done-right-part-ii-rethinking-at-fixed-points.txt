# Overview 

Every recurrence of a looped language model adds cost in training, decoding, prefill, and rein-forcement learning (RL). The closer recurrent states get to fixed points, the less the path to them matters. This enables truncated backpropagation in training; terminal key-value (KV) sharing for decoding with almost no loss in accuracy; a distilled student that prefills up to 1.79 × faster; and RL updates that compute gradients from saved rollout states, 2× faster than backpropagating through the replayed trajectory. We therefore improve the two components of training that shape these fixed points: the depth prior and input injection. Fixed-depth training breaks KV sharing, and Huginn’s broad depth prior supports sharing but dilutes supervision at the target depth more than sharing requires; we learn the prior from prediction feedback, with an entropy term that keeps it broad. Existing injection schemes let the state’s component along the input amplify or cancel the injection; we remove this component with orthogonal injection. From 100 M to 1.6B parameters, the learned prior and orthogonal injection lower perplexity at every scale relative to Huginn’s prior and existing injection schemes, respectively. At 1.6B, the learned prior with a 3× smaller KV cache matches the downstream average of fixed-depth training with the full cache. Learned depth prior

Looped language models repeatedly apply a shared block of layers [1, 2], but whether they should learn fixed points remains contested. One camp forgoes fixed points and keeps the loop count small and fixed [3–5]. The other camp shapes fixed points [6–8] and loops much deeper. In typical implementations, every loop adds cost: activations to store during training, keys and values (KV) to cache during decoding, compute to prefill each prompt, and a replay to score each rollout during reinforcement learning (RL). In this work, we show that fixed points hold the potential to cut each of these costs (Figure 1), which makes deep looped models cheaper at every stage: pretraining, serving, and post-training. The fixed-point equation z⋆ = Fθ(z⋆; x) involves only the endpoint, not the path to it, so once a model’s states are near a fixed point, the endpoint can stand in for the full trajectory (Section 3). Reusing terminal KV caches across loops tests this claim: it barely changes the zero-shot GSM8K accuracy of Huginn [2] but collapses that of Ouro [5] at four loops [9]. The split tracks convergence: Huginn’s states approach fixed points [10]; Ouro’s do not. Similarly, the same fixed point gives each remaining cost a shortcut. In pretraining, backpropagating through only the last few loops approximates the equilibrium gradient [11, 12] without the warm-up that implicit gradients require. In decoding, terminal KV sharing keeps only each prefix token’s terminal KV (Figure 3b) at little cost in performance. In prefilling, a student distilled to predict the fixed point directly replaces the loops (Figure 3c). In RL, scoring rollouts from saved fixed-point states avoids replaying the loops. Each shortcut removes a depth-dependent cost, so its savings grow with every added loop. Looped models already take the first two [2, 5, 13]; fixed points explain why they work and yield the other two. Realizing these benefits requires training that shapes the fixed points. Two components do the shaping, and current choices for each fall short (Section 4). The first is the depth prior , the distribution of training depths. Because tokens converge at different depths (Figure 2), the sampled depth decides whether training sees the converged prefix context that terminal KV sharing supplies at inference (Figure 3a). A model trained at a single depth predicts best there but breaks KV sharing; Huginn’s fixed broad prior [2] supports sharing but dilutes supervision at the target depth more than sharing requires. We learn the prior from prediction feedback, which pulls supervision toward depths that predict well, and add an entropy term that keeps it broad enough for sharing. The second component is input injection [2, 14–16], which adds the input to the state at every recurrence. Huginn and Parcae leave the state’s component along the input unconstrained; depending on its sign, this component amplifies or cancels the injection. Our orthogonal input injection (OrthoInj) removes this component, so every recurrence receives the input at the same strength. • Fixed points explain the two existing shortcuts (Section 3): truncated backpropagation alone shapes the fixed points that terminal KV sharing relies on. Fixed-depth training with full backpropagation through time does not; at 1.6B, sharing drops its GSM8K accuracy from 50 .6 to 21 .2. With tolerant fixed points, sharing cuts the KV cache 3× at five recurrences, keeping the entropy-regularized learned-prior models within 0.4 points of their full-cache downstream average. • Fixed points yield two new shortcuts. Distilled prefill (Section 5.5), trained on a quarter of the teacher’s pretraining tokens, is up to 1.79 × faster on 8K prompts and trails the teacher’s downstream average by 1.0–4.5 points; at matched latency, it scores 0.5–0.9 points above the teacher prefilling with two recurrences. Rollout-state reuse (Section 3.1) halves the scoring and backward time of each RL update relative to replaying the loops with full backpropagation through time, and stays within 1.6 points of that baseline’s GSM8K pass@1. 2• The learned depth prior keeps prediction quality under terminal KV sharing (Section 5.2): from 100 M to 

1.6B, it lowers perplexity and raises the downstream average over Huginn’s fixed prior, and at 1.6B, with a 3× smaller KV cache, it even matches the full-cache downstream average of fixed-depth training. • OrthoInj improves on the strongest injection baseline, Parcae’s (Section 5.3). From 100 M to 1.6B, it trains to a lower loss, lowers validation perplexity by 0.3–1.4% , and raises the downstream average.

3.2 Inference: Endpoint Caching and Distillation 

Decoding: cache only terminal KV banks. The KV cache of conventional looped models grows linearly with recurrence depth. Terminal KV sharing removes this growth: each prefix token keeps only its terminal KV, and every recurrence of later tokens attends to it (Figure 3b). 2 This requires a model that predicts well from terminal context. However, teacher-forced training alone does not match this setting exactly. It updates all tokens of a sequence in parallel, so recurrence r of the current token reads prefix banks from depth r (Figure 3a); sharing instead supplies terminal banks from the first recurrence. This gap is small when the prefix banks change little across depth and the current token’s update contracts, so an error introduced by early context decays over later recurrences. # 4 Improving Fixed-Point Shaping 

The benefits in Section 3 depend on how training shapes fixed points. Two common techniques do this shaping: depth sampling sets where supervision lands, and input injection sets what a fixed point retains about the input. We improve both (Section 4.1 and Section 4.2).

5.5 Distilled Prefill 

We distill the S, M, and L learned-prior models of Section 5.2 into two-block students initialized from the teacher’s recurrent core (Section 3.2) and trained on 1/4 of the teacher’s pretraining tokens. Every prefill path is followed by the same teacher decoding at R = 5 , on the tasks of Table 2. We compare against full teacher prefill ( R = 5 ), teacher prefill stopped after three recurrences ( R = 3 ) or one ( R = 1 ), and the Untied 4 baseline, which prefills and decodes on its own. 

Distilled prefill trades downstream accuracy for 1.5–1.8× faster prefill. On 8K prompts, it trails the teacher’s downstream average by 1.0 (S), 2.3 (M), and 4.5 (L) points (Table 5). At matched latency, it edges out stopping the teacher early: it is 1–4% faster than teacher prefill at R = 2 and 0.5–0.9 points higher on the downstream average. Prefill gains saturate early (Figure 11): prefill at R = 3 stays within 1.6 points of the teacher, but it saves only 21 –29% of the teacher’s latency, against 35 –44% for distilled prefill. A single recurrence is 12 –23% faster than distilled prefill but loses 2.7–3.5× as much downstream accuracy. Better distillation objectives, student architectures such as mixture-of-experts layers, and the allocation of the distillation token budget could widen this margin and close the growing gap to the full teacher; we leave them to future work. 

Teacher initialization drives the student’s quality, and the KL term raises its generation scores. # 6 Related Work 

Looped models and fixed points. Looped language models apply a shared block repeatedly [1] to scale test-time compute [7, 42] and latent reasoning [2, 5]. As depth grows, their states approach fixed points [2, 10], which DEQs solve for directly [14]. Prior work uses these fixed points to change how many recurrences run: it stabilizes them [6, 7, 43], trains supervised states as attractors [8], or halts at convergence [44]. We further identify and verify the efficiency gains that fixed points bring to training, inference, and post-training. 

Looped model efficiency. Near a fixed point, further recurrences barely change the state. We show that this property explains two shortcuts looped models already take and enables two new ones. Truncated backpropagation [2, 13] thus approximates the equilibrium gradient without the warm-up that implicit gradients need [11, 12, 45]. Of the schemes that share KV across recurrences [2, 5, 46], terminal sharing is likewise nearly lossless when training supervises near fixed points. Terminal sharing also opens a shortcut for prefill. Existing looped-model distillation keeps students recurrent (Appendix E.5), and prompt-KV predictors [21, 47] must predict each recurrence’s KV; distilled prefill instead uses a non-recurrent student to predict only the endpoint, from which one teacher recurrence builds every KV bank. Furthermore, fixed-point reuse carries endpoints across computations: it warm-starts solvers on related inputs (Appendix E.4), and we bring it to RL, scoring rollouts from saved states instead of replaying the recurrence [5]. 13 Shaping fixed points. Two choices shape the fixed point: which depths training supervises, and how the input enters each recurrence. Randomized training depths promote path-independent equilibria [42], but existing depth distributions are fixed [2], marginalized over at inference [48], or learned as exits [49, 50]. Closest to ours, Ouro [5] and RL-Halting [51] learn entropy-regularized depth distributions from the recurrent state, but they unroll every training step to the maximum depth and do not target fixed-point shaping (Appendix E.1). Injecting the input into every recurrence makes the fixed point depend on it [2, 14, 52]. Parcae [15] improves injection by forcing contractiveness to stabilize training. We further observe that the carried-over state can have a component along the injected input; OrthoInj projects it out so every recurrence receives the input intact (Appendix E.2). 

# 7 Discussion and Conclusion 

Near a fixed point, the endpoint of a looped language model can replace its recurrent trajectory in training, inference, and post-training. Whether training produces such fixed points depends on the depth prior and on input injection. A learned depth prior lowers perplexity over a fixed PLN prior at the same mean depth while keeping terminal KV sharing intact. OrthoInj projects the carried-over state off the injected input, giving lower perplexity than the injection schemes of Huginn and Parcae. On these models, terminal KV sharing and rollout-state reuse cut memory or time at a small cost in quality, and distilled prefill trades downstream accuracy for prefill latency, with a gap that grows with scale. 

Limitations. We train models of up to 1.6B parameters with one seed per configuration, and our ablations do not exhaust the design space. Larger scales and other model configurations remain untested, and the feasibility of these techniques in real-world applications needs further study.

A.2 Truncated Backpropagation and Implicit Gradients 

For fixed input x, complete recurrent state z = ( H, C ) ∈ Rdz of Section 2, and parameters θ ∈ Rdθ , we connect equation (1) to backpropagation through the final recurrent steps. 

Finite-depth and equilibrium objectives. For a training-depth distribution p, the sampled-depth and equilibrium losses optimize different predictions: 

Ldepth = ER∼p[ℓθ(zR, y )] , Leq = E[ℓθ(z⋆, y )] . (13) 21 Full BPTT differentiates the finite trajectory; implicit differentiation differentiates a local equilibrium branch. Equal states need not have equal derivatives. For Fθ,a (z; x) = az + (1 − a)e with the prelude output 

e = Pθ(x), z0 = 0 , and 0 ≤ a < 1, the equilibrium z⋆ = e is independent of a, whereas zR = (1 − aR)e

depends on it. C.3 Truncation, Depth Sampling, and Fixed Points 

Cost. Training memory grows linearly with the window: peak allocation is 48 , 86 and 124 GB for b = 5 , 10 , 15 

at local batch 8. Full BPTT under PLN-5 must also hold the deepest draw; a depth-30 draw at local batch 8 exceeds device memory, and per-layer recomputation brings it to 28 GiB at about 1.3× the step time of 

b = 10 . At a fixed depth R = 5 , b = 2 trains 1.32 × faster than full BPTT. In a Small PLN-25 model at R = 16 ,windows b = 5 and b = 10 lower saved activations from 3.95 to 1.52 and 2.63 GiB, which stay constant for fixed b once R ≥ b, while the core gradient at b = 10 keeps a median cosine similarity of 0.978 with full BPTT. 

With depth sampling, windows of four or more recurrences match full BPTT. Table 17 varies only the backward window under IID PLN-5 draws. At M, every window b ≥ 4 lies within 1% of full BPTT in WikiText perplexity at R = 5 , on either side of it, while shorter windows lose 1.6% (b = 3 ), 5.5% (b = 2 )and 15% (b = 1 ). At S, b = 5 and b = 10 lie 0.8% and 1.5% above full BPTT. For every window, including 

b = 1 , perplexity at R = 64 is below that at R = 5 and terminal-KV sharing is lossless, so truncation lowers quality without preventing fixed points. 

Truncation as a fixed-point shaper. Table 18 fixes the training depth, so depth sampling cannot shape fixed points. Full BPTT then degrades beyond the training depth and loses 21% perplexity under terminal-KV sharing. The stop-gradient removes credit for iteration-specific computation in the detached prefix: the window is trained only to map whatever state the model reaches to a good prediction, which favors states that further recurrences leave unchanged. Without input injection, the prefix state is the only carrier of the input, so this pressure erases it: truncation then neither fits the training depth nor yields a fixed point. A two-term endpoint Neumann estimate, which uses only the Jacobian at the endpoint, collapses from initialization at every LR, because no fixed point exists early in training. 

Relation to prior uses of truncation. Earlier looped models that extrapolate stably also truncate back-propagation, but in each case together with another source of depth variation. Progressive training detaches a prefix of random length, so the window lands at a random depth, and mixes in a full-depth loss [52]; its motivation, discouraging iteration-specific behavior, matches the mechanism above. The looped transformers of Yang et al. [55] detach all but the last b loops, but grow the loop count by a curriculum and supervise every loop in the window; they find that larger windows help reach a fixed point. Huginn backpropagates through the last eight recurrences of a sampled depth [2]. Both Bansal et al. [52] and Yang et al. [55] also find input injection necessary. Table 18 removes these sources of depth variation: the depth is fixed, the window is the last two recurrences, and full BPTT is the control.

C.5 Terminal KV Sharing at Deeper Recurrence

C.8 Reusing States during Post-Training 

This section specifies the rollout-state reuse estimator of Section 3.1, gives the training protocol, and reports the training cost, uncertainty, and diagnostics behind Section 5.4, and a code RL comparison on MBPP+. Saved states are used only before the optimizer step changes the parameters, and missing, stale, or token-mismatched states are rejected. 

Forward state and gradient estimator. For a saved endpoint zR, token sequence x, and input-conditioned recurrent map Fθ(z; x) of Section 2, Algorithm 1 gives the update. Its only recorded graph is one application of Fθ at the saved endpoint, which includes the prelude through input injection, and ez keeps the forward value zR. Neumann-4 runs one recurrence forward, four backward passes to the state only, and one full backward pass, all through the same graph. The parameter gradient combines

The shared checkpoint in Section 5.4 is the Large learned-prior model of Table 2 (λH = 0 .01 , 343 .6B pretraining tokens). Policy updates and sampling budget. Each arm samples 16 responses per training question once (tem-perature 0.8, 512 -token cap), forming 200 two-question batches. Dr. GRPO centers each question’s rewards within its group of 16 without standard-deviation scaling and divides the summed token loss by the batch size times the completion cap. Updates use a clipping range of 0.2, no reference KL, the recorded rollout log-probabilities as the importance-ratio denominator, and the rollout temperature during replay. Zero-advantage batches skip the optimizer step, and the others receive one on-policy update, so arms with equal sampling budgets can differ in update and token counts. Training uses replay microbatch two and gradient accumulation 16 , so each update uses 32 responses. AdamW uses LR 10 −6, moments (0 .9, 0.95) , ϵ = 10 −8, zero weight decay, gradient-norm clipping at one, and a four-update linear warmup. Evaluation uses the same seed for every endpoint. It draws eight samples per question with the rollout sampler; pass@1 averages the eight outcomes, and pass@8 counts a question as solved if any sample is correct.

D.3 Terminal-KV Evaluation 

Tables 2 and 3 and the Teacher row of Table 5 score every checkpoint at R = 5 with terminal KV sharing, using the battery’s requests and scorers. At R = 5 , a token visits the four physical attention layers twelve times: the prelude and coda once each, and each of the two core blocks five times. The cache keeps one bank per physical layer. Because the prelude and coda run once per token, their banks are exact; each core bank keeps only the final-recurrence KV of earlier tokens. A forward pass over a chunk of new tokens runs all five recurrences; at every core visit, the chunk attends to that layer’s bank and to its own KV from the same visit, and after the final recurrence its KV is appended to the bank. From an empty cache this pass 44 equals the native forward pass and differs only in what it stores; every checkpoint passes this check on MixVal prefixes. Prefill passes every prompt token but the last as one chunk, then the last prompt token alone. For the seven choice tasks, the context is the prompt, and each candidate token is then passed alone, so every scored token reads earlier tokens only through terminal banks. Generation prefills the prompt the same way and decodes one token at a time with the settings of Appendix D.5. For Val. and Wiki, the prompt is the first 4,096 tokens of each 8,192 -token sequence ( 1,216 MixVal and 38 WikiText sequences), and attention stays within documents, as in training. PPL covers the tokens after the prompt that continue a document begun inside it, and the prediction from the last prompt token: 42% of the remaining MixVal tokens, and every WikiText token, since WikiText sequences contain no document boundary. The prompt part of each such document is prefilled, and its later tokens read it through the terminal banks while attending to one another as in training, so Val. and Wiki test sharing of the prefix only. Tables 8 and 13 evaluate the same checkpoints without KV reuse: Val. and Wiki are native PPL over full sequences (Appendix D.1), choice tasks are scored without a cache, and generation uses R = 5 prefill and decoding with twelve visit-specific KV banks and a BOS token.

# E Extended Related Work 

We position the learned depth prior, OrthoInj, terminal KV sharing, rollout-state reuse, and distilled prefill against related work that Section 6 covers only briefly. 

E.1 Learned Depth Distributions and Exits 

Ouro, RL-Halting, and our prior all optimize an entropy-regularized expected objective over depths [5, 51]. Ouro takes the exact expectation with per-token exit gates and reuses the gates for early exit. 45 Closest related work. The closest related work is RL-Halting [51], which also supervises one sampled depth per example and learns the depth distribution with REINFORCE. Its stopping head reads the detached, average-pooled hidden state after each iteration and outputs a hazard πr, the probability of stopping at recurrence r given no earlier stop, so p(R = r | x) = πr

(1 − πr′ ). The reward is the negative task loss with a moving-average baseline and entropy coefficient 0.01 , and inference stops at the most probable depth. Because the hazards depend on the states along the loop, training unrolls every example to its maximum depth of 20 to form the distribution; the sampled depth selects the supervision but not the compute. A hazard that ignores the state is constant and gives a geometric distribution, so the head cannot start from a prescribed prior such as capped PLN. RL-Halting has no budget term and targets length extrapolation on synthetic tasks; it does not evaluate fixed points or KV sharing. Our prior is unconditional and used only in training, giving it three advantages over Ouro and RL-Halting. First, it saves compute: it samples one depth per microbatch before the loop and runs only that depth, and the budget term in equation (6) fixes the expected forward compute at ¯R. Ouro instead runs every step to maximum depth for the exact expectation, and RL-Halting unrolls to that maximum depth to form its state-dependent distribution. Second, it starts from a strong prior: its logits start at the capped PLN prior, so each learned run begins at the fixed-prior baseline, whereas a state-dependent hazard has no setting that reproduces a prescribed prior. Third, it deliberately promotes KV sharing: because the prior does not decide where inference stops, its entropy term extends supervision above ¯R, where settled prefix context supports terminal KV sharing (Appendix C.1). Table 11 compares its inverse-perplexity reward to RL-Halting’s raw CE. Learned exits have two limits for fixed-point training. A single exit distribution both stops inference and weights the per-depth training losses; Popescu et al. [61] trace gate failures mainly to the trajectory this joint training induces, and find that fixed-prior depth supervision yields useful trajectories. An exit also stops once prediction suffices, so training need not reach depths beyond prefix convergence, where supervision prepares the model for terminal KV sharing (Section 3.2). Depth uncertainty also learns a global depth distribution, but within a Bayesian objective that marginalizes over depths at inference [48]. 

E.2 Input Injection and Orthogonal Updates 

DEQ-Transformer injects embeddings into Q/K/V; DEQ-TrellisNet injects them before convolutional LSTM gates [14, Section 4]. TrellisNet combines causal convolution, shared depth, and persistent injection [62]. Huginn and Parcae instead inject at the recurrent-core entrance [2, 15]. Orthogonal residual updates [63] also apply a projection inside a residual block, but to the other operand. They keep only the component of a module’s output that is orthogonal to the residual stream, so an update adds new directions without rescaling the stream. OrthoInj (equation (8)) instead projects the carried-over state off the injected input qt and then adds qt, so the component along qt is fixed at every recurrence while the orthogonal part evolves freely. The two constraints act on different quantities: what a block adds, and what the recurrence retains. 

E.3 KV Sharing Across Recurrent Depth 

Huginn reports zero-shot cyclic reuse of a fixed historical-KV budget, with later recurrent visits overwriting earlier entries [2, Section 6.2]. Ouro’s last-loop reuse during decoding largely preserves GSM8K accuracy with a modest MATH-500 [64] decrease, while prefill sharing degrades performance [5, Section 5.4.2, Table 14]. Even with visit-specific prompt banks, decode-time sharing represents the generated tokens by shared banks. 46 MELT instead finds zero scores on its Table 5 reasoning benchmarks for all four combinations of first-/last-loop reuse and preserved/shared prompt caches on Ouro-1.4B-Thinking [65, Appendix B]. Its LightEval setup permits 32 K-token completions. Differences from Ouro’s few-shot CoT evaluation, alongside incomplete reproduction of the original results, leave the discrepancy unresolved [65, Section 4.1 and Appendix F.1]. MELT adapts the model to a shared cache with gated updates, chunk-wise training, a gradual transition, and attention-aligned distillation; it does not directly evaluate Huginn. Under a common GSM8K setup without retraining, Schwethelm et al. [9, Appendix C.1, Table 1] find that one shared KV slot per physical layer leaves Huginn accuracy nearly unchanged at 32 recurrences, while Ouro accuracy collapses at four. A separate first-visit slot recovers most of Ouro’s accuracy. These results suggest that cache compatibility depends on the model; Huginn under MELT’s long-reasoning protocol remains unmeasured. These studies test sharing zero-shot or after adaptation. We instead change only the training depth policy, which decides whether terminal sharing is nearly lossless (Table 2); like them, we do not measure long-form reasoning. 

E.4 Fixed-Point Reuse 

Implicit models reuse a fixed point to warm-start the solver on a related input. DEQ-Flow and StreamDEQ start each video frame from the previous frame’s fixed point [66, 67], and fixed point diffusion models reuse solutions between denoising timesteps [68]. In each case, the reused state initializes a new forward solve. Rollout-state reuse (Section 3.1) instead carries states between the two passes that RL makes over the same tokens, rollout and the policy update. The update scores the sampled tokens from the endpoints saved during rollout, so it does not replay the recurrence, and backpropagates through a single recurrence at each endpoint with a truncated Neumann series (Appendix C.8). 

E.5 Distillation for Looped Models 

Distillation has trained looped students from non-looped teachers [69] and adapted looped models to cheaper KV caches: MELT adapts Ouro to one gated cache per layer with attention-aligned distillation [65], and Looped Latent Attention distills a codec that compresses KV across loops [70]. LoopFormer trains shorter loop trajectories of the same model to match the final states of the full trajectory [71]. In each case, the student still iterates the recurrent block. Distilled prefill instead trains a separate, non-recurrent student to predict the endpoint of a frozen looped teacher and uses it only for the prompt; decoding keeps the teacher’s recurrence. The closest idea is the learned initializer of HyperDEQ, a small network that predicts the starting point of a frozen DEQ’s fixed-point solver [72]. Our student instead replaces the solver: one teacher recurrence from its prediction builds every KV bank that decoding reads under terminal sharing. 47
