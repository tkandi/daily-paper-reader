## Abstract 

Model optimizations help improve inference performance and ac-curacy of ML workflows. However, relying on a single model to perform inference across all data batches often fails to maximize accuracy and thus overall performance. In many cases, alternate models could perform better on specific subsets of data where a primary model underperforms. Our experiments with real ML workflows indeed show that switching models improves workflow accuracy by up to 23%. Yet, current systems lack the ability to adaptively switch be-tween models based on performance, forcing users to manually test models in sequence. We present FlexiFlow , a dataflow system that dynamically switches between alternate models when the current model exhibits low accuracy. FlexiFlow learns to rank models us-ing a novel multi-armed bandit approach that accounts for model runtimes, probability of passing user-defined assertions, and the computational structure of the ML workflow. We show that the standard Thompson sampling approach is insufficient for switch-ing models in ML workflows. In contrast, our proposed approaches are effective and scales to complex real-world ML workflows. Ex-periments show that switching models at runtime while reusing intermediate results provides higher accuracy, but also 48% effi-ciency gain compared to sequential workflow runs. 

## 1 Introduction 

With the increasing use of machine learning in production sys-tems, engineers often encounter practical problems in deploying and maintaining ML models and workflows. Traditionally, models are often learned in notebooks or scripts, serialized (i.e., “pickled”), and embedded within web servers— a.k.a deployed—to expose pre-diction APIs. However, deployment often reveals a range of issues, particularly related to model performance and accuracy. A recurring concern is the issue of soft failures—situations where the model does not crash but delivers incorrect or degraded predic-tions, often due to factors like data drift [16]. These soft failures compromise prediction accuracy and, in turn, the overall effective-ness of the deployed system. When workflows consist of multiple steps and models, such degradations must be promptly identified Channel Picker (CP) OCR Classifier Extract Extract Union ID card image

and addressed. Developers commonly rely on trace logs to iden-tify problematic data batches, but exhaustively testing alternative model configurations is resource-intensive and time-consuming. Consider a deployed ML workflow, visualized in Figure 1, for identity verification in KYC (Know Your Customer) scenarios using identification cards (e.g., driver’s licenses, passports). The workflow processes an image or scan of the ID card and outputs a struc-tured record with fields such as name, date of birth, ID number, and expiration date. Each step in this pipeline—such as image crop-ping, color channel selection, OCR, card classification, filtering, and field extraction—may be implemented using different ML models. For instance, the cropping step could use models like YOLO [38], SSD [32], or Faster R-CNN [39], while OCR could be performed by tools such as Tesseract, EasyOCR, Google Vision API, AWS Textract, or Microsoft Read API. In practice, only one single model is typi-cally selected for each step based on evaluation over training data. However, this static selection often fails to maintain high accuracy across all real-world inputs during deployment. If a single model un-derperforms, the entire workflow may need to be re-executed from the beginning with an alternative model, significantly increasing the management burden and latency. One approach to mitigating soft model failures on specific inputs is to dynamically switch between models based on their perfor-mance. For example, in the OCR step of an ID card processing pipeline, if EasyOCR fails on a given input, the system can switch to an alternative like Tesseract. However, this requires the system to assess relative model performance—not just using aggregate ac-curacy metrics, but by detecting soft failures (e.g., invalid outputs or low confidence) on individual inputs. Even when estimates of model performance are known, a key challenge remains: determin-ing the optimal order in which to try models to minimize latency while maintaining correctness.

Recently, these challenges have been addressed, albeit indepen-dently, in the machine learning domain. Model assertions [28] pro-vide a data-independent way to assess model performance. In the context of ID card processing, such assertions might include checks like validating that the extracted date of birth is a legitimate calen-dar date, ensuring the age is under 120 years, or applying confidence thresholds to predictions. Separately, explore-exploit strategies [42] offer a principled method for determining which configurations to use first. These approaches balance exploiting configurations that have performed well historically with exploring new alter-native configurations, adapting dynamically based on observed rewards. To our knowledge, no existing ML dataflow system in-tegrates both model assertions and explore-exploit strategies to guide model switching decisions. Integrating model assertions with explore-exploit strategies requires a non-trivial coordination of correctness and efficiency, as the system must adapt to assertion violations while maintaining acceptable performance. In this paper, we present FlexiFlow a dataflow system for multi-step ML workflows that learns to perform model selection and switching when models (soft) fail on assertions. FlexiFlow allows a user to configure each step of an ML workflow with multiple model choices. To determine the best model configuration, i.e., com-bination of model choices at each step, we equip FlexiFlow with a Bayesian statistics-based optimizer that samples from the posterior reward distributions of model choices, enabling the estimation of the most effective model configuration for a given input. If a model configuration still fails assertions, to explore alternative configu-rations, FlexiFlow rolls back to a previous model operator and efficiently tests a sequence of new configurations. We demonstrate that a variety of explore-exploit Bayesian strategies are possible within the runtime to enhance performance. Our findings indicate that while the space of all possible workflow configurations can be large, Bayesian strategies can quickly find good configurations.

A common approach to online learning is the multi-armed bandit (MAB), where the goal is to choose among 𝑘 configurations by bal-ancing exploration and exploitation to maximize expected reward. However, FlexiFlow departs from the standard MAB setting in two key ways: First, while there are 𝑘 overall workflow configurations, each config-uration is composed of multiple steps (or operators), and each step has its own set of model choices. This implies that a distinct MAB problem exists at each stage of the workflow. This MAB problem likely includes interactions across the decision at each step, result-ing in a bandit problem that does not fit neatly into the standard contextual or hierarchical bandit structure. Second, unlike traditional MAB settings where each trial is inde-pendent, in FlexiFlow , if a given configuration fails (e.g., violates a model assertion), it should not require restarting the entire work-flow. Instead, the system should be able to ‘rollback’ to the point of failure and selectively modify only the relevant operator choice. For instance, as shown in Figure 2, if a classification step yields incor-rect results, the system should be able to switch classifiers without re-running upstream components like OCR or preprocessing. These distinctions highlight the need for a bandit-based approach that leverages the computational structure of the workflow graph, enabling localized reconfiguration and efficient exploration of the model choice space for improved overall performance. ## 2 The FlexiFlow Execution Model

We begin by reviewing the fundamentals of such a language and its data model, and then introduce two novel programmatic constructs provided by FlexiFlow : operator choices and workflow assertions. ## 4 FlexiFlow ’s Bayesian Online Optimizer 

In this section, we develop a variety of optimizers, each of which uses a Bayesian explore-exploit learning strategy from the multi-armed bandit framework [42]. The optimizers estimate success probabilities and runtimes to minimize total expected runtimes (Equation 6). Below, we provide a brief background on the Bernoulli bandit problem, followed by FlexiFlow online optimizers. 

Background. Consider a scenario where an operator has 𝐾 pos-sible choices. Each action 𝑎 ∈ { 1, . . . , 𝐾 } has an unknown success probability 𝜃 𝑘 meaning that each time action 𝑎 is chosen, it yields a binary reward 𝑟 . 𝑟 is 1 with probability 𝜃 𝑎 (success) and 𝑟 is 0 with probability 1 − 𝜃 𝑎 (failure). The objective of the bandit framework is to learn the success probabilities (𝜃 1, . . . , 𝜃 𝐾 ) through experimen-tation, while maximizing cumulative rewards over 𝑁 trials, where 

𝑁 ≫ 𝐾 .Suppose we have an operator with three possible actions. Lets say over 2005 trials, these actions were selected as follows: Action 1 was chosen 1000 times, yielding 600 successes ( 𝜃 1 = 0.6), Action 2 was chosen 1000 times, yielding 400 successes ( 𝜃 2 = 0.4) , and Action 3 was chosen only 5 times, yielding 2 successes ( 𝜃 3 = 0.4, but with high variance due to the small sample size). Figure 5 illustrates the probability density functions (PDFs) for these estimated success probabilities. The variance in the estimates decreases as the number of trials per action increases. In an 𝜖 -greedy approach, the learning agent explores by sam-pling actions uniformly at random with probability 𝜖 , where 𝜖 is fixed a priori. During exploitation (with probability 1 −𝜖 ), the agent repeatedly selects the action with the highest estimated mean re-ward. For example, if action 1 consistently has a higher mean than actions 2 and 3, it will be chosen repeatedly during exploitation, while during exploration all three actions are sampled uniformly. Allocating an equal number of experimental trials to each action during exploration results in poor performance of an 𝜖 -greedy approach [40]. ## 4.1 FlexiFlow Optimizer for Single Operator with Multiple Choices

FlexiFlow introduces a key difference: when an operator choice fails, alternative choices are repeatedly attempted via rollbacks until a successful configuration is found. These rollbacks introduce additional runtime costs, as each re-execution incurs the overhead of rerunning the operator choice. To address this, we first describe a modified version of Thomson Sampling (TS), termed Operator-level TS (OTS), in which only the reward function is changed and is modeled with expected runtimes of probability of success or failure of the assertions. We consider OTS for a single-operator scenario with multiple choices. We then extend this algorithm to include the computational structure of the workflow in operator and choices along with rollbacks in a multiple operator, multi-choice scenario.

## 4.2 FlexiFlow Optimizers for Multiple Operators with Multiple Choices 

Thus far, we have focused on the case of a single operator, but in practice, workflows consist of multiple operators, each offering several choices. One approach to learning in the multiple operator, multiple choice scenario is to assume operator-level independence and se-lect the best choice for each operator individually. If the resulting configuration fails the assertion, the system proceeds to the next best configuration. For example, in case of ID card processing, we have three op-erators 𝐴 = 𝐶ℎ𝑎𝑛𝑛𝑒𝑙 𝑃𝑖𝑐𝑘𝑒𝑟 , 𝐵 = 𝑂𝐶𝑅 and 𝐶 = 𝐶𝑙𝑎𝑠𝑠𝑖 𝑓 𝑖𝑒𝑟 each with 5, 2, and 2 choices. To start, we determine the best operator choice (lowest expected runtime) independently at each of the three operators. Let’s say configuration 𝐾 1 = [𝐴 3, 𝐵 2, 𝐶 2] where 𝐴 3, 𝐵 2,

𝐶 2, represent choices within their respective operators, is the best operator choice configuration i.e., resulting in the lowest expected runtime of 10s. However, this configuration also fails the asser-tion. If 𝐾 2 = [𝐴 3, 𝐵 2, 𝐶 1] and 𝐾 3 = [𝐴 1, 𝐵 2, 𝐶 1] are other available configuration choices that result in 11s and 10.5s, then per OTS in Listing 1 we will choose configuration 𝐾 3, which has the next lowest runtime. Naively applying OTS at each operator independently has two drawbacks. First, assuming operator level independence may not be true. For example, in ID card processing, a specific channel color may be necessary for EasyOCR engine to successfully extract text but that specific channel always causes Tesseract to fail. Second, choosing each operator independently is not rollback-cost-aware, i.e. in our example, minimizing overall configuration level runtime results in choosing 𝐾 3 as the next best configuration even though the cost of incrementally running 𝐾 2 may be the lowest. Note that 

𝐾 1 and 𝐾 2 share an operator prefix and the rollback cost from 𝐾 1

to 𝐾 2 maybe lower than overall cost of incurring 10.5s for running the full configuration. We fix these two drawbacks next. 

Configuration-level TS (CTS). One option is to relax operator level independence and learn at the entire configuration-level. Ad-ditionally, failed configurations perform a conditional rollback, i.e., rollback to a configuration which is estimated to have the smallest increase in runtime from the current configuration. Listing 2 de-scribes learning at the configuration level using FlexiFlow -based TS where the set of all actions 𝐴 are the set of all 𝐾 configura-tions. For each configuration, we maintain a Beta distribution with parameters 𝛼 and 𝛽 and obtain the estimated runtime of a configu-ration bT. Each configuration is sampled in SelectAction to obtain a configuration with the lowest runtime, conditional on the current best choice. The example is processed with this configuration and actual rewards and operator-level runtimes are observed. The Beta distribution parameters are updated similarly to Listing 1 Line 18, but runtime 𝜇 and 𝜎 are updated for each operator choice in the current best configuration. If the example fails to pass assertions, the next best configuration with the lowest incremental increase in runtime is chosen in SelectAction . Hybrid TS (HTS). A drawback of CTS is that we may have a very large number of configurations, making it difficult to fully explore the configuration space. • OTS (Operator-level Thompson Sampling) maintains probability and runtime parameters at the operator level. This approach does not work for multiple operator, multiple choice workflows.

OTS, HTS, and CTS are equivalent for single operator, multiple choice workflows.

## 5.1 Performance of FlexiFlow optimizers 

In this section, we evaluate whether the optimizers proposed in this paper (OTS, CTS, HTS in Table 1) can lower runtimes for both synthetic and real-world ML workflows. We perform ablation studies by also evaluating OTS-R (OTS without runtime modeling) and CTS-RB (CTS without rollback awareness). 

5.1.1 Performance of optimizers on synthetic workflows. For each synthetic workflow, we first run it multiple times with its optimal sequence of configurations to get a series of optimal runtimes. Due to probabilistic assertions, choices may fail and cause rollbacks. Hence, the runtimes vary across different runs. Next, with a given optimizer, we run the workflow multiple times, with the optimizer updating itself after each run, to get a series of optimizer runtimes. We then compare the series of optimizer runtimes against that of optimal runtimes. Since runtimes vary across each run, we show runtime trends by plotting a sliding-window runtime average. In addition, we plot the cumulative regret curves for each optimizer. Cumulative regret is measured as the cumulative difference between the achieved runtime and the optimal runtime. Plotted as a series, cumulative regret curves show how fast an optimizer approaches the optimal runtime; a good learning strategy will show a “log-like shape" in its cumulative regret curve with a rapidly decreasing slope. Note that because of probabilistic assertions, learning strategies can sometimes get lucky and finish faster than the optimal strategy, leading to negative regret.

This is because OTS-R simply maximizes probability of passing assertions. It learns to run the sequence 𝑐 = [5, 4, 3, 2, 1],but the optimal sequence for SC is 𝑐 = [3, 2, 4, 1, 5] due to different runtimes of choices. The runtime-aware multi-armed bandit OTS

## 5.3 Runtime Performance of FlexiFlow 

Since existing systems lack native support for operator choices, assertions, and rollbacks, the accuracy advantage shown above could be achieved only by running the workflow configurations sequentially. We create this setup by writing Spark and Flink jobs for each of the 20 configurations in the reduced ICP workflow described above. All the cards are input to the workflow configuration with the highest 𝐹 1 score. The outputs are checked with the same assertion as in Figure 6(d). Cards that fail the assertion are input to the next configuration. This iterative process is continued until all the configurations are exhausted, effectively simulating rollback. 

FlexiFlow was evaluated using the same input cards to compare the total processing time. Each system is limited to 10 CPU cores. Each experiment was repeated 5 times. The variation in runtimes was negligible. Figure 12 compares the total runtime for FlexiFlow with se-quential execution of workflow configurations in Spark and Flink. Runtime of each execution in the sequence is shown in a different color for Spark and Flink. The figure shows that FlexiFlow com-pletes the run 48% faster than sequential executions in Flink and 49% faster than those in Spark. The reason for this improvement is that FlexiFlow can reuse intermediate results during rollbacks and can better leverage data parallelism as it can run different configurations for different inputs simultaneously. ## 6 Discussion 

FlexiFlow reduces programmer burden by letting them specify op-erator choices instead of maintaining multiple workflow instances. 

FlexiFlow allows a diversity of choices. FlexiFlow reduces man-agement burden of prioritizing between multiple workflow in-stances using bandit-based strategies. This paper only takes the first step in letting programmers rely on FlexiFlow strategies to pick the right choice at each operator. In future work, the proposed strategies can also take input data characteristics into account to potentially learn different optimal sequences of configurations for data with different characteristics (e.g., cards from different countries in ICP ). FlexiFlow is most effective when failure modes of configurations are uncorrelated. But, failures may not be completely uncorrelated. For example, when a heavy model fails to predict correctly, it might be more likely that the light model will also fail. FlexiFlow strategies do not separately learn conditional probability of passing assertions which may be crucial for such cases. Modeling conditional probabilities without managing large amounts of state is also part of our future work. FlexiFlow strategies currently focus on lowering runtimes. But many batch workflows are more dollar-cost sensitive than they are runtime sensitive, such as call commercial LLMs less often. We believe a similar problem formulation may suffice for reducing dollar costs. 

## 7 Related Work 

Workflow and DataFlow Systems. FlexiFlow is a dataflow sys-tem where the operator order within DAG defines the data depen-dencies. The operators follow pipeline execution in that operators execute as soon as their input data is available. This is similar to Dataflow systems such as MapReduce [13] and others [25, 50]. How-ever these systems do not have support for alternative operator choices, assertions, and rollbacks to improve on overall workflow accuracy. Timely dataflow [35] allows for some form of rollback, but without alternative operator choices. The rollback and incremental computation in FlexiFlow is simi-lar to incremental updates in incremental view maintenance [2, 7, 36, 51], streaming computation [3, 8], and provenance-based selective replay [23]. These systems typically handle updates at the input, such as base table updates in incremental view maintenance or stream appends in streaming computation. In contrast, FlexiFlow 

allows updates at intermediate outputs during rollbacks. 

Programming Models. Operator choices are similar to McCarthy’s ambiguous functions (Amb) [34] where the language runtime tries to pick an alternative to satisfy assertions. Operator choices are

also similar to choice blocks in template programs [4, 41] where programmers just write a template and let an SMT solver choose the right block so that the overall program meets a specification. To the best of our knowledge, FlexiFlow is the first to apply the concept of operator choices within the context of a dataflow system. The target use case of FlexiFlow is different from previous work. 

DataFlow Debugging. Systems such as Amber [29] and those based on Spark including Titian [24], BigSift [20], BigDebug [19], and TagSniff [12] support debugging primitives such as breakpoints (to pause and resume execution) and watchpoints (to inspect inter-mediate data). While these can be used for error detection, often they require manual changes for error correction. Similarly, model assertions perform runtime checks to detect model errors at run-time [28]. However, model assertions work in the context of a single model whereas we detect errors on overall workflows and correct them by rolling back to alternate workflow configurations. 

Online Learning. FlexiFlow strategies build upon Thompson Sampling [40, 42, 43], which samples all actions that could plau-sibly be optimal, while shifting sampling away from those that are unlikely to be optimal. Theoretically, TS has been shown to achieve asymptotically optimal logarithmic regret in stochastic MAB settings [40]. While a formal regret analysis for our approach is beyond the scope of the current paper, experimentally we benefit from learning the right choices. We also note that, while there are situations that do not require exploitation or exploration, or situ-ations where it is better to exploit a high performing suboptimal action than to invest resources exploring actions, often modeling such situations depends upon availability of some high-quality historical data. We currently do not assume access to any such historical data and we learn in an online setting. 

Confidence Estimation. When the assertion in FlexiFlow is a confidence threshold (as in IC , SCQA , LCQA ), our system is related to the work done in confidence estimation which is applied to, e.g., out-of-distribution detection and selective prediction. Examples of such work include Bayesian neural networks, ensemble of neural networks, and temperature scaling (see, e.g., the surveys [17, 18]. Unlike our work, these do not attempt to mitigate the runtime cost of the ensemble by learning an optimal sequence of models to try. 

## 8 Conclusion 

We showed that in many ML pipelines, relying on a single model to perform inference across all data batches often fails to maximize accuracy and thus overall performance. Our FlexiFlow system, incorporates operator choices, assertions, and rollbacks and uses a novel multi-armed bandit approach to perform automatic model switching and thus improves overall accuracy and efficiency of vari-ous ML pipelines such as document processing, image classification, and question-answering tasks. Moving forward, our approach opens new avenues for AutoML-based systems that autonomously adapt to evolving data distribu-tions. Future work will explore incorporating intermediate data characteristics, such as the country of an ID card, into the learning strategies. Ultimately, FlexiFlow represents a step toward more intelligent, data-aware workflow systems that reduce manual inter-vention and enhance the reliability of ML-driven automation.
