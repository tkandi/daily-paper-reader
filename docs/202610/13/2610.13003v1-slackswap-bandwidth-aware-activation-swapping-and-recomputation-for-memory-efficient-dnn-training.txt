## Abstract 

Swapping saved activations to host memory let GPUs train models larger than their memory when compute and the host link were roughly balanced: on a 2015 Titan X, PCIe could copy every VGG-16 activation out while the forward pass ran. That balance is gone. From the Titan X to the H100, peak training FLOP/s grew 141 ×

while the host link grew 3.9 ×, and on an A100 the link can hide only 11–38% of the activations of the transformers we study. Swap policies such as vDNN, SuperNeurons and SwapAdvisor still push whatever the memory budget demands through the link and stall; block checkpointing leaves the link idle. We present SlackSwap, which treats link time as a budget. From one profiled iteration, SlackSwap spends the forward-pass link budget on the tensors that are most expensive to recompute, covers the remaining deficit by recomputing the cheapest ones, and runs FIFO offloads and earliest-deadline-first prefetches with no per-layer barriers. In a trace-driven simulation of BERT-Large, GPT-2 XL and a Llama-7B-style model on an A100, SlackSwap keeps 96–100% of the throughput of an unlimited-memory GPU at half the activation memory, 1.10–1.11 ×

the best baseline, stays within 0.5% of a genetic search over 6,600– 14,000 plans, and plans in under 0.4 s.

## 1 Introduction 

A GPT-2 XL training step over 8K tokens saves 22 GB of activations for the backward pass, almost as much as the 25 GB of weights, gra-dients and Adam states of its 1.5B parameters. When saved activa-tions exceed device memory, a system must free them between their last forward use and their first backward use, in one of two ways. 

Swapping copies a tensor to host memory over the CPU–GPU link and copies it back before it is read [1–3]; unified memory does this on demand, through page faults [4]. Recomputation drops the ten-sor and re-runs the operator that produced it [5–7]; ZeRO-Offload takes this route for activations while it offloads model states [8], and POET combines both for edge devices [9]. Swapping is attractive because a copy that overlaps computation is free. vDNN made this case in 2016: on a Titan X with 12.8 GB/s of PCIe 3.0, the link could move every feature map of VGG-16 while the forward pass ran, and swapping cost 1–18% [1]. The balance has since moved. From the Titan X (FP32) to the H100 (BF16), peak training FLOP/s grew 141 × while the host link grew 3.9 ×, from PCIe 3.0 to PCIe 5.0 [10]. Figure 1 shows the result: the share of saved activations that the link can copy out during the forward pass fell from 100% to 9–27%. Most swap policies still treat the link as able to absorb whatever the memory budget demands. They choose tensors by layer type [1, 2] or by reuse distance [3], and none caps the swapped bytes at what the link can hide. In our simulation of an A100 at half the activation memory, vDNN, SuperNeurons and SwapAdvisor run GPT-2 XL at 36%, 66% and 43% of the throughput of a GPU with unlimited memory, because their copies sit on the critical path. Block checkpointing avoids stalls but leaves the link idle and re-runs 13% of the step. Two quantities decide what to do with each saved tensor. The link budget is the number of bytes the link can copy while computation runs; copies inside it are free. The recompute cost per byte is the critical-path time spent rebuilding a tensor, divided by its size. On an A100, rebuilding costs 1–11 ps per byte, while an exposed PCIe 4.0 copy costs 40 ps per byte (Figure 2). Every byte beyond the link budget should therefore be recomputed, and the budget should go to the bytes that are most expensive to recompute. We build this into SlackSwap, a planner and runtime policy for activation swapping. From one profiled iteration, Pass 1 fills the forward-pass link budget with the tensors that cost most to recompute, admitting a tensor only if FIFO offloads and earliest-deadline-first (EDF) prefetches still meet their deadlines under the memory budget. Pass 2 walks the resulting memory profile and covers every over-budget point with the cheapest recomputation. At run time, two DMA queues move data without per-layer barriers, so computation waits only for a missing input or for memory. This paper makes three contributions: 

• A link-budget model: the fraction of activations a link can hide is min (1, 𝐼 /𝛽 ), a workload’s forward FLOPs per saved byte over a machine’s FLOP/s per link byte/s. On an A100 it is 11–38% for the transformers we study. 

• SlackSwap’s two-pass planner, which runs in under 0.4 s, and its barrier-free runtime policy.

## 2 Background and Motivation 2.1 Two ways to free a saved activation 

Training saves, for every operator, the tensors its backward pass reads. With FlashAttention [11], a GPT-style layer saves 34 bytes per token per hidden unit in BF16 [12]: the inputs of the four GEMMs (one of them the attention output), Q/K/V, the GeLU input, two LayerNorm inputs and two dropout masks. A saved tensor 𝑡 is produced at time 𝑟 𝑡 in the forward pass and next read at 𝑑 𝑡 in the backward pass. In between, it can be kept ; swapped , copied device-to-host (D2H) after 𝑟 𝑡 and host-to-device (H2D) before 𝑑 𝑡 on DMA engines that run beside the compute stream; or recomputed , dropped after its last forward reader and rebuilt before 𝑑 𝑡 by re-running its producer on the compute stream. vDNN [1] offloads the inputs of every layer (vDNN 𝑎𝑙𝑙 ) or of convolutions (vDNN 𝑐𝑜𝑛𝑣 ), overlapping each copy with the layer that reads it and holding the next layer until the copy ends; in the backward pass it prefetches the closest preceding offloaded layer. SuperNeurons [2] recomputes cheap layers, offloads convolution outputs through an LRU tensor cache when memory runs out, and prefetches one checkpoint ahead. SwapAdvisor [3] evicts the tensor whose next use is furthest away [13], issues copies as early as possible, and searches operator orders and memory-pool layouts with a genetic algorithm.

## 2.3 What should happen to the rest? 

A byte that does not fit the link budget can still be copied, with the copy exposed, or it can be recomputed. Figure 2 compares the two costs for one GPT-2 XL layer on an A100. Rebuilding costs 1.0– 1.3 ps per byte for elementwise outputs and 5.3–10.6 ps for GEMM, attention and residual tensors; an exposed copy costs 1 /𝑊 : 83, 40 and 20 ps per byte on PCIe 3.0, 4.0 and 5.0. Recomputation wins for every tensor, by 1.9–87 ×. Swapping pays only inside the link budget, and there each swapped byte saves that byte’s recompute cost, so the budget is best spent on tensors that are expensive or impossible to rebuild: block inputs, the residual stream and GEMM outputs. The swap policies above neither cap swapped bytes at the link budget nor rank tensors by recompute cost: they pick tensors by layer type or reuse distance and swap as many bytes as the memory budget requires. Block checkpointing [5] re-runs whole blocks, paying GEMM time to rebuild elementwise bytes that cost 4–11 × less to recompute alone, and never uses the link.

## 3.3 Pass 2: recompute the cheapest bytes 

Pass 2 (lines 7–12) computes the memory profile 𝑚 (𝑖 ) over the oper-ator sequence. A keep tensor occupies memory from production to its last use; a swap tensor until its offload ends (and its last forward reader is done) and again from its first backward use; a recomp ten-sor until its last forward reader and again once rebuilt. ## 3.4 Validation, repair and runtime 

Validation and repair. The memory profile ignores second-order effects: prefetches that wait for memory, rebuild chains, and tran-sient buffers at the forward/backward boundary. SlackSwap there-fore replays the plan once in an event model of the iteration (in deployment, one dry-run step traced with a tool such as PASTA [16]) and repairs what it observes. A tensor whose prefetch held up the compute stream is recomputed if that is cheaper than the wait, or else removed from 𝑆 before re-planning; lagging offloads raise 𝑔 ;a plan that does not fit is re-planned for a budget 1%, 2%, 4%, . . . smaller. SlackSwap keeps the fastest plan seen in at most six rounds, including the plan with no swaps. 

Runtime. The runtime uses two copy streams. A D2H copy starts as soon as its tensor is produced; saved tensors are read-only, so the copy overlaps the operators that still read it, and the device buffer is released once both the copy and the last forward reader are done. When the backward pass starts, H2D copies are queued in deadline order, and each is admitted when used memory, its size and the allocations of the next three operators fit in 𝑀 . No operator waits on a copy it does not need: there is no per-layer barrier. If the compute stream lacks memory while no copy is in flight, the runtime frees device copies of swapped tensors that already have a host copy, furthest next use first, and queues them again. Rebuild operators run immediately before their first backward reader. In PyTorch, these mechanisms map onto saved-tensor pack/unpack hooks, pinned host buffers and two CUDA streams; this paper evaluates the policy in simulation.

## 4.5 Sensitivity 

Link bandwidth. Figure 6a sweeps the link for GPT-2 XL at 30% of the peak. SlackSwap moves from 1.6 GB swapped and 14.6 GB recomputed at 12 GB/s to 16.7 GB swapped and nothing recomputed at 200 GB/s, staying 0–12 points above the best baseline. Swap-centric policies need 100–450 GB/s per direction, 4–18 × PCIe 4.0, before they beat checkpointing. 

Sequence length. Attention FLOPs grow with sequence length while saved bytes per token do not, so 𝐼 and 𝑓 grow: for the Llama-7B model, 𝑓 rises from 36% at 2K tokens to 60% at 32K (Figure 6b, 16K tokens per batch, 32K at the last point). ## 5 Related Work 

Swapping. vDNN [1], TFLMS [24], SuperNeurons [2] and Swa-pAdvisor [3] offload activations (and, for SwapAdvisor, parame-ters) to host memory; FlashNeuron [25] offloads to SSDs and Sen-tinel [26] places tensors across heterogeneous memory. Demand paging through unified memory is slower than explicit copies [1, 27]; recent work characterizes oversubscribed memory manage-ment for deep learning training [4] and improves UVM management and prefetching [23, 28]. ZeRO-Offload and ZeRO-Infinity [8, 29] of-fload model states, and HybridGen [30] splits LLM inference across the CPU and GPU. The swapping systems above size swaps by memory pressure; SlackSwap sizes them by link time. 

Recomputation. Chen et al. [5] checkpoint every √𝑛 layers; Check-mate [6] finds optimal schedules with integer programming; DTR [7] Anonymous 

evicts greedily at run time; Megatron-LM recomputes selected at-tention operators [12]. 

Hybrids. Capuchin [31] chooses between swapping and recom-putation per tensor from access patterns observed at run time; POFO [32] combines them optimally for chains with dynamic pro-gramming; POET [9] solves a mixed-integer program for energy on edge devices. SlackSwap is a closed-form, millisecond planner built around one observation: on compute-rich GPUs the link budget is small and explicit, and it belongs to the tensors that are most expensive to rebuild. 

## 6 Conclusion 

GPU compute has outgrown the host link by more than an order of magnitude since swapping was proposed, and an A100 can now hide only 11–38% of a transformer’s saved activations. SlackSwap plans for this directly: it spends the link budget on the tensors that are most expensive to recompute and recomputes the rest, and its runtime keeps every copy off the critical path. In simulation it holds 96–100% of ideal throughput at half the activation memory and reaches budgets that block checkpointing cannot. The same analysis says when swapping becomes the main tool again: with coherent links of hundreds of GB/s or with long sequences, SlackSwap shifts on its own from recomputation to swapping.
