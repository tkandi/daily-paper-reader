# ABSTRACT 

In decentralized federated learning, heterogeneous clients may develop complementary expertise, yet each query is typically evaluated only by the client where it arrives. To exploit this distributed expertise without global coordination, we formulate inference-time collaboration as budgeted local expert referral, a graph-constrained routing process in which a query can move only between neighboring clients and only visited clients contribute to its final prediction. EdgeDRM implements this framework by learning a density-based routing potential for each client: differences between neighboring potentials provide the comparisons used to select successive referrals, while local task models generate predictions that are aggregated along the resulting route. Both training and inference are decentralized, using only neighbor-local information without a central router or graph-wide client scoring. We establish estimation guarantees for the learned comparisons and characterize how local improvement and graph structure govern prior-weighted density gains from referral. Experiments on benchmark and real-world medical data show that EdgeDRM outperforms graph-matched decentralized baselines in most settings, with the clearest benefits under sparse connectivity. 

# 1 Introduction 

Federated learning (FL) enables multiple clients to train a shared model without centralizing their raw data. Convention-ally, a server distributes the model and aggregates locally computed updates (McMahan et al., 2017). In decentralized FL (Lalitha et al., 2019), clients form a communication graph whose nodes represent clients and whose edges specify which pairs can exchange model information directly, with no central server coordinating the network. The graph determines which clients can collaborate during training, but at inference a query is typically evaluated only by the client where it arrives. This inference limitation becomes consequential when client data distributions differ, a setting known as statistical heterogeneity. Consequently, a model may perform well on queries from its local distribution but poorly on those from other clients. Such variation in local performance creates an opportunity for inference-time collaboration: a query that is poorly handled by its entry client may be better handled by another client. For instance, a patient may first visit a local hospital that lacks the expertise needed for the case, even though a better-suited specialist is available elsewhere in the hospital network. Reaching that specialist, however, may require a sequence of local referrals because each physician can refer the patient only to nearby or affiliated hospitals, which may in turn refer the patient onward. Each referral incurs delay and cost, while the referral path determines which specialists can ultimately be reached. We call the analogous inference process in decentralized FL local expert referral : a query enters at an arbitrary client, may traverse only a limited number of communication edges, and receives predictions only from the clients it visits. Here, a referral is one neighbor-to-neighbor transfer, routing is the process that selects successive referrals, and the route is the resulting sequence of visited clients. Unlike global expert selection, local expert referral is sequential and path-dependent: both the referral budget and the graph topology restrict which experts a query can reach. This raises our central question:

Existing work addresses only individual parts of this problem. Personalized and decentralized FL methods determine how clients collaborate during training, but typically leave each test query at the client where it arrives (Sun et al., 2023; Zantedeschi et al., 2020; Dai et al., 2022; Lin et al., 2025; Li et al., 2025). Federated routing and mixture-of-experts methods exploit specialization through global client scores, learned routing gates, or expert subsets, but do not model budgeted neighbor-to-neighbor query movement (Wang et al., 2026a; Reisser et al., 2021; Farhat et al., 2025). Graph-based referral and search methods support local query movement, but learn from observed referral outcomes or graph-wide search supervision rather than from the static datasets held by neighboring FL clients (KhudaBukhsh et al., 2016; Baranchuk et al., 2019). Together, these lines leave open how to learn query–client compatibility from decentralized client data and compose neighbor-local comparisons into an effective prediction path. See Appendix A for details. Addressing this gap requires overcoming two coupled difficulties specific to decentralized routing. First, query–client comparisons must be learned without the graph-wide normalization used by centralized routers such as FedDRM (Wang et al., 2026a). Second, neighbor-local comparisons must remain coherent when composed along a multi-hop referral path. We therefore propose EdgeDRM , a decentralized density-ratio routing framework. It avoids graph-wide normalization by learning each edge comparison through balanced endpoint source classification. To make these comparisons composable, 

EdgeDRM assigns each client one routing potential shared across its incident edges. Under a common representation, the resulting differences accumulate to the potential difference between the entry client and each reached client. We also characterize discrepancies caused by different local representations. At deployment, each client compares only its neighbors using its own query representation and stored neighbor coefficients, requiring neither a global score vector nor predictions from unvisited clients. This work makes four contributions: • We formulate inference-time collaboration in decentralized FL as budgeted local expert referral, in which queries move only between neighbors and only visited clients contribute to prediction. • We develop EdgeDRM , a neighbor-local framework that replaces graph-wide normalization with balanced endpoint source classification. One potential per client makes edge comparisons coherent across hops, and history-weighted aggregation uses only visited clients. • We show that, under a shared representation, EdgeDRM learns increasingly accurate neighbor comparisons as training data and optimization steps increase. When locally better neighbors consistently guide the route, these comparisons yield cumulative gains in query–client compatibility and lead to the globally best-matched client given sufficient referral budget. • Across benchmark, scaling, tiered-hospital, and preliminary real-world medical evaluations, EdgeDRM outperforms graph-matched decentralized baselines in most settings, with clear gains on sparse graphs but no consistent advantage under complete connectivity. 2Learning to Refer PREPRINT

# 3 Decentralized Training and Local Expert Referral

# 5 Experiments 

5.1 Experimental setup and evaluation Benchmarks and topologies. We first evaluate on CIFAR-10, CIFAR-20, and CIFAR-100 benchmarks under 2 non-IID partitions. In the shards-per-client (SPC) partition based on (McMahan et al., 2017), each client receives five equal-sized, label-homogeneous shards for CIFAR-10 and 25 for CIFAR-20/100. In the Dirichlet partition, each client’s class proportions are drawn from a Dirichlet distribution with concentration 0.3. The main benchmark has eight clients. We consider four communication topologies: clients are connected as a P ATH , a C YCLE , a connected random graph in which every client has four neighbors (RR4), or a C OMPLETE graph. These are standard decentralized communication topologies and are ordered by increasing connectivity. For further details on the benchmarks, graph topologies, and data construction, see Appendix D.1. 

Training and inference. We train randomly initialized ResNet-18 models for 800 communication rounds using 

DFedAvgM (Sun et al., 2023), freeze each local encoder, and then fit the personalized classifier heads and routers for 200 rounds. The routing objective weights the two endpoints of every edge equally. At inference, EdgeDRM starts from a uniformly sampled non-source entry, excludes revisits, and averages predictions from visited experts using temperature-one score weights. 

Comparisons and reporting. At test time, each query from source client s is assigned uniformly at random to an entry client other than s. Local trains one model per client using only local data and predicts with the model at that entry. The decentralized baselines DFedAvgM (Sun et al., 2023), DisPFL (Dai et al., 2022), and FedSPD (Lin et al., 2025) also predict with their learned model at that entry. By contrast, we include centralized FedDRM (Wang et al., 2026a) only as an illustrative reference because its client-selection head scores all clients globally and therefore cannot operate under the neighbor-only communication constraints of our decentralized setting. EdgeDRM-NR uses the same trained model but keeps each query at its entry client.

# 6 Conclusion and Limitations 

EdgeDRM formulates inference-time collaboration in decentralized federated learning as budgeted local expert referral. A query moves only between neighbors on the communication graph, and only visited experts contribute to its final prediction. Neighbor-local density-ratio comparisons make referral trainable without a central router or global expert scoring, while our analysis relates referral gains to comparison accuracy and graph structure. Across six CIFAR settings, EdgeDRM ranks first among graph-matched decentralized methods in 16 of the 18 sparse-graph comparisons, with a mean accuracy difference of +3 .61 percentage points relative to the strongest competing method in each comparison. The C OMPLETE graph gives EdgeDRM its highest absolute accuracy in every setting, but its mean advantage over DFedAvgM disappears ( −0.49 points). Together, these results establish graph-constrained referral as a distinct inference setting and a useful mechanism under sparse connectivity, while showing that greater connectivity can improve absolute accuracy without increasing the relative benefit of learned referral. Closing the gap between the theory and learned neural representations remains future work.

# A Related Work 

Federated routing and mixtures of experts. FedDRM is the closest starting point for our work. It learns a globally indexed source-classification head and asks a server to compare all clients for every query (Wang et al., 2026a). FedSPM 

instead uses a semiparametric mixture to represent inter- and intra-client heterogeneity (Wang et al., 2026b). Federated mixture-of-experts methods also construct specialized predictors: FedMix adapts a client-specific subset of experts, whereas DDOME jointly trains gates and experts for on-the-fly selection (Reisser et al., 2021; Farhat et al., 2025). These methods establish the value of specialization, but they do not address our information constraint: at inference, no server or client can evaluate a global gate. EdgeDRM replaces global selection with a sequence of graph-supported comparisons and forms its prediction only from the experts reached by that sequence. 

Personalized and decentralized federated learning. Personalized FL decides which information clients should share during training. pFedGraph learns a weighted collaboration graph at a server (Ye et al., 2023). Fully decentralized methods learn personalized models and sparse collaboration graphs, exchange sparse model masks, optimize budgeted collaborator selection, or form soft clusters through peer-to-peer updates (Zantedeschi et al., 2020; Dai et al., 2022; Kharrat et al., 2025; Lin et al., 2025). DFedAvgM combines local momentum with neighbor model averaging (Sun et al., 2023), while decentralized SGD theory characterizes gossip and local-update convergence (Koloskova et al., 2020). These approaches determine how models collaborate during training and ordinarily return a model at each client. Our problem begins after training: for each new query, the system must decide whether and where that query should move. We therefore use DFedAvgM as the primary graph-matched training baseline and isolate referral with EdgeDRM-NR ,evaluated on the same EdgeDRM predictors. 

Expert referral and local graph search. Expert-referral networks are closer to our deployment protocol because they model accept-or-forward decisions on sparse graphs. DIEL learns topic-dependent neighbor expertise from observed referral outcomes through a distributed active-learning rule (KhudaBukhsh et al., 2016). Decentralized-search theory studies when local connections yield short query paths (Kleinberg, 2000; Ma et al., 2019), and learned similarity-graph routing can use graph-wide search supervision (Baranchuk et al., 2019). EdgeDRM requires neither logged referral outcomes nor target paths. Its local scores are learned from the static datasets held at neighboring clients, and its movement budget makes the access constraint explicit. 11 Learning to Refer PREPRINT 

Density ratios and pairwise consistency. Classifier-based density-ratio estimation recovers pointwise density ratios from prior-corrected posterior odds (Sugiyama et al., 2012). Statistical Hodge theory shows how pairwise comparison flows decompose into gradient, cyclic, and harmonic components (Jiang et al., 2011). We do not propose a new density-ratio estimator in isolation. Instead, one potential per client induces compatible edge differences, while balanced endpoint classification estimates pairwise density-ratio terms and the sample-count prior is added only at inference. We then analyze how estimation error, representation disagreement, and graph structure affect the resulting referral policy.

D.1 CIFAR Data, Graphs, Baselines, and Traffic Construction 

The benchmark uses the client construction from the released FedDRM code (Wang et al., 2026a). CIFAR-10 has ten labels, CIFAR-20 maps the CIFAR-100 fine labels to 20 superclasses, and CIFAR-100 retains all 100 fine labels. The official training and test collections are pooled before partitioning among eight clients using one of two label-skew constructions. In the SPC construction, label-sorted data are divided into equal-sized, label-homogeneous shards, and each client receives five uniformly sampled shards for CIFAR-10 or 25 for CIFAR-20/100, yielding equal client dataset sizes. In the Dirichlet construction, client-wise class proportions are randomly drawn from a Dirichlet distribution with concentration 0.3, yielding heterogeneous label marginals and unequal client dataset sizes. Each client’s assigned images are shuffled and divided into 70% training and 30% evaluation data. The latter is split into 20% validation and 80% test data. Training, validation, and test indices are disjoint. The eight deterministic client transformations combine a gamma-correction exponent in {0.6, 1.4}, hue shift in 

{− 0.1, 0.1}, and saturation in {0.5, 1.5}. The CIFAR models use a GroupNorm ResNet-18 followed by a 256-dimensional task projection with LayerNorm and a local classifier. Within each paired run, all clients share a random initialization; no pretrained FedDRM parameters are loaded. Both CIFAR training phases retain the released random-resized crop to 32 × 32 pixels with scale 0.9–1.0, followed by the fixed client color transformation and normalization. Evaluation uses one seeded crop per image, shared across paired models and readouts. Freezing fixes encoder parameters and buffers; it does not fix the augmented input at every training access. The fixed evaluation view and non-source entry assignments are shared between each 800+200 model and its DFedAvgM control. D.3 ISIC2019: Data, Protocols, and Additional Results 

The study in Section 5.4 uses a fixed 400-round shared-training prefix followed by 50 rounds of balanced-endpoint frozen-encoder adaptation, with three training seeds. 

D.3.1 Shared data, preprocessing, and graphs Data provenance and center identities. We use the 23,247 images and six source-center assignments in the ISIC2019 split distributed with FLamby (du Terrail et al., 2022). Images come from the public ISIC2019 training collection, including BCN20000, HAM10000, and MSK contributions (Hern´ andez-P´ erez et al., 2024; Tschandl et al., 2018; Codella et al., 2018). In particular, the evaluation set here is FLamby’s held-out subset of that collection, not the separate ISIC challenge test collection. Center identities follow source and acquisition metadata and should not be interpreted as six independent hospitals. The FedSPM package (Wang et al., 2026b) serves only as a reference for the data input pipeline; we do not use its within-server subpopulation model or its pooled resplitting procedure. Each observed center is represented by one expert. 

Leakage control and fixed split. The released FLamby partition contains 18,597 training and 4,650 test images. Our metadata audit finds 2,302 lesion identifiers shared across these folds. We preserve every test image and label and remove the 6,102 training images whose lesion identifier also occurs in the test fold. We additionally link lesion identifiers through identical original or preprocessed image files and one visually confirmed near-duplicate pair. These links are computed globally, including across centers; groups with conflicting labels are excluded from the training candidates. The image-level audit removes four additional training candidates. A deterministic grouped split (seed 42) targets a 20% validation share within center–class cells, while reserving at least one training group for each represented cell. The resulting counts are given in Table 7. Training, validation, and test are disjoint in the audited lesion/duplicate groups. The grouping metadata do not establish patient-level independence, and the near-duplicate review is not an exhaustive guarantee against all transformed duplicates. Images and model. We resize each RGB image with bilinear interpolation so that its shorter side is 224 pixels, apply the FLamby shades-of-gray color-constancy transform with power six, and save a JPEG with quality 75. Training uses a random 200 × 200 crop, independent horizontal and vertical flips, rotation within ±50 degrees, and brightness/contrast jitter of 0.15/0.1. Validation and test use a deterministic center crop. All roles use ImageNet channel normalization. These torchvision transforms are shared by all methods; we do not claim exact reproduction of FLamby’s augmentation code. The model is the existing FedDRM GroupNorm ResNet-18 with its stride-one stem, a 512 → 256 projection with LayerNorm/ReLU, and a private eight-class linear head. Within each seed, all six experts and all methods start from the same random initialization; no pretrained weights are used. EdgeDRM adds a 256-dimensional slope and scalar intercept per node. 

Communication graphs. The four undirected graphs—P ATH , C YCLE , RR3, and C OMPLETE —are fixed before training. P ATH has edges {i, i + 1 } for i = 1 , . . . , 5, and C YCLE adds the edge {1, 6}. The intermediate regular graph is

D.3.2 Training Details Shared-training prefix. Each graph uses six identically initialized local models, three training seeds, and 400 rounds of task-only SGD followed by full-model Metropolis mixing. There are ten local optimizer steps per round, effective batch size 32, and microbatch size 16. The initial learning rate is 0.03 with a 400-round cosine horizon; SGD momentum is 0.9, reset each round, and weight decay is 5 × 10 −4. The RR3 prefix reuses the unchanged final checkpoint of the earlier DFedAvgM run, before its evaluation-only consensus. The other graphs require their own task-only prefixes; old joint-training EdgeDRM checkpoints are not substituted for them. The comparison uses fixed round 400, not the historical validation-selected checkpoint. 

Frozen adaptation. The backbone, feature projection, feature normalization, and their buffers are fixed to the prefix checkpoint and evaluated in inference mode. Private classifier heads inherit their prefix values, and all node routing potentials start at zero. For 50 rounds, five local mini-batches per round update only the heads and balanced-endpoint routing potentials, using equal endpoint weights and λ = 0 .9. Microbatch gradients are weighted by their fractions of the full batch. Endpoint owners accumulate both local and neighbor contributions from a common snapshot before updating. No encoder or head mixing is performed, and no extra consensus phase is added. The head/router learning rate starts at 0.01 and uses (34) with horizon 600, stopping at round 50; momentum, weight decay, and batch sizes are as above. Training retains random image augmentation despite the frozen representation parameters. 

Selection, inference, and budget interpretation. Validation readouts are saved at adaptation rounds 0, 10, 25, and 50. Within each topology, test comparisons use the fixed prefix and round-50 endpoint without selecting a model from the test scores. The main table reports P ATH , C YCLE , RR3, and C OMPLETE ; topology comparisons are descriptive rather than a preregistered test. The full prefix uses 4,000 local optimizer steps per client; adaptation adds 250 head/router steps. Thus comparison with DFedAvgM400 estimates the change from adding adaptation and referral to that prefix, and does not establish superiority at equal total training effort. The adapted entry, hard terminal, uniform history, and score-weighted history controls use identical final model parameters and matched queries.
