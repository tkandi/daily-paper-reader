## Abstract 

Retrieval-Augmented Generation (RAG) is increasingly served across geo-distributed cloud and edge infrastructures, where wide-area latency, cross-region egress cost, and data-residency rules compete with retrieval quality. Conventional pipelines rank by semantic sim-ilarity alone and ignore where content lives, inflating tail latency and cost. We formalize the Geo-RAG Selection Problem (GRaSP), a drop-in re-scoring layer that decays relevance by latency and adds an optional cost penalty under a cardinality budget and per-region caps, with no retraining. We first settle its complexity: the base formulation is a linear objective over a partition matroid, solved 

optimally by an 𝑂 (𝑁 log 𝑁 ) greedy algorithm, rather than NP-hard as a knapsack framing suggests, with hardness entering only under an additive egress budget. On a five-node CloudLab testbed emulat-ing a four-region WAN over the Natural Questions corpus (2.68M passages), GRaSP’s benefit is placement-dependent : under uniform placement it cuts fetch latency at negligible quality cost; under topical (residency-like) placement it traces a recall–latency Pareto frontier that dominates relevance-only, nearest-𝑘 , and a latency-budgeted baseline. At a moderate latency setting, GRaSP lowers P90 fetch latency by 41% while retaining 78% of relevance-only’s recall, with sub-millisecond selection overhead, stable to 200 queries/s. Findings generalize across four public BEIR benchmarks. In end-to-end question answering, GRaSP reduces context-fetch latency by 43% while remaining within 0 .062 absolute EM of relevance-only retrieval.

## 1 Introduction 

Large language models (LLMs) excel at generating text, but they can hallucinate, and their knowledge can become outdated. Retrieval-Augmented Generation (RAG) helps by retrieving relevant passages at inference time and giving them to the model as additional con-text [12]. RAG is now widely used in enterprise assistants, developer copilots, and analytics systems. In practice, the data used by these applications is often spread across multiple cloud regions and edge locations. Organizations do this to reduce client latency, control costs, improve availability, and meet data-residency requirements. This creates a new challenge for RAG: the most relevant passage is not always the best one to fetch. A highly relevant passage may be stored in a distant region, making it slower and more expensive to

retrieve. Regional policies may also limit how much content can be fetched from a particular location. As a result, relevance-only top-𝑘 

selection can increase tail latency, raise egress cost, and behave poorly under regional constraints. Consider a SaaS assistant whose knowledge base is distributed across eu-central , ap-southeast , and sa-east . A user in Paris is served from eu-central , but several of the most relevant pas-sages for a query are stored in sa-east , roughly 115 ms away. A relevance-only policy will select passages without considering their storage locations, increasing both fetch latency and cross-region egress costs. If similar passages are available in Europe, choosing them instead can keep most of the retrieval quality while reducing latency. But if the useful content exists mainly in sa-east , then avoiding the remote fetch will also reduce relevance. In other words, the benefit of location-aware retrieval depends on where relevant content is placed .We formalize this problem as the Geo-RAG Selection Problem 

(GRaSP). GRaSP runs after retrieval or re-ranking and before the selected passages are fetched. It re-scores candidates using semantic relevance, network latency, and optional retrieval cost, while also enforcing global and per-region selection limits. Because GRaSP operates only on the candidate pool produced by the existing re-triever, it can be added to dense or hybrid RAG pipelines without retraining or re-indexing. Although the objective includes latency decay, cost, and regional constraints, the base optimization prob-lem is simple. For a given query, each candidate has a fixed utility. This reduces the problem to a linear selection problem that can be solved exactly using an 𝑂 (𝑁 log 𝑁 ) greedy algorithm. The problem becomes NP-hard only when we add constraints such as a hard egress budget. Our evaluation shows that GRaSP’s benefit depends strongly on data placement. When relevant content is spread across regions or well replicated, GRaSP can reduce latency and cost with little loss in retrieval quality. When relevant content is concentrated in a remote region, latency and relevance are in direct tension. In that case, GRaSP provides a tunable trade-off rather than eliminating the cost of remote access. Replication can reduce this tension. By placing copies of fre-quently accessed content in a small number of additional regions, the system creates more nearby alternatives for GRaSP to choose from. Our results show that this can recover much of the low-latency behavior seen under uniform placement. The main contribu-tion of this work is therefore not a complex optimization algorithm, but a clear formulation and an empirical study of when geo-aware retrieval helps. Specifically: 

• We formulate GRaSP as latency-aware relevance maximiza-tion with an optional cost penalty, a global top-𝑘 limit, and arXiv:2610.26938v1 [cs.IR] 17 Oct 2026 Ruparel, Pandey, and Kumar

• We show that the base problem can be solved optimally by an O(NlogN) greedy algorithm, while NP-hardness arises only when additive resource constraints, such as a hard egress budget, are introduced (Section 4). 

• We build a five-node CloudLab [6] testbed that emulates a four-region WAN and evaluate GRaSP on 2.68M Natural Questions passages. We measure retrieval quality, tail la-tency, egress cost, and optimality against an exact solver (Section 6).

## 2 Related Work 

RAG and Adaptive Retrieval: RAG research has emphasized re-trieval quality and fusion strategies [12], along with graph-structured retrieval [14] and, more recently, distributed RAG architectures [25]. Adaptive and cascaded retrieval methods decide how much to re-trieve or when to stop [7], but they optimize over a centralized index and do not treat network location as a selection signal. GRaSP is orthogonal and composable: it re-scores whatever candidate pool an upstream retriever or re-ranker produces. 

Geo-distributed Analytics and Storage: A large systems lit-erature optimizes data and task placement across wide-area net-works for latency and cost. Iridium [15] jointly optimizes data and task placement to reduce wide-area query latency; Uluyol et al. [21] quantify latency versus cost trade-offs in geo-distributed storage, and transaction chains [26] achieve low-latency serializabil-ity. SPANStore [24] minimizes the cost of geo-replicated storage placement across multiple clouds. These works decide where to place or how to replicate fixed objects; GRaSP instead decides which 

objects to fetch at query time given a fixed placement and uncertain relevance, and is complementary to placement optimizers. 

Caching and Content Placement: Content caching in CDNs and edge networks decides which content to keep close to users to reduce latency; recent work makes this decision cost-aware , ac-counting for the external bandwidth and retrieval cost of cache misses rather than hit-ratio alone [2, 22]. In the LLM setting, se-mantic caches reuse prior responses or retrieved contexts for similar queries to cut inference cost and latency [3, 18], and in multi-tenant RAG, frequency-aware global caches outperform per-tenant parti-tioning under skewed workloads [17]. These mechanisms operate 

ahead of a query, provisioning content by predicted popularity; GRaSP instead operates at query time, selecting among already-placed candidates under per-query relevance, latency, and cost. The two are complementary—our replication result (Section 6.4) shows that caching hot content into nearby regions widens the regime in which GRaSP’s selection is near-free. 

Replica and Server Selection: Systems such as DONAR [23] and C3 [19] choose which replica of a known object to contact to minimize latency under load. The key distinction is that replica selection assumes the target object is fixed and replicated, whereas GRaSP must jointly decide which documents to include—trading semantic relevance against location—before any replica choice is made. When documents are replicated, GRaSP naturally subsumes best-replica selection as the inner fetch decision (Section 6.4). 

Constrained Selection: Algorithmically, GRaSP’s cap-constrained selection is a linear objective over a partition matroid; its budgeted variant relates to the 0–1 knapsack [9, 13]. We make this structure explicit in Section 4, which clarifies when exact selection is cheap and when approximation is warranted. GRaSP is also complemen-tary to federated and decentralized knowledge access [10].

## 4 Complexity of Geo-RAG Selection 

GRaSP may initially appear to be a nonlinear 0–1 optimization prob-lem because relevance is decayed by latency and combined with retrieval cost. In the base formulation, however, each candidate’s utility is fixed before selection, reducing the problem to a linear ob-jective with structured constraints. As a result, it is solvable exactly in polynomial time; NP-hardness appears only under additional constraints such as a hard egress budget.

## 5 Algorithms 

For the base GRaSP problem, Proposition 1 gives an exact 𝑂 (𝑁 log 𝑁 )

solution: sort candidates by net utility and greedily select them while respecting the global and regional caps. We use this cap-aware greedy as the default policy. We consider two extensions: a Lagrangian variant for harder budgeted or compliance-constrained formulations, and a simpler latency-decay function for high-throughput serving.

## 6 Evaluation 

Our evaluation answers four questions. (Q1) Does latency-aware selection reduce tail latency and cost, and what retrieval-quality trade-off does it introduce? (Q2) How does this trade-off depend on data placement? (Q3) Can replication recover quality lost to latency awareness? (Q4) Is GRaSP close to optimal while remaining lightweight and stable enough for online serving? 

## 6.1 Experimental methodology and assumptions 

We first describe the testbed and modeling choices used throughout the evaluation. Several choices—including relevance normalization, pricing, and placement—are not fixed by the GRaSP formulation itself, so we test their sensitivity separately. 

Testbed: We use a five-node CloudLab [6] cluster (Utah m510 :8-core Xeon-D, 64 GB RAM, NVMe, 10 GbE). One node acts as the query coordinator (the source region 𝑠 ), while four nodes host docu-ment payloads in separate logical regions. We emulate wide-area la-tency using Linux tc netem , with round-trip times of {1, 90 , 210 , 115 }

ms for the local, eu-central , ap-southeast , and sa-east regions, respectively. These values are consistent with representative pub-lic AWS inter-region latency measurements [5]. We use persistent connections and pin static ARP entries so that connection setup does not perturb the emulated latency matrix. 

Corpus and retriever: Our main corpus is BEIR Natural Ques-tions (NQ) [11, 20], containing 2,681,468 passages and 3,452 test queries with relevance judgments. To test generalization across do-mains, we additionally evaluate SciFact, NFCorpus, and ArguAna [20], covering scientific claim verification, biomedical retrieval, and ar-gument retrieval. Passages are embedded with a sentence-transformer model [16] and indexed using exact inner-product FAISS [8]. Each query pro-duces a top-200 candidate pool, or the full corpus when the dataset is smaller. GRaSP operates only on this supplied candidate pool: it does not change the retriever, embedding model, or index. Stronger upstream retrievers may therefore shift absolute recall, but the selection mechanism itself is retriever-agnostic. 

End-task generation: Retrieval metrics do not fully capture answer quality, so we also evaluate an end-to-end QA task on NQ. We condition a generator [4] on the context selected by each policy and report exact match (EM) and token-level F1 against short gold answers. Gold answers are obtained by joining NQ-Open [11] with the BEIR NQ queries by question text (Section 6.10). 

Relevance normalization: GRaSP assumes rel 𝑖 ∈ [ 0, 1] but does not prescribe how raw retriever scores should be mapped into this range. We use per-query min–max normalization by default. Since the latency decay multiplies rel 𝑖 , the score distribution af-fects the numerical meaning of 𝛾 𝑞 . We therefore repeat the main experiment with softmax normalization in Section 6.7. 

Cost model: We compute per-document retrieval cost as passage size multiplied by a representative regional egress price ({0.01, 0.02, 0.09, 0.06} USD/GB), consistent with published AWS data-transfer rates [1]. Costs are normalized within each candidate pool so that 

𝛽 operates on a scale comparable to latency-adjusted relevance. Batching discounts and tiered pricing are outside the scope of the model. In the default price map, distant regions also tend to be more expensive, so latency and cost are correlated. To isolate the effect of 

𝛽 from 𝛾 𝑞 , we also evaluate a decorrelated map in which a relatively near region is the most expensive (Section 6.8). 

Placement: We study two placement regimes. Under uniform placement , documents are hashed across the four regions. Rele-vant candidates are therefore spread across regions, making nearby alternatives relatively common. Under topical placement , document embeddings are clustered into four groups using 𝑘 -means and each cluster is mapped to a region. This approximates deployments in which related content is co-located because of organizational ownership, jurisdiction, or data-residency rules. The average concentration of a query’s top-5 candidates in its dominant region is 0 .89 under topical place-ment, compared with approximately 0 .40 under uniform placement. Topical placement therefore creates the setting in which the most relevant content is often concentrated in one, potentially remote, region. 

Policies: We compare Relevance-only , the standard top-𝑘 policy; 

Nearest-𝑘 , a latency-only baseline; GRaSP-greedy , the direct cap-aware greedy algorithm from Proposition 1; GRaSP-rational , which uses Eq. (8); and OPT , an exact integer-program solution of Eq. (3) used offline as an optimality oracle. Unless stated otherwise, 𝑘 = 5. Nearest-𝑘 is intentionally simple, so we additionally evaluate

## 6.2 Q1/Q2: Latency, cost, quality, and placement

This matches the approximation’s expected behavior: rational decay penalizes very large latencies less aggressively than the exponential, preserving somewhat more recall but producing a heavier latency tail.

Under uniform placement , the 𝛾 𝑞 sweep re-mains close to the top-left of the recall–latency plane: latency falls substantially before recall begins to decline. Because relevant doc-uments are distributed across regions, GRaSP can often replace a remote passage with a similarly relevant nearby one. Under topical placement , the frontier bends outward. A query’s most relevant candidates tend to reside together, so moving toward nearby regions increasingly requires accepting less relevant pas-sages. GRaSP still exposes a smooth recall–latency trade-off, but the gain is no longer free. The lesson is that latency-aware selection can exploit locality only when good nearby alternatives exist. If the relevant content is concentrated in a remote region, no choice of 𝛾 𝑞 can remove the remote fetch without sacrificing quality. This placement depen-dence is therefore a property of the available candidate set, not a limitation of the optimization algorithm. 

A stronger latency-aware baseline: Nearest-𝑘 represents the latency-only extreme and is intentionally weak. A more realistic practitioner might impose a latency threshold and then maximize relevance within it. Rel@LatencyCap models this approach. We sweep 𝐿 ∈ { 1, 90 , 115 , 210 } ms under NQ topical placement. At 𝐿 = 115 ms, the baseline achieves recall 0 .432 at 79 .8 ms. GRaSP provides nearby operating points with recall 0 .419 at 69 .5 ms and 0.506 at 97 .4 ms. More generally, its 𝛾 𝑞 sweep produces a better interior trade-off because latency is penalized continuously rather than treated as an all-or-nothing cutoff. The advantage appears in the operating range where tuning is most useful: a hard threshold cannot distinguish a relevant candi-date just beyond 𝐿 from a weak candidate just below it, whereas GRaSP trades relevance and latency jointly for each document.

## 6.5 Residency caps 

We next activate the per-region constraints by setting 𝐶 𝑟 = 2 under topical placement. Since relevant content is often concentrated in one region, the cap forces every policy to obtain part of its context from other regions. Table 3 shows the effect at 𝛾 𝑞 = 0.01. For relevance-only, the cap raises expected latency from 122 .1 to 175 .3 ms and reduces recall from 0 .534 to 0 .392. After exhausting the dominant region, relevance-only simply selects the highest-ranked feasible docu-ments without considering their location. GRaSP also loses recall and gains latency because the feasible set itself has become worse, but it continues to match OPT. The cap changes what is achievable; GRaSP still finds the best feasible selection under that constraint. A related behavior appears under strong joint latency and cost pressure. Some candidates can then have negative net utility. Relevance-only and nearest-𝑘 may still select them because their policies con-sider only relevance or locality. GRaSP instead selects fewer than 

𝑘 documents, or none, when another retrieval would lower the objective. This is the abstention behavior described in Section 4.

## 7 Discussion and Limitations 

The optimization itself is deliberately simple; our contribution is to identify and empirically characterize the conditions under which location-aware context selection helps rather than designing a sophisticated solver. 

Drop-in adoption: GRaSP is designed to fit into an existing RAG pipeline with minimal changes. It requires only a latency estimate for each candidate’s hosting region—from a static RTT table, recent measurements, or a learned predictor—and, optionally, a regional cost map. After an upstream retriever or re-ranker produces a candidate pool, GRaSP re-scores them and enforces the configured global and regional limits before payloads are fetched. It therefore requires no retraining, embedding changes, or re-indexing. 

When GRaSP helps: Our results show that the value of geo-aware selection depends primarily on where useful alternatives are located. When relevant content is uniformly distributed or replicated across regions, GRaSP can reduce latency and egress cost with little loss in retrieval quality. When relevant content is concentrated in a remote region, as may happen under residency or ownership constraints, locality and relevance are in genuine tension. In this regime, GRaSP does not eliminate the trade-off; instead, it exposes a controllable Pareto frontier through 𝛾 𝑞 and 𝛽 .This also suggests that placement and query-time selection should be considered together. Replicating frequently accessed content into even a small number of additional regions increases the chance that GRaSP can choose a nearby high-quality passage. Our replication experiments show that this can move the system back toward the regime in which latency and cost reductions are obtained with little quality loss. 

Limitations: First, the current formulation assumes a point es-timate of network latency. Real wide-area networks exhibit bursti-ness, asymmetry, congestion, and time-dependent behavior. These effects could be incorporated using percentile latency estimates, moving averages, or time-of-day profiles, but we do not evaluate those variants here. Second, our cost model is additive at the document level. Real cloud pricing may include batching effects, volume discounts, min-imum charges, or tiered pricing. Some of these can be incorporated into the objective or added as linear constraints; a tight aggregate budget leads to the budgeted variant discussed in Section 4, where exact greedy selection no longer applies. Third, our systems evaluation uses an emulated WAN on a single CloudLab cluster rather than a live multi-region deployment. We also use one retrieval setup and one primary large-scale corpus, sup-plemented by three smaller BEIR datasets. These experiments are sufficient to expose the placement-dependent behavior we study, but they do not capture all production effects, such as routing varia-tion, regional failures, provider-specific networking, or interactions with different retrievers and corpora. Fourth, the replication experiments model an optimistic setting in which the lowest-latency replica is available and can be selected without additional coordination. We do not account for replica consistency, update propagation, storage overhead, or the cost of maintaining those copies. The measured replication gains should therefore be interpreted as an upper bound on what a production system could achieve. 

Outlook: Several extensions follow naturally from this work. The parameters 𝛾 𝑞 , 𝛽 , and even 𝑘 could be chosen dynamically from workload characteristics or learned from user feedback. Placement and replication could also be optimized jointly with query-time selection, allowing the system to decide both where content should live and which copy should serve each request. Finally, the same for-mulation could be extended beyond independent passage retrieval to account for multi-hop or graph-based reasoning, where the lo-cation and cost of one retrieval may influence which document should be fetched next. 

## 8 Conclusion 

We presented GRaSP, a latency- and cost-aware re-scoring layer for geo-distributed RAG. GRaSP adds location awareness to retrieval without retraining or re-indexing, and its base formulation remains simple: the selection problem is solvable exactly with an 𝑂 (𝑁 log 𝑁 )

greedy algorithm, while NP-hardness arises only in extensions with hard additive budgets. Our evaluation on a five-node CloudLab testbed and 2.68M pas-sages shows that the benefit of geo-aware retrieval depends strongly on data placement. When relevant content is uniformly distributed or replicated, GRaSP reduces latency and cost with little loss in retrieval quality. When relevant content is concentrated in remote regions, latency and relevance are in genuine tension, and GRaSP exposes a tunable trade-off between them. Replication provides a practical way to reduce this tension by bringing useful alternatives closer to the serving region. GRaSP adds sub-millisecond selection overhead, matches the exact solution for the base problem, and remains stable up to 200 queries/s. More broadly, our results show that retrieval qual-ity alone is not sufficient for geo-distributed RAG: where relevant content resides can be just as important as how relevant it is.
