# Toward Collective Model Intelligence: Characterizing and Coordinating Models 

SinapisAI Team

Abstract 

Independently developed AI models accumulate diverse and complementary capabilities, but a growing model collection does not automatically realize Collective AI . Addressing this gap requires un-derstanding model capabilities with limited evaluation, organizing complementary models to collaborate on diverse tasks, and executing model calls efficiently under resource constraints . We present a systematic approach to pursuing Collective Model Intelligence through three connected components. First, Model Eval constructs evidence-based capability profiles by combining query-level performance estimation with cross-domain prediction, reducing the need for exhaustive evaluation. Second, Mio (Models in One) organizes complementary model candidates and supports task-conditioned, cost-aware se-lection and collaboration as task requirements evolve. Third, Mos (Model Serving OS) separates model selection from execution and provides endpoint scheduling and shared model deployment and distribution. Experiments show lower capability-estimation errors under limited evaluation. Across nine public benchmarks, Mio 1.0 Lite achieves the highest average score and lowest average cost among compared methods, with per-request costs 73.7–97.5% lower than those of the best-scoring alternatives. A prewarmed shared cache accelerates model-file retrieval by up to 23 .3×. This work lays a foundation for Collective Model Intelligence by characterizing and coordinating the diverse capabilities of an expanding model universe.

1 From Individual Models to Collective AI 

1.1 The Expanding Model Universe 

AI resources are distributed across organizations and environments. Datasets, trained models, do-main expertise, and computation cannot always be brought together because of privacy, ownership, and communication constraints. Independent training adds new models, while continued adapta-tion changes the capabilities of existing ones. Together, these processes sustain an expanding and evolving model universe : a population of models developed or adapted across organizations, datasets, and task settings. Models differ in architecture, modality, and specialization; even those derived from the same foundation model can develop distinct strengths through adaptation. These models embody knowledge and skills accumulated across developers, datasets, and tasks. Their diversity creates opportunities to reuse complementary capabilities [32]. A system can realize additional value by understanding these capabilities, organizing complementary models, and coordinating their contributions to tasks. We pursue this opportunity through Collective AI : coordinating distributed data, computational resources, and intelligent capabilities to learn, reason, and act at the system level [2, 7]. We consider three complementary levels of coordination. At the data level, Collective Data Intelligence develops model capabilities by learning from diverse data sources, accumulated experience, and feedback. Many familiar learning practices can be viewed through this lens, including large-scale language and multimodal pretraining [1, 22], learning from feedback through reinforcement learning [4], and federated learning [16]. At the computation level, distributed training and inference coordinate work across computing resources. At the capability level, coordination combines complementary knowledge, skills, and functions supplied by models, agents, and tools to accomplish tasks. Realizing these benefits requires effective coordination. At the capability level, for example, a model with a high average score may largely duplicate strengths already present in the collection, while a specialist may contribute a missing capability. A useful collective must identify and use these differences to improve outcomes or resource efficiency, accounting for coordination costs. Trained models offer a practical starting point for capability-level coordination because they embody accumulated learning. We envision a collective that can grow to incorporate diverse models, including specialists for different domains and tasks. In this vision, the collective also uses experience and its own capabilities to improve how it is organized and coordinated. Work on continual learning under resource constraints [6] and learning from interaction [25] motivates the emphasis on adaptation and experience. 

1.2 Collective Model Intelligence for Collective AI 

At SinapisAI, we focus on Collective Model Intelligence : understanding, reusing, and coordinating capabilities across an evolving population of independently developed models to improve task outcomes and resource efficiency. This complements Collective Data Intelligence by reusing capabilities encoded in trained models, with data, evaluation, and feedback guiding coordination. Reusing accumulated capabilities requires information about where a model is applicable and what it can contribute. The learnware paradigm addresses this need by pairing trained models with specifications that help users identify and reuse suitable capabilities [31–33]. For example, a task involving visual interpretation, reasoning, and tool use may benefit from different models at different stages. Other tasks may call for selecting among models with different

strengths and resource requirements. These possibilities depend on identifying and using model complementarity in the context of the task. The central challenge is to infer useful model complementarity from limited evidence and deter-mine when coordinating these capabilities yields a net gain under task and resource constraints. As models, tasks, and service conditions change, these decisions must adapt. As an initial step toward this broader vision, this report addresses three foundational questions: 1. How can capabilities be characterized with limited evaluation? Capability characterization must estimate model performance across domains and queries from sparse observations and identify complementary model strengths. 2. How can models be organized for selection and collaboration? Organization must preserve useful capability diversity, while task-dependent selection and collaboration must adapt model use to evolving needs and remaining cost and latency budgets. 3. How can inference requests be executed efficiently under service constraints? Scheduling must account for changing load and availability across heterogeneous endpoints, while efficient model distribution supports self-managed runtimes. Capability evidence informs which models are retained and used, while execution conditions determine whether a choice can be carried out within the task’s constraints. Evaluation and task feedback can revise this evidence and guide subsequent decisions. These connections motivate a system that links capability characterization, model organization and use, and efficient execution. 

1.3 A System for Collective Model Intelligence 

We address these three questions through a system that connects Model Eval for capability characterization, Mio for model organization, selection and collaboration, and Mos for shared execution. Model Space provides the shared inventory linking models, capability evidence, and execution resources (Section 2). These components form an initial foundation within the broader vision illustrated in Figure 1. 

Characterizing capabilities. Model Eval uses historical evidence and limited additional evalua-tion to construct reusable capability profiles. It combines score estimation from limited query observations with prediction across domains, providing capability evidence for model organization and use (Section 3). 

Organizing models for selection and collaboration. Mio (Models in One) is designed to organize a compact pool of complementary models for task-dependent selection and collaboration. Capability evidence guides pool construction, while task requirements and remaining cost and latency budgets guide model use. Selection assigns a suitable model to the current task, while collaboration lets complementary models contribute at different stages. Mio uses earlier results and the remaining cost and latency budgets to decide which model to call next (Section 4). 

Executing inference requests efficiently. Mos (Model Serving OS) provides shared execution for capability evaluation and application calls. It schedules calls across compatible endpoints and coordinates model-file distribution for self-managed deployment, preserving the model choice made by Model Eval, Mio, or the caller (Section 5). Section 6 reports component-level evidence of improved score estimation under limited evaluation. Across nine public benchmarks, Mio 1.0 Lite achieves the highest average score and lowest average per-request cost among the compared methods. A prewarmed shared cache also accelerates model-file retrieval.

2.2 Core System Modules Model Eval. Model Eval implements capability characterization using historical evidence and budgeted evaluation through Mos. It publishes versioned profiles linked to model and endpoint identities, the reference snapshot, and evaluation configuration. Measured scores and predictions remain distinguishable, with coverage and provenance supporting comparisons and uncertainty recorded when available. Profiles retain query populations and scoring protocols so that Mio can compare capability evidence under compatible evaluation conditions. Characterization can be requested independently of candidate-pool admission. New evidence or model revisions trigger profile refresh under the lifecycle controls below. Section 3 develops the evaluation framework and prediction methods. 

Mio: Models in One. Mio contains Organization and Collaboration. Organization uses domain coverage, query-level complementarity, and service compatibility to maintain a compact candidate pool. Pool maintenance preserves complementary successes and economical options; removing a candidate requires a retained model that meets the declared replacement-loss tolerances and service requirements. Model selection and collaboration adapt to the bounded task state, current service context, and remaining budgets as tasks progress. Mio passes the selected identity to Mos for endpoint scheduling. Section 4 develops model organization, selection and collaboration. 

Model Space. Model Space maintains the full inventory of API-accessible and downloadable models, including resources outside Mio’s active pool. Versioned identities link sources, interfaces, artifacts or API access configuration, deployment records, endpoints, and capability evidence. Family names group versions; additional endpoints for a known model need not create new model identities. Model Eval, Mio, and Mos link their evidence and decision records to these identities. Discovery exposes available interfaces and compatible evidence, while task suitability is assessed through characterization and selection. 

Mos: Model Serving OS. Mos assigns evaluation, designated-model, and Mio-selected requests to compatible inference endpoints. Its scheduling service uses current load, price, and availability observations; its distribution and deployment service prepares downloadable models for the runtime hub; and its metrics/profiler records attempts, usage, and outcomes. External APIs and self-managed runtimes share the invocation contract, with deployment and model-file distribution applying to the self-managed path. Endpoint support records specify modalities, streaming behavior, and tool interfaces. Section 5.1 describes SLO-aware endpoint scheduling and bounded recovery; Section 5.2 develops model distribution for self-managed model deployment. 

Self-Managed Model Runtime Hub. The runtime hub provides the self-managed execution path below the framework. Inference backends run model calls, instance lifecycle management controls deployment readiness and availability, and a shared model-file cache supports reuse. Mos prepares and invokes these deployments using compute and storage from AI Cloud Infrastructure. 

Cross-component controls. Lifecycle management, privacy, and security apply across the components shown in Figure 2. 

Lifecycle updates. Lifecycle management connects resource, evidence, and candidate versions. A new artifact revision requires readiness checks and characterization; updated profiles can trigger candidate review under Section 4.1. Endpoint outages change request eligibility, while price changes update cost context and can invalidate pool replacements justified by a cost tier. These service events do not themselves change measured capability. Earlier profiles and decision snapshots remain available for interpreting past calls, and models removed from Mio’s pool retain their resources and history in Model Space.

3 Model Capability Characterization 

Model capability characterization aims to describe what a model can do, where it tends to fail, and under which task conditions its capabilities are useful. It produces a reusable representation for model comparison, organization, selection and collaboration. In our framework, this process constructs and updates the capability profile Ω𝑖 introduced in Section 2.1; Model Eval is the system module that collects evidence, applies the characterization methods, and publishes the profile. Evaluation collects and scores model responses; characterization combines this evidence with performance predictions into profiles for model organization, selection and collaboration. The profile serves the role of a model specification in the learnware paradigm. Such a specification can take several forms. Semantic descriptions can record intended tasks, modalities, and application contexts. Statistical specifications can summarize properties of the data associated with a model, as in reduced kernel mean embedding specifications [26, 33]. Evaluation-based representations record model behavior on declared tasks. Together, these forms describe task identity, distributional compatibility, and demonstrated performance. This section focuses on large language models (LLMs) and their multimodal extensions, whose capabilities span diverse tasks and can vary substantially across queries within the same task. For such models, a broad semantic label provides limited resolution for comparing strengths and failures, while hosted access may provide neither training data nor internal parameters. Evaluation through a model’s callable interface supplies behavioral evidence under specified task and scoring conditions [13]. We therefore construct evaluation-based capability profiles using the domain–query hierarchy defined in Section 2.1. Each profile combines domain scores with query-level outcomes, uncertainty, and coverage. Domain scores describe broad performance; query-level evidence reveals complementary successes hidden by aggregate rankings. For example, two models may have similar coding scores yet excel on different queries, such as code generation versus debugging. Query-level prediction estimates unqueried outcomes from a few scored queries in selected anchor domains , yielding aggregate anchor scores . Cross-domain prediction uses these scores and historical model-domain relationships to estimate performance in untested domains, such as data analysis. Constructing these profiles creates an evaluation challenge. Testing every model on every query requires substantial inference and scoring resources [15], and historical evaluations cover models and tasks unevenly. The objective is to characterize model 𝑓 ∗ across evaluation domains using his-torical evidence and limited additional query evaluations. Our approach combines two prediction stages: query-level predictions estimate scores on selected anchor domains, and relationships across models and domains reconstruct the remaining scores. A multi-round procedure allocates further evaluation between broadening domain coverage and refining anchor-score estimates, building the profile Ω∗ used by Mio. Model Eval publishes the profile with observations, predic-tions, uncertainty, and provenance distinguished; renewed evaluation updates this evidence as models and tasks change. Characterization covers the declared model inventory and can be used independently of Mio’s candidate-pool admission and request-selection decisions.

4 Mio: Organizing Models for Selection and Collaboration 

Mio (Models in One) addresses model organization, selection and collaboration within our system. It aims to turn complementary capabilities into better task quality and more efficient resource use by maintaining a compact candidate pool and coordinating its members under evolving task requirements and budgets. Capability profiles from Section 3 guide which models to retain and how to match them to the current task. Capability evidence alone does not determine these decisions. Keeping only the highest average performers can discard complementary specialists and economical options, while comparing every available model increases selection overhead. A useful candidate pool must preserve these contributions, and request-level selection must match them to the current task state, access requirements, and resource constraints. As a task progresses, both its needs and its remaining budget can change, calling for a new choice. Mio addresses these requirements in two stages, summarized in Figure 6. Section 4.1 constructs and maintains a compact candidate pool C ⊆ F using domain coverage, query-level complemen-tarity, and service compatibility. Section 4.2 develops model selection and collaboration under the remaining cost and latency budgets, adapting model use and handoff as task needs evolve. Model Space retains the full inventory, and Mos executes the selected model. The reported experiments assess quality–cost trade-offs and request-level robustness (Section 6).

5 Mos: Model Serving OS for Scheduling and Operation 

Mos (Model Serving OS) provides shared execution for model evaluation and application use. Model Eval, Mio, or the caller chooses the model; Mos determines where and when its call executes. As shown in Figures 2 and 4, Mos invokes provider-managed APIs or manages the Self-Managed Model Runtime Hub. Both paths share an invocation contract, while model-file distribution and deployment apply to the self-managed path. Endpoint scheduling preserves model identity; model changes return control to Mio or the caller within remaining budgets. 

5.1 SLO-Aware Request Scheduling across Inference Endpoints 

An endpoint identifies a provider and serving configuration. Endpoints exposing the same declared model can differ in prices, interfaces, rate limits, latency, and availability, with these properties changing over time [11]. Mos schedules calls among compatible endpoints for the selected model. The Mos scheduling implementation provides admission control, local capacity accounting, priority-based scheduling, pluggable window-based request reordering policies, SLO-aware as-signment, Pareto-based optimal endpoint selection, and execution logging. Workload replay submits different types of requests through Mos according to their timestamps, allowing con-current execution. Mos makes online scheduling decisions, while execution feedback updates capacity state and informs subsequent performance estimates. Sequential failover is supported over a bounded set of ranked endpoint candidates, subject to failure type and response progress. Optional preview simulations run in isolation from online capacity accounting. 

Requests and constraints. A request 𝑥 carries the selected model, invocation mode, input-token count 𝑛 in (𝑥 ), estimated output length b𝑛 out (𝑥 ), interface requirements, and priority. Generation settings remain in the gateway request. Clients may specify SLO targets; otherwise, the configured provider assigns default end-to-end (E2E) completion targets for non-streaming requests and TTFT and TPOT targets for streaming requests. The scheduler checks endpoint compatibility, health, cooldown, and remaining local capacity. After further checking the SLO constraints, the scheduler proceeds to select a set of optimal endpoints with Pareto efficiency. The scheduler uses separate timeouts for SLO computation, queue waiting, and upstream calls. Cost estimates and E2E, TTFT, and TPOT targets guide endpoint screening and ranking. The execution design in Section 2.4 additionally specifies upstream residual allowances and shared budget and deadline enforcement across scheduling, execution, and failover.

Execution feedback and recovery. The implemented feedback path updates the capacity, usage, history, failure, and cooldown state of each endpoint maintained by Mos. By default, Mos allows up to three attempts per request on each endpoint. After these attempts fail, it sends the request to the next available endpoint in the ranked list, if one exists. Mos reports complete failure when all ranked endpoints have failed. Once a response or streaming iterator is returned, Mos considers the handoff successful; failures during subsequent streaming iteration are handled by the upstream components that consume the stream.

Metrics. We evaluate 100 evenly spaced nominal budgets between the cheapest and most expensive fixed-model mean costs on each reporting population. 7 Conclusion and Future Vision 

A central implication of this work is that a model’s value depends on its role within a collective. Domain expertise, specialized skills, and resource requirements shape where its strengths com-plement those of other models. A system can draw on that complementarity to develop broader, more integrated intelligence while using resources more efficiently. This report provides an initial foundation for making such relationships measurable and usable, and for advancing Collective AI through model capabilities accumulated across developers and domains. Our future work pursues two connected objectives. First, we aim to scale the system to a much larger model population, including diverse vertical models specialized for particular domains and tasks. Second, we aim to enable experience-driven collective evolution through recursive self-improvement of the system, using its collective capabilities to diagnose weaknesses, revise its own mechanisms, and validate the changes. Each cycle should strengthen both task performance and the system’s capacity for further improvement.
