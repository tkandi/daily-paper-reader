## Abstract 

Gradient-based parameter inference, optimal control, and solver-in-the-loop hybrid physics–machine learning (ML) require derivatives of simulation objectives with respect to model parameters, boundary conditions, or embedded neural network weights. While existing nuclear engineering codes provide mature multiphysics for-ward solvers, coupling them to modern automatic differentiation (AD) and machine learning ecosystems typically requires external cross-language wrappers or code-intrusive adjoint implementations. We present Torch-DPhys, a PyTorch/LibTorch-native framework for differentiable physics simulation and hybrid physics–ML integration that implements selected modeling concepts and discretizations from the Multiphysics Object-Oriented Simulation Environment (MOOSE) Thermal Hy-draulics Module (THM), the System Analysis Module (SAM), and finite-element continuum mechanics. Torch-DPhys expresses finite-element and finite-volume residuals as native tensor operations, supporting execution on central process-ing units (CPUs) and graphics processing units (GPUs), kernel compilation via 

torch.compile , and sparse direct linear solvers through SuperLU and NVIDIA’s cuDSS library for direct sparse solvers. Simulation sensitivities are computed via two complementary routes: reverse-mode differentiation unrolled through executed solver iterations, and discrete residual adjoints evaluated at converged im-plicit states. Forward numerical verification against analytical solutions, MOOSE, and SAM-Lite demonstrates close discrete agreement across plane-strain elas-ticity, one-dimensional single- and two-loop thermal-hydraulic transients, and two-dimensional axisymmetric molten-salt fast reactor (MSFR) flow and heat trans-port, where Torch-DPhys matches SAM-Lite within a maximum normalized field discrepancy of 1.13 × 10 −5 over 200 adaptive time steps. Transient trajectory gra-dients are verified against tangent-linear recurrences and central finite differences. We demonstrate the framework on two gradient-driven applications: recovering pump head and heating power in a thermal-hydraulic loop within 0.06% relative er-ror from synthetic MOOSE observations, and training a recurrent neural controller via differentiable predictive control (DPC) by backpropagating closed-loop trajec-tory objectives through a three-loop plant solver. Torch-DPhys connects MOOSE-and SAM-compatible physical modeling with PyTorch/LibTorch’s native differ-entiation ecosystem, enabling gradient-based inverse modeling, optimal control, and solver-in-the-loop hybrid physics–machine learning for nuclear engineering systems.

Numerical simulation in nuclear science and engineering is traditionally formulated as a forward problem: given a system geometry, material properties, boundary conditions, and empirical closure relations, a solver integrates the governing conservation equations to predict thermal-hydraulic or thermomechanical states. In many engineering workflows, however, forward prediction is embedded within an outer optimization or inference loop. Modelers frequently need to infer uncertain boundary or constitutive parameters from sparse sensor measurements, optimize component geometries or operating schedules, or synthesize feedback controllers that satisfy safety constraints during transients. Addressing these tasks via partial differential equation (PDE)-constrained optimization requires sensitivities that relate trajectory-level objectives to the underlying physical parameters or control actions. Because finite-difference approximations and derivative-free search methods scale poorly as the parameter dimension grows, access to accurate simulation gradients via adjoint methods or automatic differentiation (AD) is essential. The demand for solver gradients has grown as data assimilation and hybrid physics–machine learning (ML) have become central to nuclear system modeling and digital twin development [1–4]. Early hybrid workflows coupled ML with mechanistic solvers offline: neural networks were trained on pre-generated high-fidelity simulation databases to predict turbulent eddy viscosity or closure terms and were subsequently linked into system codes for forward deployment [1, 5]. Similarly, control-oriented studies have often trained control policies by backpropagating through learned surrogate models, such as neural ordinary differential equations (ODEs), rather than the physical plant solver itself [3, 4]. While computationally convenient, decoupling training from the numerical solver introduces distribution shift and surrogate approximation error; a closure or controller that performs well offline can degrade in accuracy or stability when deployed in closed loop with the discretized governing equations. Propagating gradients directly through the time-stepping physics solver—known as solver-in-the-loop training [6, 7]—allows neural closures and control policies to be optimized against multi-step trajectory objectives while accounting for the numerical discretization and physical coupling of the solver. Within the nuclear engineering community, the Multiphysics Object-Oriented Simulation Environ-ment (MOOSE) [8] and its domain applications, including the Thermal Hydraulics Module (THM) [9] and the System Analysis Module (SAM) [10–12], provide widely adopted infrastructure for reactor thermal-hydraulics and multiphysics analysis. MOOSE employs forward-mode AD to assemble local elemental and nodal residual Jacobians [13] and supports forward evaluation of trained neural networks via C++ LibTorch bindings [14]. However, because these production codes are architected in C++ around data structures distributed using the Message Passing Interface (MPI) and nonlinear solvers from the Portable, Extensible Toolkit for Scientific Computation (PETSc), they do not na-tively expose end-to-end reverse-mode differentiation of transient trajectory objectives directly within Python-based ML training loops. In the broader scientific computing literature, two primary strategies have emerged to bridge numerical PDE solvers and differentiable programming. The first strategy wraps an established compiled solver with an external adjoint interface. For example, Diff-NekRS [15] retains NekRS for forward spectral-element fluid simulation, implements a manual discrete adjoint for the supported time-stepping scheme, and couples the resulting sensitivities to LibTorch for multi-step neural closure training. The second strategy implements the numerical discretizations natively within tensor-computing frameworks such as JAX, PyTorch, or TensorFlow. Representative examples include JAX-FEM [16] for differentiable finite-element mechanics and PhiFlow [17] for differentiable fluid simulation. Native tensor implementation places the physical residual evaluations, linear solvers, and neural network layers within a single execution and differentiation environment [18], avoiding cross-language serialization while supporting both unrolled computational-graph differentiation and implicit adjoint formulations. In this work, we develop Torch-DPhys, a PyTorch-native framework for differentiable physics simulation and hybrid physics–ML integration tailored to nuclear thermal-hydraulic and continuum mechanics modeling. Rather than replacing full production codes, Torch-DPhys implements selected physical components and finite-element/finite-volume discretizations derived from MOOSE THM [9] and SAM [10, 11] alongside two-dimensional finite-element elasticity. Component topologies and mesh operators are lowered into structured tensor representations that execute on CPUs or NVIDIA GPUs, with support for kernel compilation via torch.compile and sparse direct linear algebra 2via SuperLU [19, 20] and NVIDIA cuDSS [21]. To accommodate different solver structures and memory budgets, Torch-DPhys provides two routes to trajectory gradients: (i) unrolled reverse-mode AD through executed nonlinear solver iterations, and (ii) discrete residual adjoints evaluated on the converged implicit equations at each time step. The main contributions of this work are as follows: • A PyTorch-native multiphysics modeling architecture. We formulate object-oriented physical models—spanning plane-strain linear elasticity, one-dimensional (1D) compressible channel and loop thermal hydraulics with conjugate heat structures and junctions, and two-dimensional (2D) axisymmetric stabilized finite-element flow and heat transport—as native PyTorch tensor operations that run in the same environment as ML optimization workflows. • Dual differentiation routes with transient gradient verification. We implement both unrolled Newton-iteration differentiation (with step-wise checkpoint recomputation) and converged-residual discrete adjoints (including variable-step second-order backward differ-entiation (BDF2) history terms), and verify the resulting sensitivities against independent tangent-linear recurrences and central finite differences. • Cross-code numerical verification against MOOSE and SAM-Lite. We verify forward simulations against archived MOOSE results across static elasticity and three 1D thermal-hydraulic loop configurations, and against SAM-Lite on a 200-step adaptive transient of an axisymmetric molten-salt fast reactor (MSFR), achieving a maximum normalized field discrepancy of 1.13 × 10 −5 while characterizing time-step stabilization sensitivities and mass-flux conservation properties. • Evaluation of sparse compiled CPU and GPU execution. Using the 200-step MSFR transient workload, we benchmark eager and torch.compile -accelerated execution across CPU threads and GPU hardware (with graphs in NVIDIA’s CUDA parallel computing platform and cuDSS sparse factorizations), analyzing component-level costs across residual evaluation, Jacobian assembly, sparse factorization, and adjoint dependency discovery. • Demonstrations of inverse parameter estimation and solver-in-the-loop control. We apply Torch-DPhys to recover pump head and heating power in a thermal-hydraulic loop from sparse transient MOOSE observations (within 0.06% relative error), and to train a gated recurrent unit (GRU) control policy via differentiable predictive control (DPC) through a three-loop thermal-hydraulic plant model. The remainder of this paper is organized as follows. Section 2 describes the Torch-DPhys software architecture and the two routes for computing simulation gradients. Section 3 summarizes the governing equations and discretizations for the structural mechanics, 1D channel thermal-hydraulics, and 2D axisymmetric MSFR models. Section 4 presents forward verification against MOOSE and SAM-Lite, transient sensitivity verification, and time-step refinement diagnostics. Section 5 analyzes CPU and GPU execution performance on the MSFR workload. Section 6 demonstrates PDE-constrained parameter identification, solver-in-the-loop DPC policy training, and extensions to uncertainty quantification. Section 7 discusses current limitations and concludes the paper. 

## 2 Framework Design and Differentiation 

2.1 Physical Models as Torch Computations 

Torch-DPhys organizes materials, boundary conditions, and domain-specific physical components as Python objects while evaluating their local constitutive relations and global residual assembly through PyTorch tensor operations. Across the supported physics modules—two-dimensional finite-element mechanics, one-dimensional system thermal hydraulics, the reduced three-loop control plant, and two-dimensional axisymmetric finite-element flow and heat transport—this design separates high-level model specification from vectorized numerical execution. For the structural mechanics module, mesh generation and finite-element basis preprocessing are performed offline using the MFEM modular finite-element library [22]. MFEM reads or generates the computational mesh, constructs first-order triangular finite-element spaces, and exports nodal co-ordinates, element connectivity, physical basis-function gradients, quadrature weights, and boundary 3node indices. Torch-DPhys loads these static arrays at initialization and executes stiffness operator evaluations and conjugate-gradient solves natively in PyTorch without runtime calls to MFEM. For the one-dimensional thermal-hydraulic models, a lowering stage translates the object-oriented component network into contiguous tensor index tables representing channel connectivity, junction topology, heat-structure coupling, and physical parameters. This lowering step reformulates the component equations for batched tensor evaluation without altering the underlying physical conser-vation laws. Residual evaluations, automatic differentiation, and linear algebra execute on either CPU or NVIDIA CUDA devices, and computationally intensive residual and Jacobian kernels can be compiled using torch.compile . 2.2 Two Routes to Simulation Gradients 

Torch-DPhys computes gradients of a scalar objective J with respect to model parameters θ via two distinct mathematical routes, which can be exposed either through PyTorch’s native autograd engine or through an explicit sensitivity interface (as in the MSFR module): 1. Unrolled differentiation of executed operations. This route applies reverse-mode AD directly to the sequence of numerical operations executed during the forward solve, such as a fixed number of conjugate-gradient steps or damped Newton iterations. Because the computational graph traces the actual solver updates, the resulting derivative represents the exact sensitivity of the finite-iteration algorithm, including dependence on the initial guess, for the executed sequence of solver updates. However, retaining intermediate solver states across long transients increases memory consumption unless step-wise checkpointing and recomputation are used, and gradients from partially converged iterations need not match the sensitivity of the exact implicit root. 2. Discrete adjoint of the converged residual equations. Rather than differentiating through the nonlinear solver path, the residual-adjoint route applies the implicit function theorem to the converged discrete equations Rn(yn, yn−1, θ) = 0 at each time step n. 3.1 Structural Mechanics 

The structural mechanics module solves two-dimensional isotropic, small-strain linear elasticity under plane-strain conditions:

Using precomputed MFEM basis gradients on linear triangular elements, tensor operators gather nodal displacements, evaluate element strains and stresses at quadrature points, and scatter-add internal nodal forces. This matrix-free operator evaluation computes the action of the global stiffness matrix directly within a preconditioned conjugate-gradient solver while symmetric boundary projections enforce Dirichlet constraints.

4.4 MSFR Forward Verification Against SAM-Lite 

We verify the two-dimensional axisymmetric MSFR implementation against SAM-Lite at both the residual evaluation level and across full transient trajectories. At eight archived reference states, the assembled Torch-DPhys residual vector matches SAM-Lite bit-for-bit at every degree of freedom except at four inlet boundary nodes in four states, where floating-point multiplication order in the inlet velocity expression introduces a one-unit-in-the-last-place (1 ULP) difference. Component-level finite-difference checks of the automatic state Jacobian, BDF2 history derivatives, and parameter Jacobians are documented in Appendix B. For transient forward verification under the baseline input deck tolerances ( 10 −6 relative and absolute scaled residual norms), Torch-DPhys forms the exact residual Jacobian via AD and solves each Newton step using a direct lower–upper (LU) factorization with cubic backtracking, whereas SAM-Lite uses preconditioned Jacobian-free Newton–Krylov (PJFNK). ## 5 Execution and Computational Performance 

We use the 200-step axisymmetric MSFR transient and its 200-step discrete adjoint to evaluate the computational performance of eager and compiled CPU and GPU execution in Torch-DPhys alongside serial and MPI-parallelized SAM-Lite. 

5.1 Sparse Linear Algebra, Compilation, and GPU Execution 

At each Newton iteration and adjoint step, the 5,596-degree-of-freedom MSFR problem requires solving a sparse linear system whose Jacobian contains 182,809 nonzeros ( 0.58% fill). To handle the wide dynamic range of the unscaled matrix entries (condition number ∼ 5.5 × 10 16 ), Torch-DPhys applies row equilibration followed by sparse direct LU factorization and one step of iterative refinement (Appendix B.5). On the CPU, sparse factorizations use SciPy’s SuperLU backend [19, 20] with column approximate minimum degree (COLAMD) ordering; on the GPU, factorizations use NVIDIA cuDSS [21] invoked through a custom C++ PyTorch operator. Topology-dependent sparsity maps are precomputed once and reused across all steps. In compiled execution, torch.compile generates fused kernels for the finite-element residual, vector–Jacobian products (VJPs), and element-Jacobian assembly, while Python retains outer Newton and time-stepping control. On the GPU, compiled kernels are captured within CUDA graphs to reduce host launch overhead, and all state vectors, sparse matrix values, and adjoint history records remain resident in device memory throughout the transient. Moreover, an auxiliary-variable reuse pass evaluates intermediate constitutive and stabilization quantities once per accepted adjoint step rather than repeating them across dependency checks and Jacobian preparation, preserving bitwise-identical gradients while reducing adjoint overhead. 13 5.2 200-Step CPU/GPU Performance Benchmark

6.2 Solver-in-the-Loop Differentiable Predictive Control 

Beyond calibrating scalar physical parameters, end-to-end differentiability enables solver-in-the-loop training of neural network components embedded in a dynamical system. We demonstrate this capability using differentiable predictive control (DPC) [28] to train a closed-loop neural feedback controller for the three-loop electrically heated thermal-fluid test facility whose computer-aided design (CAD) representation is shown in Figure 13. In previous work on this facility [3, 4], control-oriented forecasting and policy training relied on learned Graph Neural ODE surrogates, which can introduce surrogate prediction error when the trained policy is deployed on the mechanistic plant model. Here, we eliminate the intermediate dynamics surrogate by backpropagating closed-loop control objectives directly through a PyTorch reimplementation of the facility’s SAM thermal-hydraulic plant model [10]. As illustrated in Figure 14(a), the neural policy is parameterized as a single-layer Gated Recurrent Unit (GRU) with 128 hidden units. At each 1 Hz control interval, the GRU receives 12 simulated plant sensor measurements along with conditioning inputs (upcoming power demand setpoint, secondary inlet temperature, and integrated tracking error) and outputs four actuator commands regulating the 18 Figure 13: CAD representation of the three-loop thermal-fluid facility modeled in the differentiable predictive control demonstration (HX: heat exchanger). core heater power, primary and intermediate pump heads, and tertiary loop mass flow rate. The differentiable plant solver advances the coupled thermal-hydraulic state over five 0.2 s substeps per control interval across a 60 s horizon (300 plant steps total). A composite trajectory loss penalizing thermal power tracking error, outlet temperature deviation, actuator slew rate, and heater-rod temperature exceedance above a 97 ◦C soft barrier (below the 100 ◦C safety limit) is then differentiated backward through all 300 plant steps to update the GRU weights via gradient descent. To balance the competing terms in the control objective, an outer Bayesian optimization loop (Figure 14b), initialized with 20 Sobol trials followed by 20 Gaussian-process-guided trials across four independent chains (160 trained policies total, following the ensemble tuning strategy of Abulawi et al. [2]), selects the loss weights and temporal discount factor. Because all plant rollouts and backpropagation occur offline during training, online deployment requires only a single forward pass of the 128-unit GRU at each 1 Hz control step. Figure 15 shows the closed-loop responses of the four best-performing policies (one selected from each Bayesian optimization chain) across four representative power-demand profiles. Across the four trajectories, the policies achieve full-window mean absolute power-tracking errors between 99 W and 

157 W while constraining the maximum heater-rod surface temperature to 94 .5 ◦C, below both the 

97 ◦C soft penalty barrier and the 100 ◦C limit.

We have presented Torch-DPhys, a PyTorch/LibTorch-native framework that unifies selected finite-element and finite-volume physical models from MOOSE THM, SAM, and continuum mechanics with automatic differentiation and machine learning workflows. By lowering object-oriented com-ponent definitions and finite-element spaces into tensor operators, Torch-DPhys evaluates physical residuals, assembles sparse Jacobians, and propagates trajectory sensitivities within the same compu-tational environment used to train neural networks and run gradient-based optimizers. The framework supports both unrolled differentiation through executed solver steps and discrete adjoints of con-verged implicit residuals, allowing users to trade off implementation simplicity against backward-pass memory and compute cost. Forward numerical verification confirms that the implemented tensor operators reproduce the discrete behavior of the reference codes on the tested configurations. In two-dimensional plane-strain elasticity, Torch-DPhys matches archived MOOSE nodal displacements to within 2.4 × 10 −13 relative 

L2 difference. Across three one-dimensional THM channel and loop transients—incorporating HLLC convective fluxes, volume and parallel-channel junctions, conjugate heat structures, pumps, counterflow heat exchangers, and PID control—Torch-DPhys reproduces MOOSE temperature histories within 0.096 K and mass-flow histories within 0.14% . On the two-dimensional axisymmetric MSFR initialization transient, Torch-DPhys and SAM-Lite independently select identical 200-step adaptive schedules and agree to within a maximum normalized field difference of 1.13 × 10 −5

(1.94 × 10 −5 K in temperature and 2.85 × 10 −2 Pa in pressure). Transient sensitivity verification confirms that the BDF2 discrete adjoint matches tangent-linear sensitivities to 7.37 × 10 −10 across 39 parameter directions on 40-step windows and to 4.2 × 10 −13 across four directions over the full 200-step trajectory. On the 200-step MSFR benchmark, combining sparse direct linear algebra (SuperLU on CPU and cuDSS on GPU) with torch.compile reduces median forward execution time from 49 .50 s to 

29 .30 s on a four-thread CPU configuration and from 114 .22 s to 22 .65 s on an NVIDIA RTX 2000 Ada GPU. Reusing auxiliary-variable evaluations across the adjoint dependency checks lowers the compiled GPU adjoint runtime by 18 .7% (to 33 .09 s), compared with 24 .05 s on the four-thread CPU. Component-level profiling indicates that CPU forward runtime is primarily bounded by single-threaded sparse LU factorization ( 49% of step cost), whereas GPU adjoint runtime is bounded by uncompiled host/device dependency discovery (approximately 57% of the reconstructed post-reuse allocation). The two application studies illustrate how these verified gradients support inverse problems and hybrid physics–ML. In the 1D thermal-hydraulic loop, L-BFGS-B optimization driven by either unrolled AD or the discrete residual adjoint recovers nominal pump head and heating power from sparse transient observations, achieving relative errors below 0.06% when fitting synthetic MOOSE data while requiring 10 × less backward time and 8× less peak memory with the residual adjoint than with checkpointed unrolled differentiation. In the three-loop facility demonstration, differentiating a 300-step closed-loop trajectory objective directly through the SAM-derived plant solver trains a GRU feedback policy that tracks multi-step power demands while maintaining peak heater rod temperatures below the 100 ◦C limit, avoiding the surrogate mismatch incurred when training controllers on standalone neural ODE emulators. Several scope boundaries qualify the reported results and define directions for future work. First, while close agreement between Torch-DPhys and MOOSE or SAM-Lite verifies that the tensor implementations reproduce the reference discrete formulations at the tested tolerances, cross-code agreement does not by itself establish spatial or temporal continuum convergence. In the axisymmetric MSFR study, because the standard SUPG/PSPG stabilization coefficients τc, τm, and τe depend explicitly on ∆t, refining the time step alters the stationary discrete spatial operator, shifting the endpoint inlet pressure by 5.8% and retaining a 1.65 –1.90% velocity-field mass-flux imbalance on the coarse mesh. Systematic spatial mesh refinement and time-step-independent stabilization formulations are therefore needed to establish continuum accuracy. In addition, the MSFR transient adjoint differentiates trajectories conditional on a fixed time-step schedule and recorded retry history; it does not differentiate through adaptive step-size selection or discrete branch switches, nor does it currently account for parameter dependence in the initial state. 21 From an architectural standpoint, the structural mechanics, one-dimensional THM, reduced SAM control plant, and two-dimensional axisymmetric MSFR modules currently operate with separate data structures and solver interfaces. Unifying these interfaces and exposing the MSFR discrete adjoint as an end-to-end PyTorch autograd function will extend solver-in-the-loop training from control policies to embedded neural turbulence closures—building on offline SAM-ML workflows [1, 5]—as well as coupled fluid–structure interaction and MOOSE MultiApp integration via LibTorch. Eliminating the remaining uncompiled dependency-probing overhead in the GPU adjoint and reusing symbolic sparse factorizations will further reduce turnaround times for multi-step gradient evaluations. Finally, because the parameter-identification and DPC demonstrations reported here use noiseless synthetic observations and simulated plant rollouts, future work will evaluate these workflows on noisy measurements from instrumented test facilities and incorporate the ensemble uncertainty propagation and Bayesian calibration methods outlined in Section 6.3.

## C Execution Configuration and Compilation Details 

C.1 GPU Backend Verification and Device Residency 

On the NVIDIA RTX 2000 Ada GPU (PyTorch 2.13.0+cu129, Triton 3.7.1, cuDSS 0.8.0.10, float64 

precision, fused multiply–add (FMA) contraction disabled), the sparse GPU backend matches the CPU SuperLU implementation on the 40-step and 10-step retry windows within maximum normalized state differences of 1.08 × 10 −14 and 1.26 × 10 −14 , and maximum blockwise gradient differences of 1.78 × 10 −12 and 8.91 × 10 −12 , respectively. To reduce kernel launch overhead during element-Jacobian assembly on the GPU, deterministic atomic accumulation is relaxed only on element-assembly passes verified at initialization to have duplicate-free write indices, while global assembly, residual VJPs, and linear solves retain deterministic execution. Combining device convergence checks into a single scalar host read per compiled call reduces host–device scalar reads from 361 to 249 per Newton step and from 128 to 68 per adjoint step, keeping all field tensors, sparse matrix buffers, and trajectory history records resident on the GPU.
