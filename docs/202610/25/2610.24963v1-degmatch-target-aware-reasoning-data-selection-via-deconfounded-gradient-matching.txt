Abstract 

Reasoning post-training on long trajectories has substantially improved the reasoning ca-pabilities of large language models, but scal-ing such training to large datasets is increas-ingly expensive. We study target-aware rea-soning data selection , where a small reference set specifies a target capability and guides the selection of training trajectories for specializa-tion. We find that existing target-aware meth-ods can exhibit limited target discrimination on long reasoning trajectories, while dominant population-level structure in candidate gradi-ents can obscure finer target-dependent vari-ation during gradient matching. To address these limitations, we introduce De confounded 

Gradient Match ing ( DeGMatch ), which first constructs a target-relevance gate and then reranks candidates using model-specific gra-dients. DeGMatch centers gradients using the candidate-population mean and removes lead-ing principal directions before target matching, reducing the influence of dominant population-level structure and improving target discrim-ination. Across mathematics, science, and code, DeGMatch outperforms strong heuris-tic, target-aware, and reasoning-specific data selection baselines, while remaining effective across selection budgets, model families, and scales. Our code is publicly available at https: //github.com/CHLee0801/degmatch .

1 Introduction 

Recent advances in large language models have yielded substantial improvements in complex rea-soning capabilities (DeepSeek-AI, 2025; Yang et al., 2025). Reasoning post-training has emerged as a standard approach to enhancing these capabil-ities through training on long reasoning trajecto-ries (Guha et al., 2026; Chen et al., 2025a; Yuan et al., 2025; Zhao et al., 2025a). However, the growing scale of reasoning datasets and the length of individual trajectories make full-data training in-creasingly computationally expensive. This has

motivated growing interest in data-efficient rea-soning post-training, where carefully selected sub-sets of reasoning trajectories can achieve compet-itive performance with substantially less training data (Yang et al., 2026b; Li et al., 2025; Yang et al., 2026d; Yu et al., 2026; Li et al., 2026a). While data-efficient selection seeks compact sub-sets that are broadly useful for post-training, many real-world applications require specialization to a particular downstream task or capability rather than broad, general-purpose competence (Xia et al., 2024). For such specialization, training examples that benefit general reasoning performance may not be those that best support the target capability. This motivates target-aware data selection , which aims to identify training examples that most effectively support a specified downstream objective. Prior work has explored this paradigm in instruction tun-ing and general data-selection settings, using tar-get similarity or distribution matching to identify target-relevant examples, and gradient-based sig-nals to estimate their training utility for a specified arXiv:2610.24963v1 [cs.CL] 18 Oct 2026 target (Liu et al., 2024b; Ivison et al., 2025; Lee et al., 2024; Xia et al., 2024; Feng et al., 2025; Min et al., 2026). However, applying gradient-based matching to long reasoning trajectories reveals an important lim-itation. We find that candidate gradients are domi-nated by a small number of population-level direc-tions: across five mathematical target-conditioned candidate populations, the top 50 principal compo-nents explain 86.7–89.4% of the centered-gradient variance. The leading principal subspaces also re-main substantially aligned across candidate pop-ulations induced by different targets. As shown in Figure 1, rankings formed from the dominant subspace exhibit limited fine-grained target sep-aration, whereas removing these dominant direc-tions yields more distinguishable target-dependent ranking structure. These results suggest that dom-inant population-level gradient structure can ob-scure finer target-dependent variation during gradi-ent matching. Motivated by this analysis, we introduce DeG-Match , which suppresses dominant population-level structure in candidate gradients to better ex-pose target-dependent variation during gradient matching. DeGMatch first restricts selection to a target-relevant candidate region. Within this re-gion, it computes gradients with respect to the model being post-trained and, inspired by common-component removal in representation learning (Mu et al., 2018), centers gradients using the candidate-population mean and removes the leading principal directions estimated from the candidate gradients before target matching. DeGMatch then scores candidates using directional alignment with target gradients together with their gradient magnitude in the target-gradient subspace. Empirically, DeGMatch consistently outper-forms strong heuristic, target-aware, and reasoning-specific data selection baselines across multiple reasoning domains. On mathematical reasoning, it achieves the strongest aggregate performance among selection methods and the best result on six of eight benchmarks, while also outperforming competing selection methods on scientific reason-ing and code generation. Further analyses show that DeGMatch remains effective across different deconfounding settings and selection budgets, as well as across model families and scales. Our contributions are as follows: • We identify limited target discrimination in target-aware selection on long reasoning tra-jectories and show that, for gradient-based matching, dominant population-level structure in candidate gradients can obscure finer target-dependent variation. • We introduce DeGMatch , which combines target-relevant retrieval with deconfounded, model-specific gradient matching for efficient reasoning specialization. • We show that DeGMatch outperforms strong heuristic, target-aware, and reasoning-specific baselines across reasoning domains, and re-mains effective across deconfounding settings and selection budgets, as well as across model families and scales. 

2 Related Work 

2.1 Target-Aware Data Selection 

Target-aware data selection builds on broader data-selection literature, which uses signals such as qual-ity, complexity, or diversity (Chen et al., 2024; Lu et al., 2024; Liu et al., 2024a), likelihood or learn-ability (Mindermann et al., 2022; Li et al., 2024b,a), semantic representations (Chen et al., 2025b), and model-dependent utility (Agarwal et al., 2025). Target-aware approaches further condition selec-tion on a downstream task or reference set through distribution matching (Xie et al., 2023; Liu et al., 2024b), target similarity (Ivison et al., 2025; Lee et al., 2024), or optimization-based signals (Xia et al., 2024; Feng et al., 2025; Zhao et al., 2025b; Min et al., 2026; Yang et al., 2026c). These meth-ods have primarily been studied in instruction-tuning or general data-selection settings, whereas we investigate their transfer to long reasoning tra-jectories and address limited target discrimination in gradient matching. 

2.2 Reasoning-Specific Data Selection 

Reasoning-data curation has explored problem dif-ficulty and trajectory structure (Yang et al., 2026b; Li et al., 2025; Muennighoff et al., 2025; Ye et al., 2025), step-level likelihood or uncertainty (Just et al., 2025; Wu et al., 2025), and length con-founding in likelihood-based selection (Wang et al., 2026). Student-dependent signals include rank– surprisal, token entropy, perturbation-based loss dy-namics, learnability, skill coverage, and capability– difficulty alignment (Yang et al., 2026d; Li et al., 2026c; Jin et al., 2026; Yu et al., 2026; Zhang et al., Figure 2: Overview of DeGMatch . DeGMatch constructs a target-relevant gate, deconfounds the candidate gradient geometry, and ranks candidates using target-conditioned directional alignment and target-subspace magnitude to select the final training subset. 

2026b; Yang et al., 2026a). Other work adapts the generation or selection of teacher supervision to the student (He et al., 2026; Yun et al., 2026; Cui et al., 2026; Hu et al., 2026; Zhang et al., 2026a) or uses gradient-related signals for curation and reinforcement learning (Li et al., 2026a,b). Un-like these approaches, which generally emphasize reasoning-data quality or student suitability, we condition selection on an explicit target capability specified by a small reference set. 

2.3 Gradient-Based Data Selection and Geometry 

Gradient information has been widely used for in-fluence estimation (Koh and Liang, 2017; Pruthi et al., 2020; Kwon et al., 2024), subset and core-set selection (Mirzasoleiman et al., 2020; Killam-setty et al., 2021b,a; Paul et al., 2021), and target-conditioned data selection (Xia et al., 2024; Feng et al., 2025; Zhao et al., 2025b; Min et al., 2026). Prior work also shows that gradient representa-tions can exhibit substantial covariance, low-rank, and spectral structure (Achille et al., 2019; Rame et al., 2022; Wang et al., 2023; Fan et al., 2026), and that normalization or whitening can substan-tially change their similarity geometry (Ma et al., 2024; Feng et al., 2025). Common-component removal has similarly been used to suppress domi-nant shared directions in representation spaces (Mu et al., 2018). Building on these geometric perspec-tives, we suppress dominant candidate-population directions before target matching to improve dis-crimination across targets. 

3 DeGMatch

3.2 Target-Relevance Gate 

We first restrict gradient-based selection to trajec-tories whose problems are semantically related to the target capability. Using a frozen embedding model ϕ, we encode only the problem statements and rank candidates for each target reference by cosine similarity between their normalized embed-dings. We construct a relevance gate G of fixed size 

G > B by cycling through the target references and adding each reference’s highest-ranked unseen candidate until |G| = G. This stage narrows the search space before applying the more expensive student-specific gradient analysis. Embedding sim-ilarity is used only to construct G and does not enter the final DeGMatch score. The resulting gate also defines the candidate population from which we estimate shared gradient structure in the next stage. 3.3 Deconfounded Gradient Geometry 

Within G, we construct model-specific gradient rep-resentations for target-conditioned selection. Prior work on representation geometry shows that a shared mean and a small number of dominant prin-cipal directions can disproportionately shape pair-wise similarity, and that removing these common components can expose more discriminative varia-tion (Mu et al., 2018). We find an analogous phe-nomenon in reasoning gradients: candidate gradi-ents exhibit strongly concentrated population-level structure, while raw target-conditioned rankings remain highly similar across different target refer-ences, as detailed in Appendix B. This motivates removing dominant candidate-population structure before target matching.

4 Experimental Setup 

4.1 Data and Target References 

We evaluate mathematics, science, and code using OpenR1-Math-220k (Open-R1 Team, 2025c) and the science and CodeForces-CoT portions of Open-R1 Mixture-of-Thoughts (Open-R1 Team, 2025a), yielding 93K, 53K, and 26K trajectories, respec-tively. Each target uses 50 disjoint reference prob-lems paired with verified DeepSeek-R1 trajectories. Pool and reference-set construction, including con-tamination filtering, are detailed in Appendices A.1 and A.2. 

4.2 Baselines 

We compare DeGMatch against heuristic, target-aware, and reasoning-specific data-selection meth-ods. Random uniformly samples from the can-didate pool. Length selects the longest trajecto-ries, following prior work showing that response or reasoning-trace length provides a simple but strong signal for post-training data selection (Zhao et al., 2024; Yang et al., 2026b; Guha et al., 2026; Min et al., 2026). MaxPPL selects examples with the highest student-model perplexity as a simple proxy for difficulty. Target-aware baselines include representation-based RDS+ (Ivison et al., 2025), gradient-based 

LESS (Xia et al., 2024) and GIST (Min et al., 2026), and optimal-transport-based TAROT (Feng et al., 2025). Reasoning-specific baselines include RSR (Yang et al., 2026d), which combines student token rank and surprisal; TEMP (Jin et al., 2026), which uses perturbation-based loss trajectories; and Se-lect2Reason (Yang et al., 2026b), which uses student-relative difficulty and trajectory character-istics. 1 We use matched candidate pools, selection budgets, and downstream training recipes wherever applicable; full implementation and adaptation de-tails are provided in Appendix A.4. We additionally report the initial student model and OpenR1-Qwen-7B (Open-R1 Team, 2025d) as reference points; the latter is treated as a full-data reference rather than a budget-matched selection baseline.

All selected subsets are fine-tuned from the same initialization under an identical training recipe. For the seman-tic relevance stage of DeGMatch, we use Qwen3-

4.4 Evaluation 

For mathematical reasoning, we build on the eval-uation setting used in Select2Reason (Yang et al., 2026b) and evaluate on AIME24, AIME25, and AIME26 (Mathematical Association of America, 2025a), AMC23 (Mathematical Association of America, 2025b), HMMT Feb. 2025 (MathArena, 2025), MATH500 (Lightman et al., 2024), and Gaokao 2024–2025 (FrankieYao). 6.2 Data Efficiency Across Selection Budgets 

We vary the selection budget from 1K to 15K trajectories and report average performance over AIME24, AIME25, and AIME26 as a function of both the number of selected examples and the re-sulting training-token volume in Figure 3. DeGMatch remains effective across all selection budgets, matching or outperforming the strongest baselines at each scale and achieving its largest ad-vantage at 10K examples, where it also surpasses the full-data reference (41.37 vs. 39.4). The token-based view further shows that these gains are not explained by selecting longer trajectories, as DeG-Match maintains a favorable performance–token trade-off relative to Length and RDS+. Overall, the budget sweep shows that the advantage of DeG-Match is not confined to a particular subset size and that strong performance can be achieved with substantially less training data than full-data train-ing. 

6.3 Robustness Across Student Models 

Finally, we test whether DeGMatch transfers be-yond the primary 7B student using three settings: Qwen2.5-3B-Base (Qwen Team, 2025) tests scal-ing within the same model family, Qwen3-1.7B-Base (Yang et al., 2025) introduces a different Qwen generation at a smaller scale, and Phi-3.5-mini-Instruct (Microsoft, 2024) provides a cross-family comparison with a different pretraining and instruction-tuning recipe. For each student, we recompute candidate and target gradients and in-dependently select a model-specific subset under the same 3K budget. We exclude TEMP because it requires a parameter-compatible trained endpoint for each student. Across these settings, DeGMatch remains con-sistently competitive and achieves the strongest overall performance on two of the three students. Its advantage also does not come from selecting substantially longer trajectories, as it uses fewer training tokens than Length and RDS+ across all three models. These results suggest that the se-lection principle is not specific to the primary 7B student and transfers across changes in model scale, generation, and family. 

7 Conclusion 

We study target-aware reasoning data selection ,where a small reference set specifies a target ca-pability to guide the selection of training trajec-tories. We introduce DeGMatch , which com-bines target-relevant retrieval with model-specific gradient matching in a deconfounded gradient space. By centering gradients and suppressing dominant candidate-population principal direc-tions, DeGMatch reduces the influence of dom-inant population-level structure that can obscure target-dependent variation during gradient match-ing. Across mathematics, science, and code, DeG-Match achieves strong performance against heuris-tic, target-aware, and reasoning-specific baselines while remaining effective across selection bud-gets, model families, and scales, supporting de-confounded gradient matching for target-aware rea-soning data selection. Limitations 

Despite the effectiveness of DeGMatch, several directions remain open. First, our experiments fo-cus on small- to medium-scale student models, and the scalability of student-gradient-based selection to substantially larger models remains to be stud-ied. Second, we consider selection from existing reasoning-trajectory pools; jointly optimizing data generation, evolution, and selection may further improve post-training efficiency. Finally, while our analysis shows that removing dominant shared gra-dient structure improves target-conditioned match-ing, understanding what these gradient directions represent in terms of reasoning skills and behaviors remains an open question for future work.

A Experimental Details 

A.1 Candidate-Pool Construction 

Table 5 summarizes the candidate pools used in our experiments. We retain at most one reasoning trajectory per problem in all domains. Token counts are measured using the student-model tokenizer over the complete training sequence. Problems for which no verified trajectory is obtained within the attempt budget are discarded and replaced by another randomly sampled eligible problem. For OpenR1-Math-220k (Open-R1 Team, 2025c), we use the default split and retain the first verified DeepSeek-R1 trajectory associated with each problem. For Mixture-of-Thoughts (Open-R1 Team, 2025a), we retain one trajectory per problem using a fixed random seed. The resulting science and code pools contain 53,195 and 26,061 unique problems, respectively. No retained example exceeds the 32,768-token training context. 

Contamination check. We check each candidate pool against its corresponding evaluation bench-marks for both exact overlap and near-duplicate problems. We find no evaluation problems in the final candidate pools under our contamination cri-teria. This check is performed before data selec-tion, so all selection methods operate on the same contamination-filtered candidate pool. 

Analysis metadata. For post-hoc analyses of the mathematical candidate pool, we use the sub-ject and source metadata distributed with OpenR1-Math-220k. Subject categories are taken directly from the dataset-provided problem_type field, which contains Algebra, Geometry, Number The-ory, Combinatorics, Logic and Puzzles, Calculus, Inequalities, and Other; we use these labels without remapping. We define the Olympiad indicator as whether the dataset-provided source field equals 

olympiads . This indicator reflects dataset prove-nance rather than an inferred measure of difficulty and does not include examples assigned to other contest-related source tags. These metadata are used only for post-hoc analysis of DeGMatch and do not enter its candidate scoring or selection.

Live-CodeBench is a continuously updated code-generation benchmark designed to reduce contami-nation from previously released programming prob-lems. CodeForces. We use the training split of the Open-R1 CodeForces collection (Open-R1 Team) as the reference source and its disjoint test split for evaluation. Relevance gate. We use Qwen3-Embedding-8B (Zhang et al., 2025) as the frozen embedding model. Only the problem statement is embedded; reason-ing trajectories and auxiliary instructions are ex-cluded. Inputs are truncated to 2,048 tokens, left padded, and represented by the final-token hidden state followed by ℓ2 normalization. For each target reference, we independently rank the complete candidate pool by cosine similarity. Starting from retrieval depth k = 1 , we cycle through target references in a fixed order and con-sider the k-th nearest candidate of each reference. A candidate is added only if it has not already been included in the gate; duplicates are skipped. We then advance to the next retrieval depth and repeat until the desired gate size is reached. We set the gate size to three times the final selection budget, corresponding to 30K candidates for mathematics, 15K for science, and 9K for code. Embedding similarity is used only to construct the gate and is discarded before gradient-based reranking.

Training cost. Training cost depends substan-tially on the number of tokens contained in the selected subset. A 10K-example mathematical sub-set trained for five epochs typically requires ap-proximately eight hours on the same eight-GPU node, corresponding to roughly 60–70 GPU-hours per run. Representative DeGMatch runs require approximately 7.8–8.4 hours, compared with 6.6 hours for Random and 9.5 hours for Length. This variation reflects differences in the total number and length of selected training tokens rather than differences in the optimization procedure. Science and code experiments use smaller example budgets and correspondingly shorter training runs.

Across all nine target capabilities, generating 450 verified reference trajectories uses approximately 5.20M output tokens and costs $12.75 in total, or about $1.42 per target on average. B.7 Target Structure in Rankings Induced by Reference Subsets 

The analyses above show that removing domi-nant candidate-population structure makes rank-ings more responsive to changes in the supplied tar-get references. We next ask whether this increased sensitivity reveals target-dependent structure rather than arbitrary dispersion. For each of the five mathematical targets, we partition the reference set into disjoint 10-example subsets and independently compute the candidate ranking induced by each subset. We repeat the partitioning multiple times and score the same fixed 30K AIME relevance gate in every case, so changes in the ranking geometry cannot be attributed to changes in the candidate population. Distances between two induced rankings are defined as one minus their Spearman correlation. Figure 9 decomposes the effect of deconfound-ing step by step. Under raw gradients, target iden-tity can be recovered from an induced ranking with 56% accuracy, compared with a 20% five-way chance level. Population centering alone changes this only marginally to 57%. Likewise, retaining only the top 50 principal components of the cen-tered gradients yields 56% target identification. In contrast, removing the population mean and these dominant components increases target identifica-tion to 71%. For target identification, each reference-subset query is assigned to the target whose other disjoint reference subsets have the highest average Spear-man correlation with its induced ranking. The iden-tification score is therefore measured directly in the original candidate-ranking space, rather than from the two-dimensional visualization. The close behavior of the centered and dominant-subspace representations further indicates that mean removal alone does not eliminate the domi-nant population variation. Across the reference-subset queries, rankings produced after center-ing and rankings produced using only the top-50 principal-component subspace have Spearman cor-relations of 0.997–0.999, with 94–96% overlap among their top-3K candidates. B.10 Estimating Population Structure Within the Relevance Gate 

Our default implementation estimates the mean and principal directions from all examples inside the relevance gate. Table 21 compares this choice with subsampled and full-pool alternatives. DeGMatch defines the dominant population structure relative to the can-didate population being reranked. Estimating this structure from a small random subset introduces additional sampling variability, whereas estimating it from the full pool incorporates variation outside the target-relevant candidate region. We therefore estimate the mean and principal directions from the complete relevance gate.

C Analysis of the Relevance Gate 

We analyze how the relevance gate shapes the can-didate region before gradient-based reranking. This section examines the first stage of DeGMatch and verifies that the relevance gate restricts gradient-based selection to a target-relevant candidate region without determining the final selected subset. Un-less otherwise specified, the analyses use the AIME target with a 10K final selection budget. The subset-level diagnostics characterize how the gate changes the candidate region and selected data, while Sec-tion C.1 additionally evaluates downstream sensi-tivity to gate size. 

C.1 Effect of Gate Size 

The gate size controls how far gradient-based selec-tion can search beyond the trajectories most similar to the target references. Table 23 varies the gate size while keeping the final selection budget fixed at B = 10 K. A smaller gate constrains selection to trajecto-ries that are more semantically similar to the target references, while expanding the gate gives gradient matching access to longer and higher-NLL trajec-tories. When G = B, the gate itself determines the final subset, reducing selection to semantic re-trieval. At the other extreme, removing the gate increases the selected token volume from 68.9M to 115.8M and reduces AIME similarity from 0.531 to 0.493. The default 3B gate lies between these two extremes. It retains a target-relevant candidate region while leaving two-thirds of the gated ex-amples available for student-specific reranking. These subset-level statistics alone do not establish a downstream-optimal gate size; we therefore eval-uate downstream sensitivity separately below. 

Downstream sensitivity to gate size. The pre-ceding diagnostics characterize how the relevance gate changes the candidate region and the resulting selected subset. We additionally evaluate whether these differences translate to downstream perfor-mance. Using a fixed 3K final selection budget, we vary the AIME relevance-gate size while keeping the gradient representation, scoring rule, and down-stream training procedure unchanged. Table 24 reports the resulting downstream performance. Performance remains similar across the finite gate sizes, with the 30K gate achieving the strongest average among the evaluated settings. In contrast, removing the relevance restriction and reranking over the full candidate pool reduces the average score to 33.90. These results suggest that the benefit of the relevance stage is not tied to a narrowly tuned gate size: a range of target-restricted candidate regions performs comparably, while eliminating the gate altogether is less effec-tive. C.2 Embedding Representation and Retrieval Aggregation 

The round-robin construction in Section 3.2 is de-signed to prevent references with unusually dense neighborhoods from dominating the relevance gate. Table 25 compares the default construction with alternative embedding representations and aggrega-tion rules, while fixing the gate size at 30K. Round-robin retrieval provides substantially more balanced coverage across target references. Under the default construction, even the least-covered reference retains 893 of its 1,000 near-est candidates, whereas the minimum coverage falls to 96 under max-similarity aggregation and to 40 under mean-similarity aggregation. Notably, max-similarity aggregation achieves slightly higher overall AIME similarity than round-robin (0.522 vs. 0.519) despite this sharp reduction in worst-case coverage. This supports round-robin retrieval as a coverage-preserving rather than similarity-maximizing design, preventing the gate from con-centrating around only a subset of reference neigh-borhoods. The choice of embedding representation also matters. Replacing Qwen3-Embedding-8B with hidden states from the initial student yields a gate with a Jaccard similarity of 0.30 to the default gate and reduces both neighborhood coverage and AIME similarity. We therefore use a dedicated em-bedding model to identify the semantically relevant candidate region and reserve the student model for the subsequent gradient-based utility estimate.

D.2 Target-Specificity of the Selected Subsets

D.3 How Deconfounding Changes Candidate Rankings 

We finally examine which candidates undergo the largest rank changes after dominant-component re-moval. Table 30 compares trajectories that move from the raw-gradient top 1K to below rank 10K with trajectories that move from outside the raw top 10K into the DeGMatch top 1K. The promoted examples are not simply shorter or lower-loss trajectories. In fact, candidates mov-ing into the DeGMatch top 1K are longer and have higher median student NLL than those strongly demoted by deconfounding. This provides an addi-tional indication that dominant-component removal does not act as a simple correction for trajectory length or difficulty. The problem-type composition also changes: the promoted group contains more combinatorics and number-theory problems, whereas the demoted group contains more geometry and algebra prob-lems. We treat this shift as descriptive rather than as evidence that any particular subject category is intrinsically more useful. The important observa-tion is that removing dominant population structure substantially reorders candidates even when they occupy similar ranges of coarse trajectory charac-teristics.
