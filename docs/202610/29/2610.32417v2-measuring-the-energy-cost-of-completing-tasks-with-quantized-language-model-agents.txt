Measuring the Energy Cost of Completing Tasks with
Quantized Language-Model Agents
Stephen Abkin, Aldric Yang, Alex Flesher*, Nitheesh Kodarapu*
Deepak Panigrahy, Aakash Tyagi, David Kebo Houngninou
Texas A&M University
Quantization reduces the memory and energy needed to serve large language models (LLMs) and AI agents
for coding and data analysis can now run on locally hosted LLMs. In practice, this benefit is commonly
benchmarked per output token. Yet an agent calls its model repeatedly, runs tools, and revises its work, and
quantization can change both how much the agent generates and whether it succeeds. Existing energy studies
either compare quantized models on single requests or measure agents at one fixed precision, overlooking
how a change in precision plays out across a complete agent task.
To address this gap, this paper presents the first characterization of the task-level energy trade-offs of
model quantization in locally hosted agents. We develop 29 agent tasks with automatic pass/fail checks in six
families, ranging from single-answer SQL analytics to multi-step repository bug fixing, and measure CPU and
GPU energy per output token and per completed task for 4-bit and 8-bit versions of two open-weight models
on bare-metal NVIDIA A100 nodes with llama.cpp.
Our findings reveal that 4-bit precision generally saves energy per token, though whether it saves energy
per completed task is task dependent and changes with the model and the execution budget (i.e., limits on
turns, tokens, and time). We trace this discrepancy to the extra tokens needed to complete a task in full, which
translates into using up to 3.5 times as much energy per completed task. These findings offer actionable
guidance for developers and operators choosing model precision for agents, showing how tokens per completed
task, set against the per-token saving, reveal whether lower precision completes the work with less energy.
Keywords: energy measurement, quantization, LLM agents, local inference, task completion, energy efficiency
metrics

Introduction
Quantization is becoming a practical way to run LLMs locally on a single GPU instead of a provider’s
cluster. Models such as Qwen3 [56] and Devstral [37] can now drive coding and data agents on one
GPU, served by engines such as llama.cpp [13] from files that community repositories publish at
many precisions, and methods such as Samsung’s LittleBit already compress a 13-billion-parameter
model into under 1 GB [27]. Capable agents on consumer hardware are still emerging, but agents on
a single GPU that an organization owns are already a reality. There, the energy of every completed
task arrives on the electricity bill. An operator then chooses the precision of the model’s weights
to fit its memory and energy budget, and quantization appears to make this choice easy. A 4-bit
file needs less memory than an 8-bit one, usually generates each token with less energy, and often
keeps accuracy close to the unquantized model [15, 23]. Benchmarks such as TokenPowerBench
report exactly this energy per output token [38, 47, 54]. Yet the useful output of an agent is a
completed task, not a token. Completing a task can take many model calls, tool runs, and revisions,
and a quantized model may need more of them or fail more often. This raises the question, is the
cheaper precision per token also the cheaper one per completed task?
An agent turns a per-token saving into task energy only through its behavior. Each extra turn
(i.e., model response) adds a model call over a longer context, a response with a malformed tool
call wastes a turn, and a wrong submission discards the whole attempt. Quantization can add to

these costs. Jang et al. [21] show that quantized agents make more failed tool calls and need more
model responses and tool calls before finishing, and Pham et al. [42] find that failed attempts carry
a substantial energy cost of their own. Because these costs accumulate over the whole task, the task
itself is the natural unit of cost. In guidance published in September 2026, Anthropic [40] makes
this argument in terms of monetary cost, recommending that developers of coding agents measure
what a task costs rather than its price per token, since models with equal per-token prices can cost
very different amounts on the same task. Energy behaves the same way, even when both precisions
always succeed.
Motivation. Our motivation stems from quantization being one of the most accessible mecha-
nisms a developer has for running agents on their own hardware. Choosing a 4-bit instead of an 8-bit
quantized model needs no new hardware and no change to the agent, and it lowers both memory
and per-token energy. An agent runs the same kinds of tasks many times, so the energy cost of one
precision choice repeats with every task it completes. If the per-token saving carries through to
completed tasks, quantization is close to a free energy saving for local agents. If agent behavior
erases it, a developer could end up deploying the costlier precision while per-token benchmarks
point the other way. This calls for measuring quantization on the tasks an agent completes, where
its energy is actually spent.
Goal. To our knowledge, a task-level understanding of quantization’s energy is lacking. Prior
work reports energy per token or per request [5, 6, 18, 49], measures agents at a single precision [20,
34, 42], or compares precisions by correct answers on single-turn queries or with energy calculated
rather than read from hardware counters [12, 45]. To address this gap, we conduct a controlled
empirical study that measures each precision’s energy both per output token and per completed
task, and asks whether the two measures favor the same precision. We report the results as four
findings for developers and operators choosing model precision for agents, and address four research
questions, which Section 5 answers in order.
RQ1: Do energy per token and energy per completed task favor the same precision?
RQ2: Where they differ, how much extra energy per completed task does choosing by energy
per token cost?
RQ3: What in the agent’s behavior accounts for the extra energy, such as how much it
generates or how often it succeeds?
RQ4: How do the execution budget, the model, and the task set change which precision is
cheaper per completed task, and is an unquantized 16-bit model (which needs twice the
GPU memory) ever cheaper than the quantized files?
We run the 4-bit Q4_K_M and 8-bit Q8_0 files1 of Qwen3-30B-A3B [43] on 29 verifiable tasks
(i.e., an automatic check decides whether each attempt succeeds) and compare the precision that
each measure favors to address RQ1. Where the two measures favor different precisions, which we
call a disagreement, we measure the extra energy per completed task to answer RQ2. We trace each
difference to how much the agent generates, how often it succeeds, and how much energy it spends
outside model calls to answer RQ3. To address RQ4, we repeat the comparison with an expanded
budget, a 16-bit reference, Devstral Small 2 [37], and held-out tasks from the BIRD text-to-SQL and
EvalPerf code-efficiency benchmarks [28, 31].

Yet on some everyday coding and data-analysis tasks, 4-bit uses more energy per completed task,
beyond run-to-run variation. On such tasks, 4-bit uses a median of 66% more energy per completed
task than 8-bit, and in the worst supported case, 4-bit needs 3.5 times as much energy as 8-bit to
complete the same task (Section 5.2). Where the two measures disagree, the extra energy comes
from extra tokens, as the precision that is cheaper per token needs a median of 1.6–2.0 times as
many tokens per completed task. These tokens arise from malformed tool calls and unnecessary
reruns of tests that had already passed, which prolong attempts, as well as wrong answers that
cause attempts to fail (Section 5.3). As tasks scale in difficulty, the precision choice becomes more
concerned with completion rate, and which precision completes more depends on how much
context and how many turns the agent is given (Sections 5.1 and 5.4). These findings hold through
a second model, whose costly tasks differ from the first, and the patterns of our task families carry
over to held-out benchmark problems, correctly predicting where 4-bit is cheaper per completed
task. An unquantized 16-bit model is never clearly cheaper than the quantized files (Section 5.4).
We propose that precision should be chosen by measuring energy per completed task for the model
and tasks an agent will run, under the circumstances in which it will be deployed.
Contributions. We summarize our main contributions as follows:
• We characterize the task-level energy of quantized models in locally hosted agents across
29 verifiable tasks in six families and held-out benchmarks (Section 4).
• We build a measurement harness that runs the agent, attributes CPU and GPU energy to
each model and tool call, checks each attempt with hidden verifiers, and charges failed
attempts to completions (Sections 3 and 4).
• We empirically show that agent behavior can outweigh the per-token saving, making 4-
bit costlier per completed task on some tasks, by a margin beyond run-to-run variation
(Section 5).
• We derive an identity that splits energy per completed task into energy per token and tokens
per completed task, so a lower precision saves energy roughly as long as its extra tokens
per completed task stay below its per-token saving (Sections 3.2 and 6.1).

Background and Related Work
2.1
Quantized models for local inference
Local deployments commonly serve models with llama.cpp [13] from GGUF files2 [14], and com-
munity repositories publish each model at many precisions. A precision label such as Q4_K_M
names a quantization scheme, not only a bit width. Q4_K_M stores most tensors at 4 bits in blocks
that share a scale and an offset, and it keeps some attention and feed-forward tensors at 6 bits [24].
Our 4-bit files were also calibrated on sample text, so weights whose inputs are largest on that text
are rounded most carefully [1, 2]. Q8_0 rounds weights to 8 bits in blocks of 32 with one scale per
block and needs no calibration [33]. A BF16 file holds the original, unquantized weights in 16-bit
bfloat16. Because of the shared scales and the 6-bit tensors, each quantized file stores more bits per
weight than its label suggests (8.5 for Q8_0 and 4.88 for our 4-bit Qwen file). The 4-bit Qwen file is
thus about 30% the size of the 16-bit one rather than a quarter (Figure 1). The Devstral files use
the same quantized layouts. Devstral is released in 8-bit floating point, so it has no original 16-bit
weights.
Quantized models, including 4-bit ones, often stay close to their unquantized versions on general
and code-generation benchmarks [15, 23, 29, 53]. The loss is larger on long-context inputs [36] and
2GGUF is the single-file model format of llama.cpp. One file stores the weights together with the tokenizer and the model
metadata, so it fully specifies what the engine loads.

2.2
From energy per token to energy per completed task
Units beyond the token. Energy per output token is the common unit of inference bench-
marks [38, 47, 54], and studies that compare quantization methods report it beside accuracy without
combining the two [18, 49]. Several studies document its limits for whole requests. ML.ENERGY
reports energy per response because models differ in verbosity [5, 6]. Energy per request grows
with response length [19, 55], and Luo et al. [34] found that enabling a model’s thinking mode
generated 21 to 75% more tokens per problem at nearly unchanged energy per token. The mea-
sured boundary (i.e., which hardware components and periods the energy covers) matters as well.
GPU-only telemetry missed 41 to 45% of system energy on one agent server [34].
Charging energy to completions. Several measures already charge cost to successful outcomes.
The cost-of-pass of Erol et al. [10] divides the monetary cost of a model’s attempts by its success
rate. The energy per successful goal of Panigrahy and Tyagi [41] divides the energy of all attempts,
including failed ones, by the number of successes, and our energy per completed task (Section 3)
applies this goal-level accounting to the CPU packages and GPU board of the machine that runs
the model and its tools. Intelligence per Watt relates accuracy on single-turn queries to measured
energy across 16-, 8-, and 4-bit models [45], and TokenArena ranks endpoints by estimated joules
per correct answer [12]. We build on these measures by computing energy per output token and
energy per completed task on the same agent attempts, which shows when the two favor different
precisions of one model.
Agents and precision. For agents, Ifath and Haque [20] characterize multi-request workflows
under different serving setups, and Luo et al. [34] attribute a coding agent’s energy to components
and phases, both at a fixed precision. The two closest studies each hold one side of our question
fixed. Pham et al. [42] quantify the energy of failed runs of a local 4-bit agent, and Jang et al. [21]
vary precision without measuring energy, finding that a quantized agent can succeed as often while
making more failed tool calls. We combine the two sides, varying precision and measuring energy
on the same attempts, to test whether the extra tokens and failed attempts that a lower precision
induces outweigh its per-token saving.

3
Three Energy Measures
To see when energy per token and energy per completed task favor different precisions, we compute
both on the same agent attempts, together with a third measure between them, model-call energy
per completed task (Section 3.1). An algebraic identity (Equation (2)) links the three and shows
what can change the preferred precision.
3.1
Attempts and the three measures
An attempt is one run of the agent on one task at one precision (i.e., one model file served entirely
from the GPU). The attempt starts from a newly loaded model and ends when the agent submits
a result or reaches a limit of its execution budget (Section 4.3). During the attempt, the agent
alternates between model calls and tool calls and uses each result to decide its next action. After
the attempt, a deterministic research verifier checks the submitted result. It is the study’s own final
check, hidden from the agent and separate from the tests that the agent runs. An attempt that the
research verifier accepts is a verified completion, and every other attempt is a failure.
Figure 2a shows how an attempt accumulates energy, which we measure on the CPU packages
(i.e., processor sockets) and GPU board (i.e., the whole graphics card) of the machine that runs the
model and its tools. The model-call (inference) energy 𝐼𝑖of attempt 𝑖is the energy recorded during
its model calls, in which the model reads its input tokens (the prompt) and generates 𝑇𝑖output
tokens. The operational energy 𝑂𝑖is the energy of the whole task window, from the loaded model
to the end of the task. It adds every tool call, including agent-requested tests, and the time the
machine waits between calls. Model loading and the research verifier are measured separately and
excluded from 𝑂𝑖(Section 4.4). We exclude loading because a deployed server loads the model once
for many tasks, and Section 5.1 reports the worst case, one load per attempt. We write 𝑠𝑖= 1 if
attempt 𝑖is a verified completion and 𝑠𝑖= 0 otherwise.

• Model-call energy per completed task, 𝐸inf. It shares its numerator with 𝐸tok and its

All three are ratios of sums, so each attempt counts in proportion to its energy and tokens, and one
long attempt can outweigh several short ones. A precision that never completes a task (𝑆= 0) has
no 𝐸inf or 𝐸op on that task, and Section 3.3 covers such tasks.

Even the per-token ratio depends on what the agent did. Each precision’s 𝐸tok is measured on
its own attempts, and a model call spends energy reading its input tokens as well as generating
output tokens. A long tool output lengthens the prompt that the next call reads before generating,
so an attempt with long tool outputs and short responses has a higher energy per output token.
Break-even rule. Two precisions break even per completed task when they use the same energy
to complete it (𝜌op = 1). A precision that is cheaper per token stays below break-even only while
its per-token saving covers the extra tokens it needs. Formally, the token factor times the boundary
factor must stay below 1/𝜌tok. For example, in the business query (Figure 2b), 4-bit saves 14%
per token, enough to cover up to 16% more tokens (1/0.86 = 1.16), but it needs 59% more tokens
per completed task (all from longer attempts, since both precisions complete all ten), and with
a boundary factor of 0.94, the product is 1.59 × 0.94 = 1.49, above 1.16, so 4-bit uses 29% more
energy per completed task. Counting only the energy of model calls (𝐸inf) removes the boundary
factor, so the token factor alone must stay below 1/𝜌tok (the dashed curve in Figure 5).
3.3
Choosing a precision by each measure
Each measure picks a precision for every task. The per-token choice is the precision with the
lower 𝐸tok. The per-task choice is the precision with the lower 𝐸op. Table 1 covers every pattern
of completions, including tasks that one precision or neither completes. A task is comparable
when both precisions complete it at least three times, so that the choice never rests on one or two
lucky attempts. On a comparable task, the two measures disagree when the per-token and per-task
choices differ. We also use 𝐸inf to locate where a disagreement arises. If 𝐸inf already picks a different
precision than 𝐸tok, the disagreement comes from the tokens each completion takes (i.e., longer or
more failed attempts), and if only 𝐸op picks a different precision, it comes from tool and waiting
energy.

Experimental Methodology
Measuring energy per completed task asks more of an experiment than measuring energy per
token, and a fair comparison of two precisions must meet three requirements.

• Isolate precision. The two precisions of a comparison differ in the model file and share
the machine, engine build, tasks, tools, budget, and sampling seeds (Sections 4.1 and 4.3).
• Verify. A verifier hardened against known shortcuts decides whether each attempt succeeds,
and its own energy stays outside the task window (Section 4.2).
• Attribute. Attempts differ in length and outcome, so we attribute energy to each model
call and tool call and charge failed attempts to the completions (Section 4.4).

4.1
Testbed and models
Testbed. We run every attempt on bare-metal machines of the Chameleon testbed [25], so no
other tenant shares a machine. Each has two AMD EPYC 7763 processors and one NVIDIA A100
GPU with 40 GB of memory (80 GB on one machine). Model and tool work runs on eight cores of
the GPU-attached socket, and server memory is bound to that socket’s memory (i.e., its NUMA
node). These cores use the schedutil frequency governor at 1.5–2.45 GHz with idle states enabled.
Every attempt uses these settings, so host power policy is the same for every precision.
Models. We chose two models that differ in architecture and vendor and that fit entirely in
the memory of one 40 GB GPU at 4-bit and at 8-bit, so all computation stays on the GPU at
either precision. The main model, Qwen3-30B-A3B-Instruct-2507 [43, 56], is an instruction-tuned
mixture-of-experts release without a separate thinking mode. It has 30.5 billion parameters, of
which 3.3 billion are active per token (i.e., each token passes through a small subset of expert
sub-networks, while the file stores all 30.5 billion). The second model, Devstral Small 2 [37], is a
dense 24-billion-parameter coding model from another vendor. Both run on llama.cpp [13] at one
source revision.4 The model files are public GGUF conversions [1, 2], identified by hash and used
with the original release’s tokenizer and chat template (i.e., the format that turns messages and
tool calls into the token sequence the model was trained on).
Precisions. We compare the Q4_K_M and Q8_0 files, which differ in bit width, quantization
scheme, calibration (Section 2.1), and kernels. A developer chooses a file (not a bit width), so we
measure the joint effect of all four. The Qwen 16-bit file needs 57 GiB (Figure 1), so we run the
16-bit reference on the 80 GB machine, where every precision fits entirely in GPU memory at the
largest context we use.
4.2
Tasks and verification
Energy per completed task needs a completion criterion that is objective, reproducible, and identical
for every precision, so we use verifiable tasks, whose result an automatic check accepts or rejects,
as repository-level coding benchmarks do [22]. Success is then simply pass or fail, which keeps the
variance of a judged or graded score out of the energy comparison. Some problems come in two
input sizes, and counting each size as a task gives 29 tasks from 22 problems (Table 2).
Task families. The tasks cover everyday agent work, from a single SQL query to repository
repairs of up to 250 turns (i.e., model responses). Each of the six families lets precision change the
energy of a task in its own way. We wrote the first three.
• SQL analytics (6). The agent explores a sales database and submits one structured answer,
so one wrong submission fails the attempt.
• Data repair (2). The agent writes SQL that cleans and totals event records and can test it
on a visible database, so failed tests and repairs add energy within the attempt.

Verification. The research verifier alone decides success, so it must be resistant to gaming. It
runs once after submission and checks the result against references hidden from the agent, which
it computes independently of the agent’s approach. It ignores what submitted code reports about
its own success. Wherever an agent could pass by hard-coding the answer for the input it was
shown, it reruns the check on new inputs generated from private seeds. Before use, every verifier
had to accept the reference solutions and reject known shortcuts (e.g., constant answers, answers
hard-coded for the visible input, and forged success messages). During a task, read-only SQL access
and a Landlock ruleset5 [46] keep hidden inputs, expected outputs, and private seeds out of the
agent’s reach (Appendix A).
4.3
Agent protocol, budgets, and repetitions
Inference server. Each attempt starts a new inference server that serves one request at a time,
so no cached state or warm-up carries over between attempts. Within an attempt, the server keeps
the computation already done for earlier turns (i.e., the key-value cache) and processes only the
new part of each prompt. The harness renders the conversation (including the tool definitions) with
the model’s own chat template and sends the token identifiers to the native completion endpoint of
llama.cpp on the same machine, so it controls exactly which tokens each precision reads.
Turns and tool actions. The system message asks the model to solve one task with the provided
tools and to finish by submitting. Each family offers one task tool in place of a general shell, which
helps keep hidden material out of the agent’s reach. The tool has a few actions (e.g., for SQL
analytics, inspecting the schema, running a read-only query, and submitting). Tool calls run one at
a time between model calls, each in a new process, so every call’s energy is measured separately.
The parser accepts only well-formed tool calls in the chat template’s format and repairs nothing
except a missing closing tag at the end of a response. When a response has no valid call, the harness
keeps its text without the rejected call and replies with the parser error, so the response costs a
turn at any precision. The harness saves every response, tool call, and tool result and labels each
turn (e.g., a malformed call), and Section 5.3 counts agent behaviors from these records.
Setting the budgets. Limits on context, turns, tool calls, output tokens, and time keep every
attempt finite, and together they form its execution budget (Table 3). We fixed the primary budget
after pilot runs, setting each limit above what pilot attempts outside repository repair needed (e.g.,
a 600 s deadline well above the longest pilot attempt, and 2,048 tokens per turn for answers of about
1,100 tokens). The expanded budget’s turn limit equals the step limit of mini-SWE-agent [50, 57]
for SWE-bench [22], where a step is also one model response. Its context is the largest at which
every precision fits entirely in GPU memory. The model supports contexts of up to 262,144 tokens,
and the harness keeps the full conversation without truncation or summarization.
Outside repository repair, the limits rarely end a valid sampled attempt. With the primary budget,
389 of 399 attempts on the other families end by submitting, and with the expanded budget, all such
attempts end by submitting. However, about two thirds of primary-budget repository attempts end
at the context or turn limit for both precisions, which motivates the expanded budget. The limits
on tool calls, total output tokens, and time each end at most one valid sampled attempt (Table 8 in
Appendix B).

4.4
Energy measurement and attempt validity
Energy counters. Every 0.1 s, we read cumulative energy counters for both CPU packages
through Linux powercap [51] and for the GPU board through the NVIDIA Management Library [39].
The energy of an interval is the difference of two readings. It includes idle energy within the interval
and excludes DRAM, storage, fans, and power-supply losses. AMD package counters are estimates
from a power model [48], and the GPU sensor samples power intermittently [58], so we compare
precisions on the same machine. If a sensor reads high or low by the same factor for both precisions,
the 4-bit/8-bit energy ratio stays the same, and so does the precision it favors.
Measured periods. An attempt has three measured periods (Figure 2a). Model loading and
verification are reported in Appendix C, and the task window between them gives 𝑂𝑖. Within the
window, each model call and tool call is a span whose energy is interpolated between the 0.1 s
counter readings, and 𝐼𝑖sums the energy of the model-call spans.
Valid attempts. A valid attempt, one whose measurement is complete and undisturbed, passes
three checks:
• Its energy record is complete, and recomputing it from the raw counter readings gives the
stored value (Appendix C).
• Its token counts add up (i.e., reused plus newly read input tokens equal the input length).
• No process it started (e.g., a tool or server process) is still running when it ends, and a
monitor that samples CPU use about every 0.6 s finds no sustained activity by unrelated
processes.

4.5
Conditions
Each row of Table 4 is a condition, one combination of tasks, model, budget, and machine under
which we compare the precisions. The main experiment runs Qwen at 4-bit and 8-bit on all 29
tasks with the primary budget. The next three conditions test whether its result holds with a larger
budget or another model. The repository rerun repeats the repository tasks with the expanded
budget on the same machine. Qwen expanded runs all tasks with the expanded budget and adds the
16-bit reference, which needs the 80 GB machine. Devstral runs all tasks with the second model
and the expanded budget. Because a condition can change more than one of model, budget, and
machine, we compare precisions within each condition and report differences between conditions
descriptively.
Held-out tasks. The held-out tasks extend the comparison to benchmark problems outside our
task set. They include 40 simple questions on 11 databases of BIRD [28], a text-to-SQL benchmark
over real databases judged by its official execution-match rule [4]. For example, one question asks
for the eye color of the superhero whose full name is Karen Beecher-Duncan. They also include the
37 eligible EvalPerf problems [31] unused in the main experiment, such as HumanEval/0, which
asks whether any two numbers in a list are closer than a threshold. We selected both subsets by
rules independent of how any model performed (Appendix E). To test whether the patterns of our
task families carry over to new problems, we made two predictions from the main experiment’s
early results (Section 4.6) before running any held-out task. Because SQL analytics favored 8-bit
and function implementation did not, we predicted that 4-bit would use less energy per completed
task on the function-implementation problems of EvalPerf and that 8-bit would match or beat it on
the SQL questions of BIRD. Section 5.4 tests both.
4.6
Statistical analysis
Statistical support. Repeated attempts show whether a choice is more than noise. Each con-
dition uses the sampled repetitions that are complete at every precision, and partly completed
repetitions are reported separately (Appendix D). For each task, we compute the 4-bit/8-bit ratio of
each of the three measures over these repetitions. A choice is statistically supported when the 95%
bootstrap interval of its ratio lies entirely on the chosen side of one. An interval that includes one
supports neither precision. The interval comes from 5,000 draws that resample each precision’s
valid attempts [9]. When only one precision completes a task, the choice rests on completion counts
and is supported when an exact test gives 𝑝< 0.05 (McNemar’s for attempts paired by seed, Fisher’s
otherwise [11, 35]). A disagreement is supported when both of its choices are supported on the
same attempts.
Noise baseline. Because every task gets its own test, a few tasks could look like supported
disagreements by luck alone, and the noise baseline (i.e., a label-permutation test [16]) measures
how many to expect. After the runs, we randomly swap the 4-bit and 8-bit labels among each task’s
recorded attempts (i.e., in each repetition, a coin flip decides whether the 4-bit and 8-bit attempts

We answer the four research questions from Section 1 in turn, asking whether the two measures
favor the same precision (RQ1), how much choosing per token costs (RQ2), where the extra energy
comes from (RQ3), and what changes the cheaper precision per completed task (RQ4). All ratios
are 4-bit over 8-bit, so values below one favor 4-bit. The three conditions that run all 29 tasks are
the main experiment (Qwen with the primary budget), Qwen expanded, and Devstral. A median
attempt uses 2–7 kJ over 15–31 s, and a held-out attempt uses about 1 kJ over 12–14 s (Table 5).

5.2
RQ2: The cost of choosing by energy per token
Where the two measures disagree, following energy per token is expensive. On the supported
disagreements, the per-token choice (i.e., the precision with the lower energy per token, Section 3.3)
uses a median of 66% more energy per completed task than the per-task choice (i.e., the precision
with the lower energy per completed task, Figure 4). It ranges from 29% more on Qwen’s business
query at one million rows to 3.5 times as much at 8,192 rows. 8-bit spends about the same energy
per completed task at both sizes, so the larger cost at 8,192 rows comes from 4-bit, which took eight
turns per attempt rather than three and generated about four times as many tokens as 8-bit.
Condition averages (Section 4.6) dilute this cost, because tasks that favor each precision offset
each other. The average 4-bit/8-bit ratio per completed task is 1.17 in the main experiment (17%
more for 4-bit) and 0.92 in Qwen expanded and for Devstral (8% less). Such an average suggests a
modest difference either way, while single tasks cost up to 3.5 times as much.
Retest on separate repetitions. To check that the cost holds on new runs, as it would for an
operator who chooses a precision from past runs, we find supported disagreements using one half
of the repetitions and measure their cost on the other half, then swap the halves. For Qwen, the
per-token choice still costs a median of 31% and 45% more per completed task at the two budgets,
less than the 66% above but still large. The per-task choice stays cheaper in 10 of 11 cases. For
Devstral, the per-task choice stays cheaper in 2 of 5.
Finding 2. Choosing by energy per token can be costly. On the tasks of Finding 1, the precision
that is cheaper per token uses a median of 66% more energy per completed task, and up to 3.5
times as much. For Qwen, the extra cost persists at a median of 31–45% on separate repetitions,
and averages over all tasks dilute it (Figure 4).
5.3
RQ3: Where the extra energy comes from
The extra energy comes from extra tokens (Figure 5). Across all disagreeing tasks, supported or
not (Equation (2) holds exactly on each), the precision that is cheaper per token needs a median
of 1.6 to 2.0 times as many tokens per completed task in the three conditions (the token factor of
Equation (2)). A per-token saving of 6–16% covers at most 6–19% extra tokens (Section 3.2), so
these token factors lie far past break-even. Per-token ratios cluster between 0.8 and 1, while the
token factor varies by more than ten times, so tokens per completed task largely decide which
precision is cheaper. Tool and waiting energy (the boundary factor) changes the per-task choice on
at most one disagreeing task per condition, so nearly all disagreements arise inside the model calls.
Longer attempts and failed ones. Under the primary budget, failed attempts (the second term
of Equation (4)) carry the bulk of the extra energy per completed task in most disagreeing tasks (7
of 10), and under the expanded budget, longer attempts carry that extra energy. On SQL analytics
in the main experiment, Qwen submits a wrong answer about three times as often at 4-bit as it
does at 8-bit (20 rather than 6 out of 60 attempts). Even its successful 4-bit attempts generate about
1.8 times as many tokens. In the 18 disagreeing tasks of Qwen expanded and Devstral, the precision
that is cheaper per token generates a median of 1.5 to 1.8 times as many tokens per attempt (the
length factor of Equation (3)), but completes as often (a median completion factor of one). In these
tasks, the extra energy comes mainly from longer attempts.

output, narrated plans (i.e., text describing the next step before each tool call), and repeated tests.

RQ4: What changes the cheaper precision per completed task
Across conditions, 4-bit’s per-token saving persists while the cheaper precision per completed task
varies (Figure 6). The per-token ratio lies below one in every condition (clearly so in all except BIRD
and the repository rerun, which repeats the nine repository repairs with the expanded budget).
However, per completed task, only the EvalPerf condition average clearly favors 4-bit. The tasks
where 4-bit costs more per completed task differ between the two models, and for Qwen, the budget
decides which precision completes more of the hardest repairs.
Task families. Families favor different precisions across conditions (Table 6). SQL analytics
favors 8-bit in the main experiment but favors 4-bit for Devstral, regression control favors 8-bit
in Qwen expanded and for Devstral, and data processing favors 4-bit in Qwen expanded and
for Devstral. In the main experiment, the regression-control average (0.47) hides a supported
disagreement, because 4-bit uses far less energy per completed task at 8,192 rows (0.13, about an
eighth of 8-bit’s) but uses more at one million rows (1.72, or 72% more).

while per completed task the two precisions stay within run-to-run variation. Summed over the

with 8-bit ahead on the hardest ones (Section 5.1). About two thirds of these attempts end at the
turn or context limit (Section 4.3). However, in the repository rerun, 4-bit completes about 70%
more repairs than 8-bit (45 rather than 26 out of 89, 𝑝= 0.006, Appendix D), and Qwen expanded
shows the same gain (27 rather than 16 out of 54). Nearly half of the rerun’s gain comes from one
repair that 4-bit completed in 9 of 10 attempts and 8-bit completed in none, because 8-bit kept
working until it reached the turn or context limit (about 220 turns per attempt on average, against
about 30 for 4-bit). Devstral, run only with the expanded budget, completes 75 out of 90 repair
attempts at each precision. Per completed task, the rerun’s ratio is 0.71 [0.32, 1.24], or about 29%
less energy for 4-bit.
16-bit reference. In Qwen expanded, the condition that adds the 16-bit reference, 16-bit uses
31% more energy per token than 8-bit and 14% more per completed task [0.93, 1.49]. Quantizing
from 16-bit to 8-bit thus saves energy per token, and per completed task it leaves a 14% gap within
run-to-run variation. Energy per token picks a quantized file on every task, and the three tasks
where the per-task choice picks 16-bit are all within run-to-run variation.
Held-out tasks. For Qwen, the patterns of our task families carry over to new benchmark
problems, as both predictions stated in Section 4.5 hold. On EvalPerf, 4-bit uses less energy per
completed task (0.90 [0.85, 0.98], or 10% less) and completes about as often (65 out of 74 attempts at
4-bit and 68 out of 74 at 8-bit). A partly finished repetition on another machine widens the interval
to [0.79, 1.01] (Appendix D). On BIRD, as predicted, neither precision is supported per completed
task (0.91 [0.77, 1.09]).

6
Discussion
6.1
Choosing a precision in practice
A deployment can check which precision is cheaper per completed task from token counts and the
per-token saving. By the break-even rule (Section 3.2), 4-bit stays cheaper only while its per-token
saving covers the extra tokens it needs, and a saving of 6–16% covers at most 6–19% more tokens per
Preprint
18


completed task in model-call energy. We thus suggest that developers track tokens per completed
task to see which precision is cheaper, using the logs of repeated attempts at each precision.
For developers of local agents.
• Short coding functions. A developer whose agent mostly writes and tests short functions
can start with 4-bit. On our function-implementation and held-out EvalPerf tasks, 4-bit
used the same or less energy per completed task on average.
• Structured SQL answers. A developer whose analytics agent answers business questions
with one structured SQL result should compare both precisions on a sample of their own
questions, with several attempts each. In our SQL analytics family, 8-bit used less energy
per completed task in one condition and 4-bit in another (Table 6).
• Long repository sessions. In long sessions with a large context window and many turns,
4-bit completed at least as many repairs as 8-bit in every condition with the expanded
budget (Section 5.4). A developer whose agent works through long sessions on a code base
can thus start with 4-bit and switch to 8-bit where 4-bit’s extra tokens per completed task
exceed what its per-token saving covers on their own tasks.
For benchmark and measurement designers. Five practices make agent energy results
comparable across studies, and per-attempt records let others recompute both measures under
another boundary.
(1) Report energy per completed task beside energy per output token.
(2) Charge failed attempts to the completions.
(3) Repeat attempts, and compare per-task differences with run-to-run variation.
(4) State the energy boundary (e.g., CPU packages and GPU board, or wall power), whether
model loading is included, the budget, and the number of repetitions.
(5) Include the tests the agent runs while working in its task energy, and exclude the separate
check that scores its final result.
6.2
Scope and limitations
Deployment setting. We study one agent that sends one request at a time to an open-weight
model of 24 or 30 billion parameters, resident on a dedicated GPU and served by one engine, with
model loading excluded as for a server that stays loaded (Section 3.1). This setting charges all
energy in the task window to one task and keeps model calls, tools, and host activity separable.
Shared or batched serving would divide idle and host energy among tenants. Our machines cover
two memory sizes of one GPU generation and one processor family.
Agent interface and budgets. Each task family offers a few tool actions in place of a general
shell, and a response with a malformed tool call costs a turn (Section 4.3). This strict protocol
charges each precision for its own malformed output under the same rule, which is one source of
the extra tokens (Section 5.3). A deployment could reduce this cost for both precisions by repairing
malformed calls or by constraining the output format with a llama.cpp grammar, and energy per
completed task would show how much it saves. Both precisions share one budget, as under a single
deployment policy, and a budget set for each precision could give 4-bit the longer context that its
smaller file allows.
Task set and verification. We study a fixed set of verifiable tasks, because an automatic check,
as in SWE-bench and BIRD [22, 28], makes completion directly measurable at every precision.
The held-out benchmarks test whether the patterns of our task families carry over beyond this
set (Section 5.4). A repository patch counts only when it passes the verifier, as in SWE-bench,
Preprint
19


so the energy of partly correct patches is charged to the completions. Open-ended work such as
web interaction, research, or planning would add judged or graded outcomes. Our tasks represent
the short and medium-length work of a local agent, and the same per-task accounting applies
to multi-hour projects and to models with a thinking mode, which raises energy per problem at
nearly unchanged energy per token [34]. On longer and harder tasks, where completion rates fall,
failed attempts weigh more in each completion’s energy (Equation (4)), so the per-task measure
matters more there. The precisions of one model share their base weights, so both saw the same
training data, and privately seeded verifier inputs limit what a model gains from having seen public
benchmark problems during training.
6.3
Reproducibility
The per-attempt records let others recompute every reported energy and completion figure. Each
attempt with a complete measurement record stores its settings, raw energy-counter readings, the
time span of each model and tool call, token counts, the verifier decision, and its validity checks,
and every incomplete or invalid attempt is logged with its reason. File hashes identify the plans,
model files, task assets, and the analysis scripts behind the reported intervals and figures, and
one manifest file records the machine, tool interface, and harness version of each condition. Upon
acceptance, we will release the code, task definitions and verifiers, run settings, and per-attempt
records on Zenodo, and identify by source and version any third-party asset whose license prevents
redistribution.
7
Conclusion
Quantization is making it practical to run agents on a single local GPU, and we asked whether a
precision that is cheaper per token is also cheaper per completed task, a question for quantized
models in general. Measuring both on the same attempts of locally hosted coding and data agents, we
find that 4-bit is cheaper per token almost everywhere, while the cheaper precision per completed
task depends on the task, the model, and the execution budget. We trace the difference to extra
tokens from malformed tool calls, narrated plans, repeated tests, and failed attempts, which can
outweigh the per-token saving. We suggest that developers track tokens per completed task over
repeated attempts at each precision, and that benchmark designers report energy per completed
task beside energy per token. For anyone who hosts an agent, the energy bill arrives per completed
task, not per token.

B
How attempts ended under each budget
Limits end attempts mainly in repository repair (Section 4.3, Table 8). Each model call may generate
at most the smaller of the per-turn limit and the unused part of the total, and an attempt ends
with a context stop, without sending a request, when its prompt plus this allowance would exceed
the context. The deadline counts from the start of the attempt, so it includes 8 to 12 s of model
loading with the primary budget and 8 to 17 s with the expanded budget, at most 2% of either
deadline. Every valid sampled attempt in the main experiment ended before the 600 s deadline, and
the expanded deadline of 1,800 s ended one valid sampled attempt, a 4-bit repository attempt in
Qwen expanded after 97 turns.
C
Energy-record checks, model loading, and verification energy
Every stored energy value is recomputed from its raw counter readings (Section 4.4). A decrease
of a CPU package counter [51] counts as a wrap (i.e., an overflow back to zero) only when it falls
from the top quarter of its range of about 65.5 kJ to the bottom quarter, and any other decrease
invalidates the attempt. The recomputed energy of each period must match the stored value to a
relative tolerance of 10−12.
A 4-bit load takes 0.4 to 0.7 kJ less than an 8-bit one, with median loading energy per sampled
attempt of 1.55 to 2.14 kJ for 4-bit and 2.00 to 2.87 kJ for 8-bit across the conditions. Because loading
costs about as much as a short attempt (0.07 to 1.88 times the median operational energy of an
attempt for 4-bit and 0.05 to 2.40 times for 8-bit), Section 5.1 charges one load to every attempt

D
Sensitivity analyses and energy drift
Table 9 confirms the results of Section 5 under other handling of remote logins, missing energy
records, partial repetitions, pairing, and answer matching.
Missing energy records. All three incomplete energy records support the same conclusions
once rebuilt (Section 4.4). Table 9 rebuilds the two that the deadline cut off during generation from
the readings taken before it, and bounds the 8-bit record lost after a verified completion by the
task’s range.
Partial repetitions. Two conditions have a partial repetition (Section 4.6), the eighth in Qwen
expanded and a held-out repetition on machine A. Held-out repetitions were assigned to machines
before any ran, and the analysis uses machine C, where both sampled repetitions finished.
Drift. Any drift in energy per token across repetitions affects both precisions alike (Section 4.3).
For attempts of the same task and precision with identical per-call token counts in every sampled
repetition (105, 68, and 182 attempts on machines A, D, and B), energy per output token changed
by −0.06%, +0.35%, and −0.02% per repetition. The 4-bit and 8-bit slopes differed by less than 0.1%
per repetition on every machine, with 95% intervals within 0.2% on machines A and B and within
0.75% on machine D. Machine D swings by about 3% between repetitions, but both precisions run
in the same repetition, so the swings cancel in the ratio.
E
Held-out task selection and statistical procedures
Both held-out subsets were fixed by rules independent of how any model performed (Section 4.5).
Function-implementation problems. Of the 112 EvalPerf [31] problems outside the main
set, 37 were eligible and all 37 were used (58 lacked the runtime separation, 15 had an incorrect
reference solution, and two lacked computable canonical outputs). A problem is eligible if its
canonical, fastest, and slowest reference solutions are correct and the slowest takes at least three
times the time limit (the greater of 2 s and three times the canonical solution’s median runtime), so
that a correct but slow solution fails. Every failure had a cause other than the time limit or the 10−6
regression tolerance, so both thresholds leave the reported comparisons unchanged.
