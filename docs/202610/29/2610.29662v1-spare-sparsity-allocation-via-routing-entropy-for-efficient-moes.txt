Abstract
Mixture-of-Experts (MoE) architectures scale
model capacity efficiently, yet their substan-
tial memory footprint poses a major barrier
to deployment. Expert pruning reduces this
footprint, but most methods focus on which ex-
perts to remove and prune every layer equally.
We propose SPARE (Sparsity Allocation via
Routing Entropy), a training-free, selector-
agnostic method that decides how many ex-
perts to remove from each layer using routing
entropy, the entropy of tokens’ normalized Top-
K gate weights on calibration data. When a
token’s gate weight is spread evenly, removing
one active expert discards little of it, so SPARE
prunes layers with more diffuse routing more
aggressively. A perturbation bound under ran-
dom pruning formalizes this intuition, and con-
trolled interventions with a fixed selector link
concentrated routing to larger output changes.
With the same selector and budget, SPARE im-
proves average accuracy over uniform alloca-
tion on Mixtral, Qwen3, and DeepSeek mod-
els, especially at high pruning ratios. At 50%
expert pruning, it raises the eight-task aver-
age of Qwen3-30B-A3B from 57.2% to 63.8%,
and the math, code, and science average of
DeepSeek-R1 from 80.1 to 82.8.

Introduction
The pursuit of stronger reasoning capabilities has
pushed Large Language Models (LLMs) to the
trillion-parameter scale, incurring prohibitive com-
putational costs. The Mixture-of-Experts (MoEs)
architecture addresses this challenge through con-
ditional computation, which activates only a sparse
subset of parameters per input token (Shazeer et al.,
2017; Lepikhin et al., 2020; Fedus et al., 2022; Dai
et al., 2024). While conditional computation drasti-
cally reduces FLOPs, the memory overhead neces-
sitates loading the full set of experts. Furthermore,
the requirement to distribute these experts across
devices complicates the communication topology,
leading to efficiency bottlenecks. This highlights
the urgent need for compression techniques capable
of shrinking pre-trained MoEs.
To address this, recent research has focused on
expert pruning, which removes entire experts to
reduce model size. Existing approaches usually
estimate an expert-level importance score from
activation frequency, routing statistics, or recon-
struction error (Dong et al., 2025; Lasby et al.,
2025). These scores provide useful criteria for
selecting experts within a layer. However, using
expert importance directly for cross-layer pruning
is less reliable. Scores computed in different lay-
ers are not necessarily calibrated, since activation
scales, expert functions, and token distributions
vary across depth. The calibration domain can fur-
ther change which experts appear important. As
a result, globally ranking experts across all layers
may over-prune sensitive layers (Chowdhury et al.,
2024; Lasby et al., 2025).
This limitation comes from conflating two de-
cisions: inter-layer allocation, which determines
how many experts to remove from each layer under
a global budget, and intra-layer selection, which
determines which experts to remove within a layer.
Existing criteria such as REAP and EASY-EP ad-
dress the second; SPARE addresses the first and
can be paired with any of them. Non-uniform al-
location has been explored in dense LLMs (Men
et al., 2025; Yin et al., 2024) and, more recently,
in MoE expert pruning (Yang et al., 2024; Li et al.,
2026), using signals such as calibration loss or
expert-usage frequency. SPARE instead allocates
budgets by how gate weight is distributed among
each token’s active experts, a training-free signal
distinct from usage statistics across inputs.
To capture these routing dynamics, we use rout-
ing entropy: the Shannon entropy of each token’s
normalized Top-K gate weights, averaged over cal-
ibration tokens. As illustrated in Figure 1, high-
entropy layers spread gate weight broadly among

the active experts, so removing one active expert
discards only a small share of each token’s weight,
which is then reassigned to the remaining experts.
Low-entropy layers concentrate weight on fewer
experts, suggesting stronger input-conditioned spe-
cialization and making aggressive pruning riskier.
This signal is layer-level rather than expert-level:
it assumes neither that particular experts are in-
dispensable nor that the same experts are selected
across domains. Although expert identities shift be-
tween web text, code, math, and science, layer-wise
entropy rankings remain stable across calibration
domains, making them a robust basis for allocation.
Leveraging this insight, we introduce SPARE,
a training-free sparsity allocation framework that
assigns non-uniform pruning budgets across MoE
layers. Our main contributions are:
• Entropy-guided, plug-and-play allocation. We
formulate MoE expert pruning as an inter-layer
budget allocation problem and replace uniform
allocation with entropy-aware budgets. SPARE
decides how many experts to prune in each layer,
while existing criteria such as REAP and EASY-
EP decide which ones, without retraining or mod-
ifying the router.
• Theoretical foundation. We derive a perturba-
tion bound that, under uniform random pruning,
grows with the routing entropy gap. The analysis
provides a layer-level sensitivity signal and an
existence argument for deterministic masks.
• Empirical validation. With the same selector
and budget, SPARE improves average accuracy
over uniform allocation on Mixtral, Qwen3, and
DeepSeek models, especially at high pruning ra-
tios. At 50% expert pruning, it raises the eight-
task average of Qwen3-30B-A3B from 57.2% to
63.8%, and the math, code, and science average
of DeepSeek-R1 from 80.1 to 82.8.

Related Work
Routing Heterogeneity.
MoEs scale model ca-
pacity through conditional computation (Shazeer
et al., 2017; Fedus et al., 2022). Despite load-
balancing objectives, expert behavior can remain
non-uniform: Su et al. (2025) identify “super-
experts” that produce extreme activation outliers,
and Lo et al. (2024) examine relationships between
routing weights and expert outputs. These obser-
vations motivate studying routing heterogeneity
across layers.
Expert Pruning and Compression.
Expert prun-
ing reduces the memory footprint of pretrained
MoEs.
Selection criteria use routing statis-
tics (Dong et al., 2025; Koishekenov et al., 2023),
router-weighted activation saliency (Lasby et al.,
2025), or reconstruction loss (Lu et al., 2024);
EEP (Liu et al., 2024) instead uses evolutionary
search. Shapley-MoE (Huang et al., 2025) ranks
experts globally across layers via router-guided
sampling; we exclude it from comparisons under
fixed layer budgets, which would alter its selec-
tion rule. Chowdhury et al. (2024) provide a prun-
ing guarantee for fine-tuned sparse MoEs based on
changes in router distributions. SEER-MoE (Muzio
et al., 2024), MoE-SVD (Li et al., 2025), and
AdaWhiten (Anonymous, 2026) explore further
pruning and decomposition strategies, alongside
task-specific pruning (Chen et al., 2022) and bench-
marks such as MC-Suite (Jaiswal et al., 2025). Ex-
pert merging, skipping, dynamic Top-K routing,
and intra-expert pruning (Xie et al., 2024) oper-
ate at other granularities; SPARE instead targets
budget allocation.
Layer-wise Sparsity Allocation.
In dense LLMs,
ShortGPT (Men et al., 2025) studies block re-
moval, whereas OWL (Yin et al., 2024) allocates
weight sparsity using activation-aware outlier statis-
tics.
SlimGPT (Ling et al., 2024) also adopts
non-uniform pruning schedules. For MoEs, MoE-
I2 (Yang et al., 2024) estimates layer importance
from calibration losses after removing individual
experts, assigns non-uniform pruning ratios, and
selects experts through genetic and block-wise
search; its full pipeline additionally includes low-
rank decomposition and LoRA recovery. C-GNN-
PRUNE (Li et al., 2026) computes the entropy of
expert-selection frequencies pooled over calibra-
tion samples and retains more experts in layers
with higher usage entropy. Its expert-selection pro-
cedure uses learned graph embeddings. SPARE
averages, over tokens, the entropy of each token’s
normalized Top-K gate weights and prunes more
experts in layers with higher gate entropy. Usage
entropy describes how broadly expert identities
are used across inputs; gate entropy describes how
evenly weight is spread among the experts active
for a single token. The rules therefore treat high en-
tropy in opposite ways without contradiction: they
measure different distributions. SPARE contributes
this routing signal and a selector-independent allo-
cation rule.

Theoretical Analysis: Routing-Entropy
Perturbation Bounds
We analyze how routing entropy can guide the allo-
cation of a fixed expert-pruning budget across lay-
ers. Under the stated routing and bounded-output
assumptions, we first bound the output perturba-
tion for any fixed pruning mask using the routing
mass assigned to removed experts. For uniform
random pruning, this bound takes an explicit form
involving the routing entropy gap. We then give
a sufficient condition under which a deterministic
expert selector inherits the same entropy-dependent
bound, with an additive term when the condition
holds approximately. Finally, we describe how lo-
cal pruning perturbations propagate through the
network.

4.3
Implications for Pruning Strategy
For fixed (m, N, K, Bℓ) and selector residual, the
bound increases with Sℓ= log K −¯Hℓ. SPARE
does not minimize the summed bound; it uses Sℓ
as a layer-ranking signal and ignores the layer-
dependent scale Bℓ. The allocation score in Sec-
tion 5 is therefore a heuristic.
Since exact Lipschitz products are intractable
for large MoEs, SPARE uses a cosine-based proxy
as an empirical propagation regularizer; entropy
remains the primary allocation signal.

Experiments
We ask three questions: (RQ1) Does entropy-
guided allocation improve benchmark performance
over uniform allocation at the same pruning bud-
get? (RQ2) Does the gain persist across the eval-
uated expert selectors, and does entropy correlate
with pruning risk when the selector is fixed? (RQ3)
How stable are entropy profiles across calibration
domains, and how does non-uniform allocation af-
fect throughput in the tested implementation?

Models.

Benchmarks.
We assess capabilities across four

(i) Multiple-choice tasks:

et al., 2024); (iii) Science question answering:
GPQA-Diamond (Rein et al., 2023); and (iv) Code:
LiveCodeBench (Jain et al., 2025).
Calibration.
For Mixtral and Qwen3-30B-A3B,
we use a calibration set of 1024 randomly sampled
sequences from C4 to estimate layer-average rout-
ing entropy ( ¯Hℓ). For large-scale reasoning models
such as DeepSeek-R1, we sample 25 instances each
from AIME (math), LiveCodeBench (code), and
GPQA (science).
Baselines and Protocols.
To disentangle the ben-
efits of sparsity allocation from expert selection,
we structure our comparison along two orthogo-
nal dimensions. 1) Intra-layer Expert Ranking:
Determines expert priority within a layer. We em-
ploy: (i) Random; (ii) EASY-EP (Dong et al., 2025);
and (iii) REAP (Lasby et al., 2025). 2) Inter-layer
Sparsity Allocation: Determines the pruning bud-
get mℓper layer. We compare: (i) Uniform; (ii)
OWL (Yin et al., 2024); and (iii) SPARE.
6.2
Main Results
Table 1 shows improvements in macro-average ac-
curacy in all ten evaluated combinations of model,
pruning ratio, and intra-layer selector. The largest
gain occurs on Qwen3-30B-A3B at 50% pruning:
with EASY-EP fixed, SPARE raises mean accuracy
from 57.2% to 63.8%, a gain of 6.6 percentage
points. At the same pruning ratio, gains with Ran-
dom and REAP are 0.9 and 2.3 points, respectively,
showing that the benefit depends on the selector.
Improvements at 25% pruning are smaller, ranging
from 0.1 to 0.7 points.
6.3
Controlled Analysis of Pruning Risk
Entropy groups with a fixed selector.
On
Mixtral-8×7B, we estimate routing entropy us-
ing 128 C4 samples and divide layers into high-
, middle-, and low-entropy groups. Within each
group, we use the same selector, REAP, to prune
four of the eight experts per layer while retaining
the expert with the highest REAP score. Table 3
reports normalized output perturbation on held-out
samples. The low-entropy group has higher per-
turbation than the high-entropy group in all three
domains, although the middle group does not fol-
low a uniformly monotonic ordering. The point
estimates indicate an association under this selec-
tion procedure.
Single-layer pruning and output-distribution
changes.
We next test whether this association
holds when each layer is pruned in isolation. For
each MoE layer, we remove its lowest-saliency
experts under REAP, with saliency estimated on
C4, while keeping all other layers intact; the prun-
ing ratios in Table 4 apply only to the intervened
layer. We measure the resulting output-distribution
change on C4, Math, Code, and MMLU with KL
divergence truncated to the unpruned model’s top-
64 tokens, and summarize each layer by its mean
over the four domains and by its worst (maxi-
mum) domain. We define routing concentration
as Cℓ= 1 −¯Hℓ/ log K = Sℓ/ log K, so a larger
Cℓcorresponds to lower routing entropy. In both
models, at both pruning ratios, and under both ag-
gregations, the correlation between Cℓand pruning-
induced KL is positive, ranging from 0.26 to 0.45.
These correlations are moderate: routing concen-
tration accounts for only part of the layer-to-layer
variation in pruning risk. We therefore use it to
rank layers for budget allocation, not to predict the
perturbation of an individual layer.

Conclusion and Future Work
We introduced SPARE, a training-free method that
uses token-level routing entropy to allocate expert-
pruning budgets across MoE language-model lay-
ers and can be paired with existing intra-layer se-
lectors. A perturbation analysis and controlled in-
terventions with a fixed selector associate concen-
trated routing with greater pruning risk. Under the
same expert selector and global budget, SPARE
improves average performance in the evaluated set-
tings, with larger gains at high pruning ratios: at
50% expert pruning, it raises the Qwen3-30B-A3B
eight-task average from 57.2% to 63.8%, and the
DeepSeek-R1 three-benchmark average from 80.1
to 82.8, close to the unpruned 83.4. Future work
includes studying calibration shift and combining
entropy-guided allocation with quantization and
expert merging.
8


Limitations
SPARE yields its largest gains at aggressive prun-
ing ratios; at moderate ratios such as 25%, its im-
provements over uniform allocation are small. We
study SPARE only for expert pruning; combining
it with quantization or expert merging is left for
future work.
