Is High Local Accuracy Enough? Geometric
Consensus with Support-Adaptive Statistical
Personalization for Federated Learning
Junyu Lin
, Bosong Qi
, Pengcheng Shi
, and Jiayuan Li
Abstract—Real-world deployment of personalized federated learning (PFL) requires adapting to heterogeneous clients while preserving
recognition performance beyond local distributions. However, high local accuracy can conceal failures on locally rare or absent classes.
Effective personalization should therefore strengthen global recognition while raising the personalization floor. We propose an evaluation
protocol that complements local accuracy with global, rare-class, and unseen-class evaluations to reveal generalization obscured by
local scores, and assesses personalization floors among the worst-performing clients to expose weaknesses hidden by aggregate
performance. To meet these deployment challenges, we propose FedGRC, a unified PFL framework integrating generalization and
personalization through (i) Consensus-Guided Representation Learning (CGRL), which learns discriminative representations through
shared directional and magnitude guidance; (ii) Multilayer Metric Synthesis (MMS), which constructs a shared covariance metric from
these multilayer representations for statistical classification; and (iii) Support-Adaptive Shrinkage Personalization (SASP), which adapts
class means within this metric by shrinking local estimates toward shared means according to local sample support, enabling adaptation
while retaining shared knowledge. Extensive experiments, including benchmarking against 21 methods across 5 heterogeneity settings
on 3 datasets and comprehensive ablation studies, validate the effectiveness of FedGRC. On CIFAR-10 and CIFAR-100, FedGRC
surpasses state-of-the-art (SOTA) baselines in global recognition and personalization floors by at least 2.17% and 2.13%, respectively.
On the real-world heterogeneous FedRS dataset, it outperforms the respective best baselines by 2.15%, 3.30%, and 4.61% in local
adaptation, global recognition, and the personalization floor, demonstrating its practical deployment value.
Index Terms—Personalized federated learning; Statistical heterogeneity; Real-world generalization; Representation learning.

INTRODUCTION
P
ERSONALIZED federated learning (PFL) adapts client
models to local data using knowledge shared across
the federation, without centralizing raw training data [1],
[2], [3]. This combination of knowledge sharing and local
adaptation is relevant to healthcare [4], autonomous driv-
ing [5], and remote sensing, where data distributions and
acquisition conditions vary across clients. Remote sensing
provides a representative example: differences in sensors
and geographic coverage produce heterogeneous image
appearances and skewed local label distributions, leaving
some classes well represented at a client and others rare
or absent [6], [7]. This poses a core deployment challenge
for heterogeneous PFL: combining all-class recognition with
effective local adaptation.
Several PFL methods, however, prioritize local person-
alization without adequately preserving generalization be-
yond the local distribution [8]. DBE [9] and GPFL [10]
identify a loss of general feature information when repre-
sentations are trained to serve personalized classifiers, while
other studies examine the tension between personalization
and generalization under changing test distributions [11],
[12]. Motley [13] provides a concrete example: additional
local fine-tuning can improve accuracy on local data while
reducing accuracy on data sampled from other clients. These

•
Comprehensive PFL Evaluation beyond Local Ac-
curacy. We introduce a protocol that jointly assesses
local adaptation, generalization across the full label
space, and recognition of locally rare and absent
classes. It also measures a recognition floor by taking
each client’s lowest accuracy across local adaptation
and rare- and unseen-class recognition, then sum-
marizing the lowest-performing clients. This reveals
weaknesses hidden by aggregate scores.

RELATED WORK
2.1
Balancing Personalization and Generalization in
Federated Learning
PFL methods combine shared knowledge with local adapta-
tion through parameter regularization, representation shar-
ing, or predictor combination [3], [6]. Ditto [23] regularizes
personalized models toward a shared model to improve
robustness and fairness, but requires additional model up-
dates for personalization. FedRep [1] separates shared repre-
sentations from local heads; however, these heads still guide
representation learning, allowing local label imbalance to
influence the shared features. FedROD [2] combines a class-
balanced generic classifier with a personalized residual clas-
sifier on a shared feature extractor. This additive combina-
tion supports both generic recognition and local adaptation,
but does not adjust the classifiers’ relative contributions as
the test distribution changes.
Test-time weighting and predictor selection offer alter-
natives to fixed predictor combinations. FedTHE [11] opti-
mizes a mixture weight using entropy and feature alignment
informed by test history. BTFL [12] instead infers weights
from current and historical observations through Bayesian
modeling, reducing iterative adaptation while relying on
approximate feature likelihoods. HPFL [8] uses markers to
select among shared personalized plug-ins, expanding the
available predictor pool at the cost of storage and matching
operations. These mechanisms improve deployment flexi-
bility, but their decisions depend on cues extracted from
test observations. When data sources or class distributions
change, confidence and feature similarity may not reliably
identify the most suitable predictor or combination. Fed-
GRC completes client adaptation before deployment and
therefore requires neither test-time predictor selection nor
mixture-weight estimation.
Training or selecting personalized predictors does not
by itself establish whether each deployed model retains
recognition across all federation-known classes. Motley [13]


3
shows that local fine-tuning can improve local accuracy
while degrading performance on other clients’ data. Other
studies evaluate personalized models on both local and
global test sets [19], [24], [25], [26], while broader bench-
marks and fairness studies examine generalization and dis-
parities across clients [18], [27], [28], [29]. Aggregate perfor-
mance can nevertheless conceal poor recognition of locally
rare or absent classes, as well as weaknesses concentrated
in particular clients. Our evaluation protocol assesses the
same deployed client models on both local and all-class test
distributions, explicitly evaluating locally rare and unseen
classes. Our protocol further measures each client’s weakest
assessed ability and summarizes the lowest of these client-
level scores, so that stronger abilities or performance at other
clients do not mask these weaknesses.
2.2
Learning and Preserving Shared Representations
under Data Heterogeneity
Shared training signals differ in both the information they
convey and the aspects of learning they constrain. At the
logit level, FedLC [30] uses class frequencies to calibrate
predictions under label imbalance, but this correction does
not by itself ensure that local updates preserve knowl-
edge of locally absent classes. Methods targeting locally
absent classes address this retention problem directly [31],
[32]. FedVLS combines global-model distillation with logit
suppression against minority-to-majority errors; distillation
uses only teacher predictions on locally available inputs.
At the representation level, methods differ in the gran-
ularity of their shared references and the feature properties
they constrain. DBE [9] corrects client-specific bias through
local bias memory and global mean regularization, but a sin-
gle client-wide mean cannot fully characterize class-specific
differences across clients. GPFL [10] combines angular guid-
ance and Euclidean alignment to class references through
additional feature transformations and auxiliary objectives
along a personalized training path. FedProto [33] exchanges
class prototypes for feature regularization, while FedFA [20]
uses Euclidean anchors for feature alignment and classifier
calibration. Class-reference methods align feature centers
or directions across clients [34], [35], [36]. Feature mag-
nitudes have also been shaped through learned transfor-
mations [37], class-average norm regularization [38], and
layerwise squared activation-norm penalties [39]. These rep-
resentation constraints do not by themselves determine how
a classifier should use within-class variation and feature
correlations. FedGRC uses final-layer directional guidance
and shared magnitude references for each class and selected
layer. Its multilayer statistical classifier uses common layer
scales that preserve within-layer magnitude differences.
Fixed classifiers provide another way to establish shared
feature geometry. FedETF [21] uses an equiangular tight-
frame (ETF) classifier to impose common class geome-
try and prevent classifier drift. Within this line of work,
FedDr+ [40] identifies missing-class forgetting under ordi-
nary dot-regression and adds global feature distillation to
improve retention, at the cost of computing teacher features.
Separately, FedYoYo [41] combines self-distillation across
weakly and strongly augmented views with distribution-
aware logit adjustment to strengthen minority-class learning
and generalization. These advances improve shared repre-
sentations, but preserving their recognition ability during
local adaptation remains a separate design problem. In our
personalization experiments, local fine-tuning of FedETF
improves local accuracy but substantially reduces all-class
and locally unseen-class recognition (Section 5.3). FedGRC
links representation learning to personalization through sta-
tistical classification based on shared multilayer features.
For FedGRC, the remaining design choices concern which
classifier statistics to keep shared and how strongly each
local class estimate should influence personalization.
2.3
Federated Statistical Classifier Construction and
Local Adaptation
Statistical classifiers construct predictors from distributed
feature summaries and have been used in one-shot and
communication-efficient FL. Their designs differ in how
they construct decision rules, represent within-class varia-
tion, and estimate feature correlations. CCVR [22] calibrates
a classifier using sampled virtual features and server-side
optimization, while leaving the underlying representation
fixed. FedNCM [42] provides a simple mean-based readout,
but does not account for class spread or feature correlations
when comparing samples with class centers. Fed3R [43]
uses second-order feature statistics to construct a closed-
form ridge classifier whose solution is independent of how
fixed features are partitioned across clients. Exchanging
second-order matrices increases communication cost rela-
tive to mean-only summaries and adds matrix-processing
overhead. FedCGS [44] constructs a Gaussian classifier
from a full covariance centered at the global feature mean,
without synthetic sampling or iterative head training. GH-
LDA [45] instead estimates shared within-class covariance
by pooling residuals around class means. Covariance es-
timation introduces a further distinction: FedCOF [46] re-
duces communication by estimating covariance from client
means, but class-conditional shifts can affect the estimate
and the resulting decision boundaries. These methods differ
in both the decision rule derived from shared statistics and
the variation represented by those statistics. In continual
learning, LayUP [47] constructs a Gram-matrix-based ridge
readout, whereas FedGRC separates shared within-class
covariance estimation from local class-mean adaptation. A
related design choice is how to scale multilayer features.
For OOD detection, MM++ [48] normalizes each sample’s
feature vector at each layer to unit length before estimat-
ing a shared within-class covariance from the concatenated
features. FedGRC instead applies a common scale to all
samples at each layer, preserving within-layer magnitude
differences for its statistical classifier.
Beyond constructing a shared classifier, statistical per-
sonalization must determine which quantities to adapt and
how local sample support should influence that adaptation.
FedCGS [44] additionally supports personalization through
prototype-regularized local fine-tuning. FedLog [15] per-
sonalizes local feature extractors and learns a shared head
from sufficient statistics through iterative server-side maxi-
mum a posteriori (MAP) optimization. Its adaptation there-
fore operates on local representations, whereas FedGRC
keeps the representation shared and adapts class means.


4
pFedFDA [14] guides representations with a global gen-
erative classifier and interpolates local and shared means
and covariances. Its interpolation coefficients are selected
per client using local validation, rather than explicitly de-
termined for each class from its sample count. Its local class
priors favor locally frequent classes, whereas FedGRC uses
equal class priors to avoid adding this frequency preference
to the prediction rule. pFedGM [16] combines inter-class and
intra-class representation objectives with count-informed
distribution fusion, followed by local optimization of class
means, scales, and biases. This flexibility requires estimating
several quantities from potentially sparse class observations
and can increase the risk of local overfitting. TOFA and
SPEAR also use class counts to regulate mean shrinkage.
TOFA [49] uses local and global class counts to shrink local
means toward global estimates while estimating a client-
specific covariance. SPEAR [50] pools local means shrunk
toward other clients’ averages into a shared classifier.
FedGRC combines a shared multilayer covariance met-
ric with client-specific class means adapted according to
local class counts, using a reference strength fixed across
clients and classes. By keeping the representation and co-
variance metric shared, FedGRC limits personalization to
class means and avoids estimating a separate local metric.
FedGRC retains shared means for locally absent classes,
keeps estimates for sparsely observed classes close to the
shared means, and permits stronger adaptation for well-
observed classes. Local counts govern mean adaptation;
equal priors support all-class recognition without favoring
locally frequent classes.

METHOD
We present FedGRC, a unified PFL framework that in-
tegrates geometric representation consensus with statis-
tical personalization for local adaptation and full-label-
space recognition. The framework comprises three succes-
sive stages: (i) Consensus-Guided Representation Learn-
ing (CGRL) regularizes feature directions and magnitudes
through shared class references; (ii) Multilayer Metric Syn-
thesis (MMS) constructs a shared statistical geometry from
the frozen multilayer representations; and (iii) Support-
Adaptive Shrinkage Personalization (SASP) adapts class
means according to local sample support within this geome-
try. Together, these stages aim to retain recognition of locally
rare or absent classes during personalization and strengthen
recognition floors across clients. Fig. 2 provides an overview
of the framework; the complete procedure is summarized in
Algorithm 1 and detailed in the following subsections.

5
EXPERIMENTS
We evaluate local adaptation, generalization across the
full label space, and client recognition floors through four
complementary analyses. After describing the experimental
setup and evaluation protocol, we (i) benchmark FedGRC
against external baselines in Section 5.2; (ii) compare recog-
nition before and after personalization to examine the effects
of local adaptation in Section 5.3; (iii) assess the contri-
butions of the framework’s components and their internal
designs through ablations in Section 5.4; and (iv) examine
backbone–classifier synergy and gains over strong baseline
combinations in Section 5.5.
5.1
Experimental Setup and Evaluation Protocol
5.1.1
Baselines and Datasets
We benchmark 21 external methods, comprising 15 com-
plete FL methods and 6 statistical classifiers evaluated on 2
backbone types. The resulting 27 baseline configurations fall
into three categories:

•

•
FedRS [7]. We use the NIID-1 partition, comprising
15 unified classes and 135 clients from 8 remote-
sensing datasets. This setting combines label and
sample-size imbalance with sensor- and region-
dependent feature shifts. Dynamic participation is
simulated by sampling 10 of the 135 clients per round
to represent clients joining and leaving training.
All experiments use 3 random seeds. Results are re-
ported as mean ± sample standard deviation, with means
alone shown in the ablation, personalization, and cross-
comparison tables. Each metric is computed within each
run and then averaged across seeds. The comparison tables
group the three baseline categories into separate blocks: (+)
denotes an attached classifier, (†) and (∗) mark the best and
second-best results, and (∆) gives FedGRC’s gain over the
best baseline for each metric. Accuracies are reported in (%),
and gains are absolute differences in percentage points.

Tables 1–3 compare local adaptation, generalization across

Local Adaptation and Class Coverage. As heterogene-
ity increases from Dir(0.3) to Dir(0.1), pFedFDA [14], Fed-
Log [15], and pFedGM [16] achieve higher local but lower
global accuracy on both CIFAR datasets (Fig. 1). The same
pattern occurs for FedRep [1], FedROD [2], FedTHE [11],
BTFL [12], and HPFL [8]. For example, the local accuracy
of FedLog [15] on CIFAR-10 rises from 76.80% to 88.90%,
while its global accuracy falls from 29.32% to 19.50%. As
labels become more concentrated, frequent classes receive
more weight in local evaluation, so higher local accuracy can
coexist with weaker recognition across the full label space.
Indeed, under Dir(0.1), pFedFDA [14], FedLog [15], and
pFedGM [16] attain 87.37%–88.90% local accuracy on CIFAR-
10, yet their F@25 scores round to 0.00%. Higher local scores
therefore conceal severe weaknesses in individual clients’
least reliable recognition abilities.
Benefits of Statistical Classifiers. The gains from sta-
tistical classification depend on the underlying represen-

raise FedYoYo’s [41] global accuracy from 34.59% to 48.51%–
51.56%, whereas the same classifiers on FedAvg [56] yield
33.05%–35.47%, compared with 34.80% for its original head.
This contrast underscores the importance of designing the
representation and statistical classifier together. FedGRC
further exceeds the strongest baseline combinations in global
accuracy and F@25 across all five settings.
Comprehensive Performance on FedRS. On FedRS (Ta-
ble 3), FedGRC reaches 89.11% local and 87.08% global accu-
racy, exceeding the respective best baselines by 2.15% and
3.30%. It also improves unseen and rare accuracy by 3.46%
and 1.44%, respectively. These gains span local adaptation
and all-class generalization, including recognition of classes
with little or no local training support. This combination
is especially relevant to remote-sensing clients with limited
class coverage and sensor and geographic heterogeneity.

Dir(0.1), FedGRC trails the best local baseline by 15.64% on
CIFAR-10 and 3.72% on CIFAR-100, despite leading in global,

89.45% local accuracy, compared with FedGRC’s 73.81%.
BTFL’s global accuracy and F@25 are 29.64% and 0.84%,

5.3
Recognition Before and After Personalization
Table 4 compares predictors immediately before and after
terminal personalization for 3 methods with explicit local
adaptation stages. FedETF [21] adapts both the representa-
tion and classifier, whereas pFedGM [16] freezes its shared
generator and adapts the statistical classifier. FedGRC keeps
the shared CGRL backbone, MMS layer scales, and covari-
ance metric fixed and replaces shared class means with
SASP means, isolating the effect of its final adaptation stage.
Both reference methods improve local accuracy while
substantially reducing generalization and client recognition
floors. On CIFAR-100 Dir(0.3), their local gains of 7.12%–
13.40% accompany losses of 6.99%–13.19% in global accuracy
and 17.54%–22.69% in F@25. The same pattern occurs on
FedRS: FedETF [21] gains 15.75% locally but loses 25.25%
in global accuracy and 31.78% in F@25. These changes show
that fitting the local distribution can substantially weaken
recognition beyond it, even when adaptation is confined to
the classifier. They motivate controlling the influence of local
evidence during personalization.
SASP improves local adaptation and recognition floors
while largely retaining the shared classifier’s all-class accu-
racy. On CIFAR-100 Dir(0.3), it raises local accuracy from
55.53% to 55.61% and F@25 from 46.93% to 47.56%, with a
0.27% decrease in global accuracy. On FedRS, all five metrics
improve, including gains of 1.41% in local accuracy and
1.61% in F@25. These results support class-mean adaptation
within a shared representation and metric as a way to pre-
serve broad recognition during personalization. Next, ab-
lations isolate the contributions of representation learning,
statistical readout, and support-adaptive mean estimation.

5.4.2
Ablation of CGRL: Directional Alignment and Magni-
tude Regulation for Statistical Classification
Table 6 separates the effects of directional and radial guid-
ance, with cross-entropy training and the MMS+SASP read-
out retained in every configuration. Within each dataset
block, the rows correspond to (i) neither guidance com-
ponent; (ii) radial guidance only; (iii) directional guidance
only; and (iv) both components. Directional guidance com-
bines supervised contrastive learning with shared class
directions, while radial guidance regulates feature magni-
tudes. Used separately, directional guidance produces larger
global accuracy gains than radial guidance on both datasets.
On CIFAR-100 Dir(0.3), radial guidance alone improves
global accuracy by 0.90% while leaving F@25 nearly un-
changed. Adding radial guidance improves all five metrics
over directional guidance alone, including rare accuracy
from 57.13% to 58.00% and F@25 from 46.44% to 47.56%.
These gains support complementary magnitude regulation
and directional discrimination.
On FedRS, adding radial guidance to directional guid-
ance further improves global accuracy by 0.38% and F@25 by
2.23%, while reducing local and rare accuracy by 0.12% and
0.17%. The magnitude penalty regularizes within-class log-
norm dispersion and the mean log norm’s deviation from
the shared reference (Lemma 4.1). It therefore complements
directional guidance by regulating the magnitude structure
retained in the class means and covariance used for statisti-
cal classification.
Using the same full MMS+SASP readout, CGRL im-
proves global accuracy and F@25 over cross-entropy train-
ing by 6.15% and 7.37% on CIFAR-100 Dir(0.3), and by
4.68% and 5.12% on FedRS. These gains under the same
MMS+SASP configuration demonstrate the contribution of
geometric consensus to the deployed statistical classifier.

either single-component replacement in all five settings.

component depend on its pairing with the other.

ment exceeds the sum of the gains from the two individual
replacements. It is positive for F@25 in all five settings,
ranging from 0.86% to 2.61%, and for global accuracy in four
settings. Together, these results support jointly designing
geometric representation learning and statistical personal-
ization to strengthen client recognition floors.
6
CONCLUSION
We revisited personalized federated learning from the per-
spective of global recognition and personalization floors,
showing that high local accuracy alone can conceal weak-
nesses across classes and clients. Our evaluation protocol
exposes these weaknesses through complementary assess-
ments of local adaptation, recognition across federation-
known classes, and the weakest assessed abilities of poorly
performing clients. Guided by this perspective, FedGRC
couples geometric representation consensus with multilayer
statistical classification and support-adaptive class-mean
shrinkage. Extensive comparisons and ablations demon-
strate improved global recognition and personalization
floors across heterogeneous settings, with simultaneous
gains in local adaptation on FedRS. These findings support
the joint design of representations and personalized classi-
fiers for practical deployment.
Under extreme label heterogeneity, broader recognition
gains accompany substantially lower local accuracy than the


17
strongest local baselines, highlighting the remaining trade-
off between generalization and local adaptation. FedGRC re-
tains a shared representation and covariance metric during
personalization; its shrinkage weights are determined by lo-
cal class sample counts and do not explicitly account for the
magnitude of client-specific domain shifts. We will explore
personalization jointly guided by class support and distri-
bution shift, alongside representation and metric learning
robust to domain shift, to improve local adaptation while
preserving broad recognition and personalization floors.

B.3
Communication and Computational Overhead
B.3.1
Communication Cost
As in FedAvg [14], the server broadcasts the current global
model parameters to participating clients, which upload
their updated parameters after local training. FedGRC adds
O(CdL + C|Λ|) scalar entries in each direction per par-
ticipating client per round: clients upload class directions,
log-norm sums, and counts, while the server broadcasts
updated directional and magnitude references alongside the
model parameters.
After training, all K clients participate once in the
additive-moment construction of Appendix A.5. Each up-
loads class counts, class-wise first moments, and a sin-
gle d × d second-moment matrix summed over all local
samples, totaling O(Cd + d2) entries. From the uploaded
moments, the server derives the shared layer scales, class
means, and covariance metric. It broadcasts these quantities
in O(d2 + Cd + |Λ|) entries per client, after which each
client constructs its personalized head locally. For fixed
feature dimensions and class count, this terminal payload
is independent of local sample size. Thus, MMS and SASP
together require only one additional communication round
to construct the personalized statistical heads. In our ex-
periments, FedGRC’s total communication volume is 0.08%,
0.41%, and 0.25% higher than that of FedAvg on CIFAR-10,
CIFAR-100, and FedRS, respectively.
B.3.2
Computational Cost
CGRL processes two augmented views per example, adding
one view’s forward and backward computation over single-
view FedAvg for the same examples and updates. The
feature-level regularizers cost O(B2dL + BCdL + Bd)
per update for contrastive, class-direction, and magnitude
terms, respectively. After local training in each participating
round, one additional forward-only pass over the local
training set extracts class directions; magnitude statistics are
accumulated during training. Server reference updates cost
O(|St|C(dL + |Λ|)) per round.
To construct the personalized statistical heads with MMS
and SASP, each client makes one pass over its training
set using the frozen backbone; moment accumulation adds
O(N (k)d2) operations. Aggregation, covariance construc-
tion, and construction of all personalized heads together
cost O(d3 + KCd2) across the server and clients with dense
arithmetic. The server inverts the shared covariance once;
each client constructs its head in O(Cd2) time.
At inference, multilayer features come from a single
backbone pass, followed by layer scaling and an O(Cd)
linear head, compared with FedAvg’s O(CdL) head. The co-
variance metric is incorporated into the precomputed head
parameters. Thus, FedGRC incurs negligible computational
overhead at inference relative to FedAvg.
