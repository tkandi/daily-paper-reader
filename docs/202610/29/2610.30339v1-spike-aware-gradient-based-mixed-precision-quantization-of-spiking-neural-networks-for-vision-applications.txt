Abstract
In recent years, Spiking Neural Networks (SNNs) have
gained massive attention as an energy-efficient neural com-
puting paradigm due to their event-driven and sparse com-
puting capability. However, maintaining accuracy under
low-precision constraints, necessary for practical deploy-
ment, has been largely unexplored for SNN models. In ad-
dition, existing Hessian-based approaches for quantization,
such as HAWQ-V2, incur significant memory and compu-
tational overhead due to temporal unrolling across multi-
ple SNN timesteps. We propose a spike-aware gradient-
based mixed-precision quantization framework for SNNs
that adaptively assigns bit-widths using first-order gradient
information, avoiding costly second-order Hessian compu-
tations. We evaluate our method across various SNN-based
architectures, including VGG, ResNet, and Vision Trans-
former models obtained from both ANN-to-SNN conver-
sion and direct training frameworks, on CIFAR10/100, Ima-
geNet, and DVS-CIFAR-10 datasets. Notably, on ResNet-34
evaluated on ImageNet, our method achieves 73.30% ac-
curacy at 4.78× compression, which is comparable to that
provided by HAWQ-V2, while reducing memory consump-
tion by 2.31× and bit-allocation runtime by 100×. We also
present an energy analysis for mixed-precision SNNs show-
ing that the proposed quantization strategy enables energy-
efficient deployment without compromising performance.
1. Introduction
Spiking Neural Networks or SNNs [10], which are moti-
vated by the computational efficiency of the human brain,
have emerged as an energy-efficient alternative to state-of-
the-art Artificial Neural Networks (ANNs). Unlike ANNs,
which perform dense multiply-accumulate (or MAC) opera-
tions on continuous-valued activations, SNNs process infor-
mation using discrete and binary spikes that a neuron emits
only when its membrane potential crosses a firing thresh-
old. Since computations are triggered by spikes, this event-
driven behavior makes neural activity sparser in both space
and time, allowing the MAC operations in ANNs to be re-
placed by much cheaper accumulate (AC) operations. Neu-
romorphic hardware architectures [1, 5, 14, 23] are designed
to take advantage of this sparse, event-driven, and spike-
based computation, enabling SNNs to realize their energy-
efficiency benefits during deployment.
Recent advances in SNN training techniques [4, 22, 24,
25] have enabled them to achieve inference-time accuracy
competitive with their ANN counterparts on a range of vi-
sion datasets, making them increasingly viable candidates
for real-world deployment. However, realizing the practical
benefits of SNNs still requires reducing their memory foot-
prints and computational budgets. Methods such as quan-
tization are therefore necessary to realize efficient deploy-
ment of SNNs on resource-constrained edge and neuromor-
phic hardware.
Quantization is an effective technique to reduce the
model size and computational cost by using fewer bits
to represent parameters and activations [12, 13].
Exist-
ing SNN quantization methods primarily use uniform bit-
widths across layers [27, 28]. However, it is now well es-
tablished that the layers in a neural network differ substan-
tially in their sensitivity to quantization noise [7]. This mo-
tivates mixed-precision quantization, where bit-widths are
assigned according to individual layer sensitivity.

spike-driven activity statistics. Our key contributions are as
follows.
1. A spike-aware gradient-based mixed-precision quan-

2. An efficient first-order sensitivity estimation strategy.
The proposed method eliminates the expensive second-

2. Related Work and Background
Spiking Neural Networks.
SNNs process information
over discrete timesteps using spiking neurons that integrate
weighted inputs and produce spikes when the membrane
potential exceeds a threshold. They are primarily trained
using either ANN-SNN conversion [4, 24] or direct train-
ing [22, 25]. ANN-SNN conversion first trains an ANN us-
ing standard backpropagation and then replaces activation
functions with spiking neurons, where ANN activations are
approximated using spike rates accumulated over multiple
timesteps. Methods such as QCFS [4] and PASCAL [24]
reduce conversion error by introducing quantization-aware
activations during ANN training. On the other hand, direct
training optimizes SNNs directly in the spiking domain us-
ing surrogate gradients [22] and Backpropagation Through
Time (BPTT) [29], which unrolls the network over all sim-
ulation timesteps. Recent methods such as Temporal Effi-
cient Training (TET) [25] further improve optimization by
applying supervision at every timestep. Additional details
of both training methods are provided in Sec. 1 of the sup-
plementary material.
Quantization of SNNs.
Recent works on SNN quanti-
zation have made notable progress in reducing both the
memory and computational costs. Q-SNN [27] binarizes
weights during training using a weight-spike dual regulation
objective, while QP-SNN [28] combines uniform fixed-bit
post-training quantization with structured pruning. A re-
cent fixed heterogeneous-precision strategy [18] employs a
W1/W2 configuration, assigning W1-bit weights to the first
layer and W2-bit weights to the remaining layers. While
this exploits the different data representations across layers,
the precision assignment is fixed by layer position rather
than quantization sensitivity.
Mixed-precision quantization instead allocates higher
precision to sensitive layers and lower precision to robust
ones. Prior works apply the HAWQ framework [7, 8, 19]
to SNNs using Hessian-based sensitivity estimation. How-
ever, they require expensive second-order computations and
manual bit allocation, while not explicitly accounting for
spike-driven temporal dynamics. In contrast, our method
uses a spike-aware first-order sensitivity metric to automat-
ically determine the layer-wise precision, avoiding Hes-
sian computation while incorporating spiking activity into
the bit-allocation process. Hardware-oriented approaches
such as QUANTISENC [21] support configurable layer-
wise precision for efficient neuromorphic hardware, but pri-
marily focus on hardware realization rather than automatic
sensitivity-driven bit allocation.

4. Experiments and Results
We evaluate the proposed framework on VGG, ResNet,
and ViT-based SNNs using ANN-to-SNN conversion
(QCFS [4], PASCAL [24]) and direct training (TET [25])
across CIFAR-10/100 [15], ImageNet [6], and CIFAR10-
DVS [17]. For ViT conversions, we use SpikeZip-TF[31]
with a ViT-Small backbone. All results reported in this sec-
tion, unless otherwise noted, use post-training quantization
directly on SNNs. For all experiments we used a calibration
size of 20, β = 0.7 and Seed as 42.

4.2. Computation and Efficiency
Figure 3 illustrates that the proposed method is significantly
more memory- and time-efficient than HAWQ-V2 during
bit allocation. The higher cost of HAWQ-V2 arises from
repeated Hutchinson trace estimation for Hessian approxi-
mation, which is further amplified by temporal unrolling in
SNNs. By relying on a single first-order backward pass to
estimate layer sensitivities, the proposed method eliminates
this overhead, resulting in substantially lower memory con-
sumption and runtime. Specifically, on ResNet-34 trained
on the ImageNet dataset, the proposed method reduces peak
GPU memory from 26.1 GB to 11.3 GB (i.e. 56.9% reduc-
tion) and decreases bit-allocation time from 22 min 23.5 s
to 13.38 s, achieving an approximately 100× speedup over
HAWQ-V2.

S1. SNN Training Paradigms
SNNs process information through discrete spiking events
over multiple timsteps. At each timestep t = 1 . . . , T, a
spiking neuron integrates incoming weighted spikes into its
membrane potential. When the membrane potential exceeds
a predefined firing threshold Vth, the neuron emits a binary
spike and updates its internal state. The event-driven com-
putation leads to sparse neural activity and enables energy-
efficient inference on neuromorphic hardware.
Since SNNs produce outputs over multiple timesteps, the
final prediction is typically obtained by aggregating the out-
put spike across the entire simulation window. A commonly
used decoding strategy is rate encoding, where the predicted
class is computed as

ANN-to-SNN Conversion using QCFS [4].
QCFS is an
ANN-to-SNN conversion framework designed to achieve
accurate and low-latency spiking inference. Instead of using
the ReLU activation during ANN training, it uses the Quan-
tization Clip Floor-Shift (QCFS) activation function. This
activation constrains the network output to discrete levels
that closely resemble the spike rates produced by SNNs,
thereby reducing the mismatch between ANN activations
and SNN firing behavior. During conversion, the trained
ANN is transformed into an equivalent SNN by replacing
the quantized activations with spiking neurons while pre-
serving the learned thresholds and scaling factors. As a re-
sult, the converted SNN achieves high accuracy using fewer
simulation timesteps, making QCFS an efficient ANN-to-
SNN conversion framework.
Transformer-based SNN.
To demonstrate the generality
of the proposed mixed-precision quantization framework,
we evaluate our method on spike-based vision Transformer
(ViT) models using the SpikeZIP-TF [31] ANN-to-SNN
conversion framework. Unlike convolutional networks, Vi-
sion Transformers rely on self-attention, softmax, and layer
normalization operations, which are not directly compatible
with spike-based computation.
SpikeZIP-TF addresses this issue by first quantizing a
pretrained Vision Transformer through quantization-aware
training. During conversion, ANN activation is replaced by
a spiking neuron called ST-BIF+. SpikeZIP-TF proposes
Spike-Equivalent Self-Attention (SESA), Spike-Softmax
and Spike-LayerNorm, which preserve the functionality of
the corresponding Transformer modules in the spiking do-
main. These operators enable the converted SNN to closely
mimic the behavior of the quantized Vision Transformer
models, while maintaining high classification accuracy with
relatively fewer simulation timesteps.
S2. Theoretical Justification of the First-Order
Sensitivity Metric
Hessian-based methods estimate parameter sensitivity us-
ing the curvature of the loss landscape. For a model with
parameters w, the Hessian matrix is defined as
  H 
= \nabla ^2_w L(w), 
where L(w) denotes the training loss. The Hessian captures
the local curvature. Computing Hessian is expensive, par-
ticularly for temporally unrolled spiking neural networks.
We instead utilize first-order gradient statistics derived from
the Fisher information matrix [2, 20], which measures the
sensitivity of the model output distribution with respect to
parameter perturbations.

  p(y| x;w), 
where w are the parameters of this model, x are the input

  L( w ) = -\log p(y|x;w). 
To measure how sensitive the output distribution is to pa-

  s( w ) = \nabla _w \log (y|x;w)

  F = \mathbb {E}[s(w)s(w)^T].

S6. Ablation Studies on Surrogate Gradients
We further investigate whether the choice of surrogate gra-
dient affects the proposed mixed-precision bit allocation
strategy. Since surrogate gradients determine the backward-
pass approximation of the non-differentiable spiking func-
tion, different surrogate functions can potentially alter the
estimated layer-wise sensitivity and, consequently, the re-
sulting bit-width assignment. To assess the robustness of
our approach to this choice, we evaluate five commonly
used surrogate gradients—Atan, Sigmoid, FastSigmoid,
4


Piecewise Linear, and Triangular—on a ResNet-18 [9] ob-
tained through the QCFS conversion framework on the
CIFAR-10 dataset. For each surrogate gradient, we inde-
pendently compute the layer-wise sensitivity scores used
by our bit allocation method and derive the corresponding
mixed-precision configuration under the same overall bit-
budget. This allows us to isolate the effect of the surro-
gate gradient while keeping the network architecture, con-
version framework, dataset, and allocation procedure fixed.
We then compare the resulting bit-width assignments and
compression performance across the five surrogate-gradient
choices to determine whether the proposed allocation strat-
egy remains consistent under different backward-pass ap-
proximations.
