# Resource-aware Sub-network Allocation for Heterogeneous Decentralized Learning 

Zhuojun Tian, Ming Xiao and Mikael Skoglund, Fellow, IEEE 

Abstract —System heterogeneity in computation, communica-tion, and memory can substantially limit synchronous decen-tralized learning, where each training round is constrained by resource-limited nodes. Existing approaches provide limited sup-port for jointly determining node-specific model capacities from deployment resources. We propose RASA-DL, a resource-aware sub-network allocation framework that treats the maximum sub-network width assigned to each node as a deployment variable. RASA-DL combines offline hardware profiling of computation and memory costs with deployment-aware profiling of learning utility under heterogeneous decentralized training. Using these profiles together with node- and link-level resource information, we formulate deployment as a bottleneck-latency minimization problem subject to per-node memory constraints and a required aggregate learning utility. Exploiting the monotone feasibility of the latency threshold, RASA-DL efficiently identifies the deploy-ment through a binary-search-based allocation procedure. Ex-periments under controlled resource heterogeneity demonstrate favorable accuracy–latency tradeoffs. Under the Moderate and Severe regimes, the minimum-latency operating point improves accuracy over the largest globally feasible uniform deployment by 2.80 and 2.43 percentage points, respectively, at the same modeled round latency. The advantage is further amplified under strong Non-IID data heterogeneity. 

Index Terms —Decentralized learning, heterogeneous edge sys-tems, resource allocation, sub-network allocation, system hetero-geneity. 

I. I NTRODUCTION 

Decentralized learning enables networked devices to collab-oratively train machine learning models through peer-to-peer communication without relying on a centralized parameter server [1]. By distributing both local computation and model exchange across participating nodes, it provides a flexible learning paradigm for edge and distributed networks while avoiding the communication bottleneck and single point of failure associated with centralized aggregation. In practical deployments, however, participating devices are rarely ho-mogeneous. Their computation capabilities, communication bandwidths, and available memory can differ substantially, resulting in highly diverse per-round training costs. Such sys-tem heterogeneity is particularly problematic for synchronous decentralized learning, where the progress of each communi-cation round is constrained by slow or resource-limited partici-pants. Consequently, deploying an identical model architecture across all devices can lead to severe synchronization delays, inefficient utilization of resource-rich devices, or even memory infeasibility on constrained nodes.

Existing decentralized learning algorithms primarily focus on enabling efficient peer-to-peer optimization and model exchange [2]. For example, DFedAvgM allows each node to perform multiple local updates and subsequently aggregate models received from neighboring nodes without centralized coordination [3]. Communication-efficient approaches further reduce network overhead through techniques such as sparse model exchange and adaptive peer communication [4]. While these methods improve the efficiency and scalability of de-centralized training, decentralization itself does not eliminate the impact of heterogeneous device resources. When all nodes are required to train models of comparable complexity, differ-ences in computation speed, communication conditions, and memory capacity can still translate directly into system-level synchronization bottlenecks. System heterogeneity has been extensively investigated in federated learning. Representative approaches accommodate heterogeneous devices by allowing variable amounts of local computation or by selectively involving efficient participants [5, 6]. Other studies reduce computation and communication overhead through adaptive model pruning or jointly optimize pruning and resource-management decisions [7, 8]. More recently, model-heterogeneous federated learning has emerged as an alternative approach in which clients directly train models with different capacities. HeteroFL distributes sub-networks according to client capabilities [9], while FjORD constructs nested sub-networks through ordered dropout [10]. FedRolex introduces rolling submodel extraction to improve parameter coverage across heterogeneous clients [11], and ScaleFL jointly adapts model width and depth for resource-constrained devices [12]. These studies demonstrate the po-tential of adapting model capacity to heterogeneous device capabilities. Model heterogeneity has also begun to receive attention in serverless decentralized learning. The authors in [13] study communication-efficient personalized distributed learning un-der both data and node heterogeneity, with the goal of reducing communication overhead while accommodating heterogeneous participants. DisPFL employs personalized sparse masks and decentralized sparse training to reduce communication and computation costs [14]. DeSA facilitates knowledge transfer among clients with heterogeneous local models through syn-thetic anchors [15]. CompFL supports cooperative training of multiple predefined model architectures in both centralized and device-to-device settings [16], while DeMoFL further investigates peer-to-peer knowledge federation among model-heterogeneous mobile devices [17]. The works in [1, 18] propose to transmit semantic information instead of model arXiv:2610.22015v1 [cs.DC] 15 Oct 2026 2

parameters to enable heterogeneous models among agents. These methods mainly focus on enabling efficient training, personalization, aggregation, or knowledge transfer under het-erogeneous decentralized settings. The question of how het-erogeneous model capacities should be configured according to the deployment resources of individual nodes, however, remains comparatively less explored in fully decentralized learning. Resource allocation provides another important approach to deal with system heterogeneity. In wireless federated learning, joint computation and communication resource management has been investigated by optimizing variables such as band-width, processing workload, and device computation speed [19]. Other studies jointly consider model pruning, device selection, and wireless resource allocation to balance learn-ing performance and training latency [8]. Resource-aware designs have also been considered in decentralized settings. For example, grouped federated learning organizes devices with similar computation and communication capabilities to reduce training latency [20], while GossipFL adapts sparse peer-to-peer communication to improve bandwidth utiliza-tion [4]. Nevertheless, these approaches primarily optimize communication, computation, scheduling, or topology-related decisions. They do not directly address the deployment-stage problem of jointly assigning node-specific model capacities in a decentralized system while simultaneously accounting for computation, communication, memory feasibility, and network-wide learning capability. Motivated by this gap, we consider sub-network allocation, where the maximum model capacity deployed at each node is determined before decentralized training. The key challenge is to avoid resource bottlenecks on constrained nodes while still utilizing the capacity of resource-rich nodes and maintaining sufficient learning capability. To address this problem, we pro-pose Resource-aware Sub-network Allocation for Decentral-ized Learning (RASA-DL), a profiling-guided resource-aware sub-network allocation framework for heterogeneous decen-tralized learning. RASA-DL combines offline characterization of candidate sub-networks with current node- and link-level resource conditions to determine heterogeneous node-specific model capacities. The resulting allocation problem is formu-lated to minimize the synchronous bottleneck latency while satisfying memory and aggregate learning-utility requirements, and is solved efficiently by exploiting its monotone feasibility structure. The main contributions of this work are summarized as follows.  

> •

We propose RASA-DL, a profiling-guided resource-aware sub-network allocation framework for hetero-geneous decentralized learning. RASA-DL treats sub-network size as a deployment variable and jointly consid-ers heterogeneous computation, communication, memory, and learning requirements during configuring.  

> •

We develop a deployment-aware profiling methodology that characterizes candidate sub-networks from both sys-tem and learning perspectives. Offline hardware profiling captures the computation latency and memory consump-tion of candidate sub-networks, while reference decentral-ized environments profile their learning capability under the nested sub-network training and aggregation.  

> •

We formulate heterogeneous sub-network allocation as a synchronous bottleneck-latency minimization problem subject to memory and aggregate learning-utility con-straints. By exploiting the monotone feasibility structure with respect to the latency threshold, we develop an effi-cient binary-search-based allocation algorithm that avoids exhaustive enumeration of heterogeneous sub-network assignments.  

> •

Extensive experiments are conducted under controlled system and statistical heterogeneity to evaluate the pro-posed framework from both learning and system perspec-tives. The results demonstrate that RASA-DL provides favorable accuracy–latency tradeoffs. Additional experi-ments validate the benefit of deployment-aware capability profiling and show that the heterogeneous deployments remain effective under different datasets and increasingly Non-IID data distributions. The rest of this article is organized as follows. Section II introduces the decentralized learning system model and for-mulates the resource-aware sub-network allocation problem. Section III presents the offline profiling framework. Section IV develops the proposed resource-aware allocation algorithm and describes the subsequent heterogeneous decentralized learning procedure. Section V presents the experimental setup and eval-uates RASA-DL under different levels of system and statistical heterogeneity. Finally, Section VI gives the conclusion. II. C. Resource-Aware sub-network Allocation 

System heterogeneity can lead to severe synchronization inefficiency in decentralized learning. Assigning an identi-cal model size to all nodes may cause resource-constrained devices to become persistent stragglers, while resource-rich nodes remain idle waiting for neighboring updates. We there-fore determine the node-specific retention ratios before de-centralized training to balance the synchronous round latency against the learning capability of the allocated sub-networks. III. O FFLINE PROFILING FRAMEWORK 

The resource-aware allocation in Section II-C relies on three types of prior information: a common family of nested compact sub-networks, the empirical learning utility associated with each candidate sub-network, and the resource profiles re-quired to estimate its execution cost on heterogeneous devices. Rather than obtaining this information during decentralized training, we construct it through an offline profiling procedure, which is detailed below. 

A. Overall Offline Profiling Procedure 

The offline procedure consists of three stages. First, a canon-ical parent model Mp is established as the common structural and parameter reference. A shared channel-importance ranking is then learned from the parent model and used to construct the nested compact-sub-network family {M (r) : r ∈ R} .Second, the learning capability of each candidate width is evaluated in a lightweight reference decentralized environment to obtain the deployment-aware utility profile QU. Third, the executable sub-networks are benchmarked on reference hardware to obtain their training-latency and memory profiles. These operations are performed before decentralized train-ing and are independent of the subsequent node-specific al-location. In particular, the channel ranking, compact architec-tures, and learning-utility profile remain fixed after the offline stage. At deployment time, the offline information is combined with the current computation and communication conditions to evaluate the candidate configurations and solve the resource-aware allocation problem in (11). This offline-online separation is important for two reasons. First, architecture construction and capability evaluation do not need to be repeated whenever the system conditions change. Second, the allocation stage does not require trial execution of decentralized training under every possible combination of node-specific sub-networks, whose number grows combinato-rially with the number of participating nodes. 

B. Nested Compact-sub-network Construction 

The construction starts from a canonical parent network 

Mp. Rather than independently pruning the parent network for each retention ratio, we learn auxiliary channel scores using labeled training data while keeping the parent parameters and BN statistics fixed. Channels are ranked within each residual stage according to the aggregated L2 norms of the learned score filters. All candidate subnetworks retain prefixes of the same stage-wise orderings, ensuring nested parameter alignment across model sizes and facilitating overlap-aware decentralized aggregation. For each structured prunable layer l ∈ L s with Cl out-put channels, let πl = [ πl(1) , π l(2) , . . . , π l(Cl)] denote the learned channel ordering from the most to the least important channel. Given a candidate retention ratio r ∈ R , the retained output-channel set is obtained through prefix selection: 

{πl(1) , . . . , π l (⌈rC l⌉)} . (12) The corresponding executable compact network associated with r can be denoted by M(r) and induces the nested model 5

family assumed in Section II. The retained parameters are inherited from their corresponding locations in Mp. Unlike element-wise masking, this construction produces an exe-cutable network with reduced tensor dimensions. Therefore, the resulting architecture can provide actual reductions in com-putation, memory consumption, and communication payload rather than merely reducing the number of active weights. The common ranking also provides a consistent parameter correspondence across different sub-networks. Specifically, if ra < rb, every parameter represented in M(ra) has a corresponding parameter in M(rb). This property is essential for heterogeneous decentralized learning because neighboring nodes with different retention ratios can identify and aggregate their overlapping parameters without introducing an additional architecture-matching procedure. The common channel or-dering is learned only once and remains fixed after the of-fline construction stage. Consequently, no channel-ranking or structural-pruning operation is performed during decentralized training. The resulting nested family M(r) constitutes the structural component of the offline sub-network library. 

C. Deployment-Aware Learning-Capability Profiling 

The retention ratio determines not only the resource cost of a compact sub-network, but also the learning performance that it can sustain during decentralized training. A straight-forward approach is to evaluate each candidate sub-network independently and use its standalone validation accuracy as an architecture-level utility. However, as shown in Section V-C, such an estimate may not accurately reflect deployment-time behavior and degrade the training performance. This discrepancy arises because, in the decentralized system, a compact sub-network is trained jointly with heterogeneous neighboring models and is affected by nested-width sampling and overlap-aware parameter aggregation. To obtain a utility estimation that better reflects the intended deployment regime, we construct a lightweight reference decentralized environment , denoted by Eref . The reference environment contains a small set of decentralized nodes, denoted by Vref , and includes representative maximum-width configurations from the candidate set R. It uses the same nested sub-network construction, local training rule, width-sampling policy, normalization semantics, and overlap-aware decentralized aggregation as the target system, while requiring substantially less computation than repeatedly evaluating full-scale deployments. A. Experimental Settings 

We first construct a nested family of sub-networks from a common canonical parent model. The parent model is ini-tialized from an ImageNet-pretrained ResNet-18 and adapted to CIFAR-10 through full-model fine-tuning. A fixed struc-tured channel ranking is then obtained from the parent model and shared by all candidate sub-networks. Based on this ranking, four nested configurations with retention ra-tios R = {1.0, 0.75 , 0.5, 0.25 } are constructed, correspond-ing to stage widths of [64 , 128 , 256 , 512] , [48 , 96 , 192 , 384] ,

[32 , 64 , 128 , 256] , and [16 , 32 , 64 , 128] , respectively. Their pa-rameter counts are approximately 11 .18 , 6.29 , 2.80 , and 0.70 

million. All sub-networks inherit the corresponding initializa-tion parameters from the same canonical parent and preserve a nested parameter structure. To estimate the learning capability of each candidate width, we use the deployment-aware profiling procedure described in Section III-C. The reference environment consists of eight decentralized nodes connected by a fixed ring topology. The maximum widths {0.25 , 0.5, 0.75 , 1.0} are assigned twice each across the eight virtual nodes. Profiling uses an IID parti-tion of CIFAR-10, with 45,000 samples used for decentralized training and 5,000 held out for validation. Each profiling run lasts 30 communication rounds with 100 local optimization steps per node and a batch size of 64 . Nested-width sampling uses ρ = 0 .7, together with the same overlap-aware aggre-gation rule employed in the main decentralized training. The round-30 validation accuracies are averaged across nodes with the same maximum width and across three training seeds. This yields the frozen deployment-aware utilities, which are used only as profiling-based capability estimates for deployment optimization and should not be interpreted as the final test accuracies of the system-level experiments. Unless otherwise stated, the CIFAR-10 training data are partitioned IID across the 20 nodes with equal sample sizes. The execution characteristics of each candidate sub-network are profiled on an NVIDIA GH200 GPU, which serves as the reference hardware platform. A complete training step includes forward propagation, backward propagation, and the optimizer update. With a batch size of 64 , the measured step latencies for retention ratios 1.0, 0.75 , 0.5, and 0.25 are 

15 .42 , 14 .31 , 12 .83 , and 11 .42 ms, respectively, averaged over three profiling runs after warm-up. The corresponding training-memory requirements are approximately 1.629 , 1.222 , 0.845 ,and 0.471 GB. These measurements provide the empirical execution profiles used as the basis of the subsequent system-level evaluation. Since a physical testbed containing a sufficiently diverse set of heterogeneous devices and network links is not available, we evaluate RASA-DL in a controlled simulated decentralized environment. Specifically, we consider 20 nodes connected by a fixed undirected graph with 40 communication edges and an average node degree of 4. Node-level computation costs are derived from the measured reference-hardware profiles and ad-justed according to simulated computation capabilities, while memory budgets and link-level bandwidths and propagation delays are independently configured. Communication costs are then evaluated using the analytical model in Section II. Con-sequently, the reported system metric is a modeled bottleneck round latency , rather than measured end-to-end wall-clock latency on a physical heterogeneous testbed. To systematically study the effect of system heterogene-ity, we construct three resource regimes, denoted as Mild ,

Moderate , and Severe . The same topology and mean resource levels are maintained across the three regimes, while only the dispersion of node and link resources is varied. B. Decentralized Learning Performance 

We next evaluate the decentralized learning performance of RASA-DL under the three controlled resource regimes. All methods use the same decentralized topology, data partition, communication-round budget, and evaluation protocol. Unless otherwise stated, training is conducted for 50 communication rounds, with 100 local optimization steps per node in each round, a batch size of 64 , and SGD with a learning rate of 

0.001 , momentum of 0.9, and weight decay of 5 × 10 −4.Each configuration is evaluated using three random seeds, and the weighted mean test accuracy across all nodes is reported. System efficiency is characterized by the modeled bottleneck round latency determined by the compute and communication costs of the corresponding deployment. We define the modeled bottleneck round latency as the maximum, over all nodes, of the sum of local-training compute latency and parallel-neighbor communication latency. 

a) Baselines and RASA-DL operating points: We con-sider both system-level baselines and an existing decentral-ized learning method. Fixed Uniform assigns the same fixed-width sub-network to all nodes and trains only that width, whereas Nested Uniform uses a common maximum width but adopts the same nested-width sampling mechanism as RASA-DL during local training. Both methods aggregate through averaging. We further include DFedAvgM [3] as a literature baseline for decentralized federated learning. Its method-specific momentum persistence and neighbor-mixing rules are preserved, while the deployed model width is chosen as the largest width that is feasible on every node. Accordingly, the maximum feasible uniform width is 0.5 in the Mild regime and 0.25 in the Moderate and Severe regimes. For RASA-DL, we select three representative operating points from the deployment frontier under different utility targets. RASA-DL-A denotes the minimum-latency endpoint, RASA-DL-B corresponds to the common intermediate pro-filing target U tar = 0.89 , and RASA-DL-C denotes the maximum-profiled-utility endpoint. The allocation of RASA-DL-C coincides with the Memory-Max assignment, which independently selects the largest memory-feasible sub-network at each node. Hence, RASA-DL-C also serves as the resource-only maximum-capacity reference without requiring a dupli-cate training run. For RASA-DL and Nested Uniform, the nested-width sampling parameter is fixed to ρ = 0 .7.

b) Accuracy–latency tradeoff: Fig. 2 presents the pri-mary system-level comparison by jointly considering final learning performance and bottleneck latency. For each re-source regime, we report the maximum-feasible Fixed Uni-form and Nested Uniform deployments, DFedAvgM, and the three representative RASA-DL operating points. Each point denotes the three-seed mean weighted node accuracy, with vertical error bars indicating one sample standard deviation. The baseline methods are shown as isolated operating points, whereas RASA-DL-A, RASA-DL-B, and RASA-DL-C are connected to visualize the accuracy–latency frontier enabled by deployment-aware heterogeneous allocation. The results exhibit a clear change in behavior across the three resource regimes. Under Mild heterogeneity, every node can accommodate the 0.5-width sub-network. Consequently, the maximum-feasible uniform deployments and DFedAvgM can employ a relatively expressive common model and re-main competitive with the RASA-DL frontier. In this regime, RASA-DL mainly provides a broader set of deployment choices spanning lower latency and higher learning capability, rather than uniformly dominating the strongest common-model deployment. The situation changes under Moderate and Severe hetero-geneity. Although the mean primitive resource levels remain unchanged, increased resource dispersion reduces the largest globally feasible uniform width to 0.25 . Uniform-model meth-ods must therefore restrict every node to the smallest can-didate sub-network, even though many individual nodes can accommodate larger models. In both regimes, RASA-DL-A achieves higher accuracy than both the maximum-feasible uniform deployment and DFedAvgM at the same modeled bottleneck latency. Moving from RASA-DL-A to RASA-DL-B and RASA-DL-C further improves learning performance at the cost of additional latency, yielding multiple non-dominated operating points rather than a single fixed deployment. The corresponding numerical results are summarized in Table I. At the minimum-latency operating point, RASA-DL-A improves the latency-matched maximum-feasible uni-form deployment by 2.8017 and 2.4265 percentage points in the Moderate and Severe regimes, respectively. Compared with DFedAvgM, RASA-DL-A achieves gains of 3.0505 and 

2.6753 percentage points, respectively, at the same modeled bottleneck round latency. In Mild, by contrast, the maximum-feasible uniform width remains 0.5, and both Fixed Uniform and DFedAvgM achieve accuracies above 92% , confirming that a sufficiently expressive common deployment remains competitive. 

c) Convergence behavior: To further examine whether the final performance differences arise from stable decen-tralized training, Fig. 3 compares the learning trajectories under the three resource regimes. To keep the comparison focused, we show the maximum-feasible Fixed Uniform de-ployment, DFedAvgM, and the two endpoint configurations of the RASA-DL frontier, RASA-DL-A and RASA-DL-C. The curves report the three-seed mean weighted node accuracy at each communication round. Across all three regimes, the evaluated configurations ex-hibit stable learning behavior over the 50 communication rounds. RASA-DL-A and RASA-DL-C converge consistently to their respective final operating points, with RASA-DL-C attaining higher final accuracy due to its higher-capacity de-ployment. The trajectories also show that the final differences in Fig. 2 emerge progressively during decentralized training rather than from transient fluctuations at the final round. 

d) Discussion: Taken together, the results indicate that the benefit of heterogeneous deployment is governed less by the scalar heterogeneity index H itself than by the capacity gap induced by heterogeneous resource constraints. In particular, RASA-DL becomes most useful when the largest model that is feasible for all nodes is substantially smaller than the models supported by many individual nodes. E. Robustness to Statistical Heterogeneity 

We further evaluate whether the heterogeneous deployments identified by RASA-DL remain effective when the client data are statistically heterogeneous. To isolate the effect of data heterogeneity from system heterogeneity, we fix the Moderate resource realization, network topology, deployment allocations, utility profile, training hyperparameters, and com-munication protocol throughout this study. Only the client data partition is changed. In particular, the CIFAR-10 training set is partitioned across the 20 nodes using Dirichlet label skew with concentration parameters α ∈ {1.0, 0.5, 0.2, 0.1}, where a smaller α corresponds to stronger statistical heterogeneity. For each α, a single partition realization is fixed across all methods and training seeds. Importantly, the RASA-DL utility profile and the resulting A/B/C deployments are kept unchanged from the original deployment design. Table III summarizes the final weighted mean node accuracy together with the frozen modeled bottleneck round latency. All methods experience performance degradation as the client data become more heterogeneous, particularly for α = 0 .2

and α = 0.1. Nevertheless, the heterogeneous RASA-DL deployments consistently outperform both Fixed Uniform and DFedAvgM across all evaluated Dirichlet settings. A direct cost-matched comparison is provided by RASA-DL-A and Fixed Uniform-25, which have the same modeled bottleneck round latency of 4.2358 s under the frozen Moderate system realization. RASA-DL-A improves accuracy by 4.79 , 4.42 ,

12 .48 , and 18 .68 percentage points for α = 1 .0, 0.5, 0.2, and 

0.1, respectively. The improvement is positive for every train-ing seed in all four settings, and becomes more pronounced as statistical heterogeneity increases. These results indicate that the RASA-DL deployments re-main effective under substantial statistical heterogeneity with-out modifying the utility profile or reallocating model capacity. In particular, when strong label skew causes the globally feasible minimum-width deployment to suffer a large accuracy loss, retaining larger sub-networks on resource-capable nodes provides a more favorable accuracy–latency tradeoff. VI. C ONCLUSION 

This article studied resource-aware model-capacity config-uration for synchronous decentralized learning over hetero-geneous devices. We proposed RASA-DL, a profiling-guided sub-network allocation framework that jointly considers com-putation, communication, memory, and learning capability when assigning node-specific sub-networks. By formulating deployment as a bottleneck-latency minimization problem sub-ject to memory and aggregate-utility constraints, and exploit-ing the monotone feasibility of the latency threshold, RASA-DL enables efficient allocation without exhaustive search. Experiments under controlled resource heterogeneity demonstrated favorable accuracy–latency tradeoffs compared with uniform deployments and DFedAvgM. The results further validated the benefit of deployment-aware capability profiling and showed that the resulting heterogeneous deployments remain effective under Non-IID data distributions. Future work will consider time-varying resource conditions and online adaptation of model capacity, as well as joint optimization of sub-network allocation and decentralized communication strategies.
