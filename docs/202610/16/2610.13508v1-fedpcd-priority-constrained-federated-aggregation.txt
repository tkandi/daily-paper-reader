Abstract 

Federated learning (FL) seeks to optimize a shared global model from decentralized client data. While effective for average performance, this process can produce unfair performance across clients, particularly under statistically heterogeneous data distributions. Existing fairness-aware and multi-objective FL methods address this through client re-weighting, gradient correction, or common-descent formulations, but often change the global optimiza-tion direction or treat client objectives symmetrically. We propose FedPCD, a priority-constrained federated optimization framework that separates the global optimization ob-jective from client protection. FedPCD preserves the conventional federated aggregate as the primary objective, while allowing only the minimum correction necessary to meet client performance constraints. Across heterogeneous FL benchmarks, FedPCD consistently out-performs state-of-the-art baselines, achieving the highest global accuracy across all config-urations and leading worst-10% client accuracy in nearly all. FedPCD further preserves competitive trade-offs between global accuracy and client performance variability. Beyond this trade-off, FedPCD demonstrates faster empirical convergence, reaching matching or superior performance in substantially fewer communication rounds. 

1 Introduction 

Federated learning (FL) enables multiple clients to collaboratively train a shared model while keeping their data decentralized (McMahan et al., 2017; Kairouz et al., 2021). Standard federated averaging (FedAvg) con-structs a global update by aggregating the locally trained model updates of participating clients (McMahan et al., 2017). This aggregation provides a simple and effective way to optimize a shared objective. How-ever, client data distributions are commonly heterogeneous (Li et al., 2020b; Karimireddy et al., 2020), and when local data distributions differ, the resulting direction can make substantial progress for some clients while providing little, none or even adverse progress for others (Wang et al., 2021; Hu et al., 2022; Pan et al., 2023). This motivates performance fairness in FL, in which the goal is to improve the uniformity of client-level performance while maintaining the utility of the global model (Li et al., 2019; Shi et al., 2024) A substantial body of work has consequently modified the optimization objective to improve client-level fairness Shi et al. (2024). Among the earliest works, Agnostic FL (AFL) formulates a minimax objective over mixtures of client distributions and optimizes it by adaptively updating the mixture weights according to their losses (Mohri et al., 2019). Similarly, q-Fair FL (q-FFL) modifies the objective to place greater emphasis on clients with higher losses (Li et al., 2019). Other approaches formulate federated learning as a multi-objective optimization problem, treating client losses as separate objectives and seeking an update direction that provides simultaneous progress across clients (Hu et al., 2022; Pan et al., 2023; 2024). Gradient-conflict-based methods similarly modify the aggregated direction when client updates are incompatible (Wang et al., 2021). Despite their different formulations, these methods share the broader goal of modifying the global optimization process to account for client-level objectives rather than aggregate performance alone. 1arXiv:2610.13508v1 [cs.LG] 9 Oct 2026 Multi-objective approaches are of particular relevance to our approach because they treat client losses as separate objectives and seek a common descent direction that improves the objectives of all participating clients. These methods typically seek convergence to a Pareto-stationary solution, at which no direction exists that can simultaneously decrease all client objectives to first order (Hu et al., 2022). While this provides a principled way to account for client-level progress, the client objectives are treated symmetrically when constructing the global update. In standard FL, however, the FedAvg direction already represents the update preferred by the shared global objective. Replacing this direction may be unnecessary when it already provides sufficient progress for the individual client objectives. This idea motivates FedPCD, a federated adaptation of Priority-Constrained Descent (PCD) (Varam & Alhajri, 2026). PCD is a hierarchical multi-objective optimization framework that distinguishes a primary objective from secondary objectives and seeks the smallest deviation from the primary descent direction required to guarantee progress on secondary objectives. In FedPCD, the FedAvg update assumes the primary role, while individual client update directions define secondary constraints. Therefore, the server seeks a direction that remains as close as possible to the FedAvg update while satisfying the prescribed client-level requirements. If the FedAvg direction already satisfies these requirements, FedPCD leaves it unchanged. Otherwise, it introduces the minimum correction necessary to satisfy the violated constraints. Consequently, improved uniformity of client performance arises from the client-level protection, rather than optimizing an explicit fairness surrogate. The contributions of this paper are as follows: • We introduce FedPCD, a priority-constrained formulation that treats the conventional federated aggregate as the primary objective and individual client updates as secondary constraints, allowing only for the minimum deviation required to satisfy these constraints. • We extend the formulation to partial participation through a client-history mechanism that retains recently observed client directions to increase representation. • We evaluate FedPCD across several FL settings, including 2 levels of participation, 3 datasets, and 3 data heterogeneity levels. FedPCD consistently achieves the highest global accuracy, superior worst-10% client accuracy, and competitive client-performance spread compared to state-of-the-art fairness-aware FL methods, indicating that preserving client-level progress naturally translates into strong tail-client and fairness performance. • We demonstrate how FedPCD substantially improves empirical communication efficiency, reaching FedAvg’s final accuracy using only 10%–44% of the total communication rounds and outperforming the strongest baseline in convergence speed in 16 of 18 configurations. • We establish an O(1 /T ) convergence rate for the round-averaged squared ℓ2-norm of the global gra-dient under deterministic full participation, and show convergence to simultaneous client stationarity under the stated geometric conditions. 

2 Related Work 

Existing approaches to client fairness broadly fall into 3 paradigms: reweighting client objectives, resolving gradient conflicts, or formulating fair aggregation as a multi-objective optimization problem (Fig. 1). We examine these approaches below and contrast them with the asymmetric formulation of FedPCD. 

2.1 Fair Federated Learning 

Performance fairness in FL seeks to reduce disparities in the performance of a shared global model across clients Shi et al. (2024). A major line of work addresses this problem by changing the relative influence of client losses during optimization to shift the training away from average performance. These works range from worst-case optimization over mixtures of client distributions (Mohri et al., 2019) to emphasizing disadvantaged clients in the weighted objective (Li et al., 2019; 2020a; Huang et al., 2022). More recent methods move beyond a single client-level weight by also modifying parameter contributions. Another line of work connects performance disparities to conflicts among client updates. FedFV identifies conflicting gradients as a source of unfairness and modifies client directions through iterative projection before aggregation (Wang et al., 2021). More recently, CRAFT formulates conflict resolution as a constrained correction problem, seeking an update close to a reference direction while enforcing positive alignment with the participating clients (Wang et al., 2026). Furthermore, partial participation complicates fairness-aware learning because the updates available in a communication round represent only a subset of the population. Therefore, historical information has been used to account for absent clients in FedFV, FedMDFG, FedLF, and FedMC+ (Wang et al., 2021; Pan et al., 2023; 2024; Shen et al., 2025). These approaches motivate the use of historical client information to improve representation under partial participation while exposing a trade-off between broader client coverage and gradient staleness (Pan et al., 2024). 

2.2 Multi-Objective Federated Optimization 

Beyond reweighting and conflict-aware aggregation, several methods approach fair aggregation as amulti-objective optimization (MOO) problem. Building on the Multiple Gradient Descent Algorithm (MGDA) (Désidéri, 2012), FedMGDA+ seeks Pareto-stationary solutions by constructing a common de-scent direction across all client gradients (Hu et al., 2022). Subsequent works refine this common-descent paradigm: AdaFed scales updates by client loss magnitudes to prioritize lagging participants (Hamidi & Yang, 2023), FedMDFG and FedLF incorporate explicit fairness regularizers, with FedLF operating layer-wise (Pan et al., 2023; 2024), and DQN-Fed extends the formulation to quasi-Newton directions (Hamidi & Ye, 2025). While these methods treat client losses symmetrically to find a shared compromise, FedMC+ (Shen et al., 2025) uniquely separates the average objective from local conflict mitigation. FedPCD shares this distinction between aggregate and local objectives, but departs fundamentally in execution. Rather than solving a joint multi-objective problem, FedPCD treats client objectives as hard minimum-progress secondary constraints, projecting the standard federated update only by the minimal margin needed to satisfy them. 33 The Federated PCD Framework

3.3 Partial Participation and Client History 

Under partial participation, Equation 10 contains constraints only for clients sampled in the current round. This provides only a narrow representation of the client population, especially when the participation rate is small and data heterogeneity across clients is severe. Therefore, FedPCD incorporates a history window 

H ≥ 0 of communication rounds that stores the pseudo-gradient of each client that participated within this window, representing additional constraints to the QP in Equation 10. Each client contributes only their most recent update, replacing their previously stored direction if they participate again within the history window. If a client has not participated within the H rounds, its cached pseudo-gradient is removed. This way, the current participants contribute their latest directions, while recently absent clients continue to influence the federated aggregate through their cached updates for a specific history window H. Importantly, client history is used only to construct the secondary constraints. The primary direction ¯ gt remains the current federated aggregate and is computed exclusively from clients participating in round t. 5 Experiments 

5.1 Experimental Setup 

Datasets and Federated Setup. We evaluated FedPCD on CIFAR-10, CIFAR-100, and Fashion-MNIST datasets Krizhevsky et al. (2009); Xiao et al. (2017). We use a CNN with 3 convolutional layers for CIFAR-10, Resnet18 for CIFAR-100, and a multilayer perceptron (MLP) with two hidden layers for FashionMNIST. To simulate non-IID data, we use Dirichlet partitioning following Acar et al. (2020) with α ∈ { 0.01 , 0.1, 1},8where smaller values of α correspond to stronger statistical heterogeneity across clients. We consider 5% and 10% participation rates of clients, corresponding to ρ ∈ { 0.05 , 0.1}, in a pool of N = 100 clients for each dataset. Selected clients perform one epoch of local training before returning the update to the server. We train the CIFAR-10 and CIFAR-100 dataset models for 500 communication rounds, and FashionMNIST for 300 rounds. For FedPCD-specific hyperparameters in the main results, we experiment with H = 10 and 

τ ∈ { 0.005 , 0.01 , 0.02 , 0.05 , 0.075 , 0.1}. Additional model and training details are provided in App. A. 

Metrics and Baselines. Performance is evaluated using the mean global accuracy, the standard devia-tion, and the mean worst-10% client accuracy as a measure of tail performance, averaged across 5 seeds. We compare FedPCD against methods representing several families of federated optimization and fairness approaches. FedAvg (McMahan et al., 2017) serves as the standard aggregation baseline. We include tradi-tional fairness-aware methods AFL (Mohri et al., 2019), q-FFL (Li et al., 2019), and Ditto (Li et al., 2021). We further compare against multi-objective and conflict-aware approaches FedMGDA+ (Hu et al., 2022), FedFV (Wang et al., 2021), FedLF (Pan et al., 2024), and CRAFT (Wang et al., 2026), along with more re-cent fairness-aware aggregation methods FedHEAL (Chen et al., 2024) and FedPW (Guan et al., 2025). For methods with tunable hyperparameters, we perform a hyperparameter sweep and use the best performing variant per-dataset. All experiments use the same 5 seeds that correspond to the same data splits for a fair comparison between the baselines. The detailed set of hyperparameter settings is included in App. A.

5.2.1 Global and Tail Accuracy 

FedPCD achieves the best global accuracy in all 18 configurations of Table 1, and the highest worst-10% accuracy in 17 of them. The strongest baseline changes with the dataset and with the heterogeneity level, so we discuss the three datasets in turn.

The spread σc cannot answer this alone either, since a method can score well on spread while holding the global accuracy down. CIFAR-10. At both participation rates, FedPCD reaches FedAvg’s final accuracy by round 110 out of 500, requiring only 22% of the communication budget and corresponding to approximately 4 .5× fewer rounds to reach the same accuracy. At 5% participation, FedPCD reaches the strongest baseline’s final accuracy by round 280, corresponding to a further 1 .8× reduction in communication rounds. The advantage is smaller at 10% participation, where FedLF closely tracks FedPCD and their final accuracies are matched at the end of training. 

CIFAR-100. The communication efficiency advantage remains relevant in CIFAR-100. FedPCD reaches FedAvg’s final accuracy by rounds 180 and 140 at 5% and 10% participation, respectively. This corresponds to approximately 2 .8× and 3 .6× fewer communication rounds to match FedAvg’s performance at 500 rounds. The final accuracy of the strongest baseline is also reached earlier, at rounds 360 and 410, corresponding to 140 and 90 rounds before the end of training and approximately 1 .4× and 1 .2× better communication efficiency, respectively. 

Fashion-MNIST. The largest relative reduction in communication rounds occurs on Fashion-MNIST. FedPCD reaches FedAvg’s final accuracy after only 40 of 300 rounds at 5% participation and 30 rounds at 10%, corresponding to 7 .5× and 10 × reductions in communication rounds, respectively. It also reaches the strongest baseline’s final accuracy by rounds 170 and 180, respectively, amounting to 1 .8× and 1 .7× savings in communication budget, respectively. Overall, FedPCD demonstrates strong communication efficiency across all tested settings (see App. C.1). It reaches FedAvg’s final accuracy using only 10%–44% of the total communication rounds, and achieves the strongest baseline’s final accuracy earlier in 16 of the 18 configurations. These results show that FedPCD not only achieves stronger final performance, but also reaches a competitive performance in substantially fewer rounds. 13 6 Conclusion 

This work presents FedPCD, a priority-constrained federated optimization framework that separates global federated aggregation (FedAvg) from the protection of individual client objectives. Instead of replacing the global update with a re-weighted or symmetric multi-objective direction, FedPCD retains FedAvg as the primary descent direction and applies the minimal correction required to satisfy client-level constraints. To address partial participation, we incorporate a client history mechanism to preserve recent client directions as secondary constraints. Under deterministic full participation, we establish an O(1 /T ) convergence rate, further showing client-level progress toward simultaneous stationarity under persistent feasibility. Extensive experiments on CIFAR-10, CIFAR-100 and FashionMNIST under 2 participation levels and 3 data heterogeneity levels show superior performance in comparison to 10 tested fairness-aware FL baselines, maintaining the highest global accuracy in all cases, better worst-10% client accuracy in 17 out of 18 tested configurations, while converging in significantly fewer communication rounds. Several directions remain open as limitations of the FedPCD framework. Firstly, we use a fixed τ parameter across all secondary constraints, although the observed sensitivity to heterogeneity suggests that an adaptive client- or round-dependent parameter may further improve our gains. Additionally, the current history mechanism requires the server to observe each client’s pseudo-gradient individually rather than only their aggregate, limiting applicability to secure aggregation systems. This motivates privacy-compatible variants of FedPCD. Finally, we note that the evaluation is restricted to N = 100 clients and one local epoch, so the behavior of FedPCD under many local steps, where pseudo-gradient directions degrade through client drift, is not established.

A Reproducibility Details 

Model and Training Details. For CIFAR-10, we use a three-layer CNN with 3 × 3 convolutional layers containing 32, 64, and 128 channels. Each convolutional layer is followed by GroupNorm, ReLU activation, and 2 × 2 max pooling, followed by a classification head with dimensions 2048 → 256 → 10. For CIFAR-100, we use a ResNet-18 with a 3 × 3 stride-one stem and no initial max pooling. Each of the four residual stages contains two residual blocks with 64, 128, 256, and 512 channels, respectively, with GroupNorm used throughout the network. For FashionMNIST, images are flattened before being passed to an MLP with dimensions 784 → 256 → 128 → 10 and ReLU activations after each hidden layer. The local batch sizes are 128 and 64 for CIFAR-10 and CIFAR-100, respectively, while full-batch local training is used for FashionMNIST. The corresponding local and global learning rates are 0.05, 0.01, and 0.1. 

Experimental Control. All experiments use five paired random seeds. For a given dataset, heterogeneity setting, and seed, all compared methods use the same client data partition, initial model parameters, sequence of participating clients, minibatch ordering, and client test partitions. Clients are sampled uniformly without replacement at each communication round. Local optimization uses SGD with cross-entropy loss, without momentum or weight decay. All performance metrics are reported using the model obtained at the final communication round. 

Baselines Implementation and Hyperparameters. The baselines included in our experimental eval-uation were selected based on their public implementation availability to support faithful comparisons. For methods with tunable method-specific hyperparameters, we evaluate the grid in Table 2 and report the best performing hyperparameters per dataset after averaging the five seeds. For a fair comparison, the methods that implement similar history-window mechanisms, FedFV Wang et al. (2021) and FedLF Pan et al. (2024), were both evaluated at H = 10. 

B Expanded Theoretical Analysis 

B.1 Assumptions and Proof Overview 

The proof controls the individual client gradients before bounding the global gradient. This is necessary because the global gradient can be small through cancellation among client gradients, whereas simultaneous client stationarity requires every client gradient to be small. We first show that the alignment condition prevents such cancellation among the pseudo-gradients. We then bound the error from local training and establish a decrease in the global objective proportional to the squared average of the client gradient norms. Setting and regularity. We analyze the updates in Equations 7–12 under full participation, deterministic local gradient descent, a common fixed number K ≥ 1 of local steps, and H = 0. The weights pi > 0 are fixed and sum to one. Each Fi has an L-Lipschitz gradient, with L > 0, and F has a finite lower bound F⋆.Thus, F is also L-smooth. The local and server learning rates are positive constants satisfying the conditions of Theorem 4.1; they do not depend on the number of rounds T . We require neither convexity nor a uniform bound on the local gradients. Stochastic gradients, partial participation, and historical directions are outside this theorem’s scope. 

Feasibility and uniform alignment. Recall that It = {i : gi,t ̸ = 0 } denotes the clients with nonzero pseudo-gradients. At every round with It̸ = ∅, we assume that the QP is feasible and its solution satisfies Equation 13 for every i ∈ I t, with the same γ ∈ (0 , 1] throughout the run. D.1 Component Ablation 

To assess the contribution of the individual components of FedPCD, we experiment with removing the history mechanism, the secondary progress constraints by setting τ = 0, the normalization step, and the primary objective independently. All of these experiments are conducted with 10% participation rate with the CIFAR-10 dataset averaged over 5 seeds. For nonzero-τ variants, we use τ = (0 .01 , 0.02 , 0.075) for 

α = (0 .01 , 0.1, 1), respectively, which are the best τ values in their respective α from Table 1. For the history-free ablation, we report the best tested values, τ = (0 .1, 0.1, 0.1) for α = (0 .01 , 0.1, 1), respectively. Because removing history reduces the number of secondary constraints, the QP can accommodate larger progress thresholds, which explains why the preferred τ values are higher than those used with history. The results of these experiments are reported in Table 4. The history mechanism has the largest effect, particularly under stronger heterogeneity. Removing history reduces FedPCD from 74 .84% to 59 .26% global accuracy at α = 0 .01, with a corresponding drop in worst-10% accuracy. This supports the motivation for retaining recently observed client directions when only a small fraction of clients participate in each round. Setting τ = 0 also consistently reduces performance relative to the complete method, showing that enforcing positive client progress provides an additional benefit beyond the history mechanism itself. Removing normalization leads to smaller but consistent degradations in global and tail-client accuracy, while removing the primary direction causes a larger loss, confirming the importance of anchoring the constrained solution to the current federated aggregate. D.2 History window sweep 

To examine how much historical client information is needed under partial participation, we vary the history horizon H from 0 to 10 and report global accuracy, worst-10% client accuracy, and the number of active secondary constraints in Fig. 17. Increasing H consistently improves both global and tail-client performance, with the largest gains occurring over the first few additional rounds of history. On CIFAR-10, for instance, the 5% participation setting increases from below 50% to over 70% global accuracy and from approximately 20% to above 50% worst-10% accuracy as the history window is expanded. CIFAR-100 exhibits a similarly strong effect, with both metrics continuing to improve across tested range. In contrast, Fashion-MNIST reaches most of its global accuracy improvement by approximately H = 3–5, after which the benefit of additional history becomes comparatively small. The bottom row of Fig. 17 shows that these gains coincide with a steady increase in the number of active secondary constraints, confirming that a larger history window allows a broader set of recently observed clients to influence the constrained update. The effect is stronger at 10% participation, where more secondary objectives are taken into account over the same history horizon. Importantly, performance eventually exhibits diminishing returns even though the number of constraints continues to increase. This is particularly visible on CIFAR-10 and Fashion-MNIST, suggesting that once sufficient client coverage has been obtained, retaining 28 increasingly old directions provides limited additional benefit. Overall, the sweep supports the use of history as a mechanism for compensating for partial participation, while also showing that excessively long history windows are not necessary to obtain most of the improvement. 50 60 70

D.3 Normalization Study 

The main FedPCD formulation uses instantaneous ℓ2 normalization of the current client directions, whereas the original PCD formulation also considers normalization based on exponential moving averages Varam & Alhajri (2026). Therefore, we compare instantaneous normalization against EMA normalization with 

β ∈ { 0.5, 0.8, 0.9} to determine whether maintaining normalization statistics across rounds is beneficial in the federated setting. As shown in Table 5, instantaneous ℓ2 normalization achieves the best global accuracy at all three heterogeneity levels and gives the strongest results for most of the remaining metrics. The main exception occurs at α = 0 .1, where EMA with β = 0 .8 slightly improves the client standard deviation from 7.45% to 7 .44% and the worst-10% accuracy from 61 .28% to 61 .83%. These differences are small and do not persist across the other settings. The basic ℓ2 normalization provides better performance without requiring client-specific normalization statistics to be maintained across intermittent participation, supporting its use in the main FedPCD formulation.
