# Memory-Efficient Integer-Only Training of Deep Neural Networks

## Abstract 

Training neural networks in reduced precision can yield significant efficiency gains, yet all existing methods that scale to standard computer vision benchmarks retain floating-point in at least one stage, typically the weight update or optimizer state. We present a training algorithm that scales to modern deep neural networks, in which every tensor (weights, gradients, and activations) is stored as INT8, and every operation (forward pass, backward pass, weight update, optimizer update) is performed in integer arithmetic. Specifically, tensors are stored as signed integer mantissas paired with explicit power-of-two exponents, on which integer-only operations are performed using five custom composable building blocks (products, sums, divide-by-constant, nonlinear functions, and weight updates). This allows complete implementation of trainable integer layers, including convolutions, batch and layer normalization, softmax, and GELU. A per-group velocity mechanism with independent exponent tracking preserves gradient precision across neurons and channels with diverse magnitudes. As every tensor fits in INT8, training memory is roughly 4× smaller than the FP32 baseline. We validate our training algorithm on CNNs (ResNet-18/34/50/101, MobileNetV2) and Vision Transformers (ViT-Ti / B), demonstrating for the first time that training neural networks with INT8 tensors and integer arithmetic can achieve competitive accuracy on CIFAR-10, CIFAR-100 and ImageNet at a reduced memory budget. 

## 1 Introduction 

The cost of training deep neural networks continues to grow with model size and dataset scale. A natural way to reduce this cost is to lower numerical precision, replacing 32-bit floating-point arithmetic with narrower integer operations that are cheaper in energy, area, and latency. Mixed-precision training Micikevicius et al. [2018] showed that FP16 suffices for forward and backward propagation with minimal accuracy loss, and later methods pushed the datapath further to FP8 Micikevicius et al. [2022] and INT8 Zhu et al. [2020], Zhao et al. [2021]. However, these approaches still rely on floating-point arithmetic in at least one critical component, such as the weight update, optimizer state, or nonlinear functions. This residual dependence on floating-point arithmetic remains a practical bottleneck for two comple-mentary reasons. On GPUs, although floating-point computation is efficient, the persistent training state, including weights, gradients, and optimizer moments, still requires 12 bytes per parameter. As a result, model and batch size are often constrained by FP32 optimizer state rather than by the low-precision compute path itself. On integer-only platforms, including microcontrollers, fixed-function accelerators, and in-memory compute substrates, floating-point support may be absent entirely. In this setting, eliminating all floating-point operations is a fundamental prerequisite for on-device learning.

Existing approaches that move toward fully integer training Wang et al. [2022], Hu et al. [2025] either do not include standard optimization mechanisms such as momentum and learning-rate schedules, or remain restricted to small models and tasks. We address these challenges through a unified integer-only training framework. Our key observation is that any tensor can be represented as a signed integer mantissa together with an explicit power-of-two exponent, so arithmetic remains in the integer domain while scale is tracked exactly through exponent addition. From this representation, we derive five reusable primitives: products, sums, divide-by-constant, nonlinear functions, and weight updates. These primitives compose into complete layers with integer-only forward and backward computation. To preserve gradient precision when magnitudes vary substantially within a weight tensor, we further introduce a per-group velocity mechanism that maintains separate exponent tracking for different parameter groups, such as output channels, and enables stable integer-only SGD with momentum. Together, these ideas define, to our knowledge, the first fully integer-only training framework spanning forward propagation, backward propagation, and parameter updates that scales to modern deep neural networks and standard computer vision benchmarks. The framework is general enough to realize standard CNN and ViT layers, including integer-only softmax, GELU, batch and layer normalization. 

## 2 Related Work 

We organize prior work by how far each approach eliminates floating-point from training; Table 1 summarizes the comparison and Table 2 lists the per-tensor storage format of each method. 

Low-precision training. Mixed-precision training Micikevicius et al. [2018] showed that FP16 suffices for forward/backward passes if FP32 master weights are maintained. Subsequent methods reduced the datapath to FP8 Micikevicius et al. [2022]. Quantization-aware training methods (PACT Choi et al. [2018], LSQ Esser et al. [2020], DoReFa-Net Zhou et al. [2018], F8Net Jin et al. [2022], DQT Shalby et al. [2026]) quantize the forward pass while keeping FP32 master weights and a floating-point backward; Jacob et al. [2018] introduced an integer-arithmetic-only inference framework adopted in mobile deployment. All methods retain floating-point for the weight update. 

Integer forward and backward passes. Banner et al. [2018] were the first to quantize weights, activations, and a substantial portion of the gradient stream to 8-bit on ImageNet, though the final weight-gradient accumulation remains in higher precision. Zhu et al. [2020] introduced gradient clipping strategies for full INT8 training on ImageNet-scale CNNs; Zhao et al. [2021] added per-channel scaling to close the accuracy gap. Fixed-point variants adapt bit-widths dynamically Zhang et al. [2020], while AMPA Ding et al. [2024] pushes inference below INT8 via mixed-precision allocation. All leave the optimizer in FP32. 

Quasi-integer training. SWALP Yang et al. [2019] trains in 8-bit block floating-point, quantizing weights, activations, gradients, and the optimizer state via stochastic rounding; a higher-precision stochastic-weight-average running mean remains in float. WAGE Wu et al. [2018] was among the first to discretize weights, activations, gradients, and errors to low-bit integers simultaneously, but replace batch normalization layers with constant scaling to avoid floating-point operations. NITI Wang et al. [2022] performs all three stages (forward, backward, weight update) in integer arithmetic with a single per-layer block exponent, but this shared exponent forces small-gradient channels to lose their effective integer range once the scale adapts to large-gradient channels; the resulting sub-less-significant-bit (sub-LSB) vanishing is the failure mode that our per-group velocity (Section 3.3.1) addresses. NITI also lacks momentum and LR scheduling, and in practice scales only to AlexNet. Ghaffari et al. [2022] propose INT8 mantissas with an integer weight update, but their reference implementation relies on additional operations to switch back-and-forth from the floating point representation to the integer one, and falls back to float softmax in the ViT attention block. Tin-Tin Hu et al. [2025] targets microcontrollers but has been applied only to tiny sensor tasks. 

Integer-only nonlinear functions. On the inference side, I-BERT Kim et al. ## 3 Method 

In our framework, all persistent tensors (weights, gradients, velocity, saved activations) are stored as INT8 mantissas with power-of-two exponents (Section 3.1). Arithmetic operations (inner products, sums) accumulate in transient INT32 accumulators and are immediately compacted back to INT8 by applying a tensor range dependent shift. Whenever this shift moves bits to the right, we use stochastic rounding so that the compacted value remains unbiased while still using the available integer range effectively (Figure 1a). This representation lets standard layer operations such as multiplication, sum, division, and nonlinear transformations be implemented directly on mantissas, while the corresponding exponents are updated separately through addition (Section 3.2). For weight updates, our framework combines two mechanisms to maintain numerical efficiency and precision. First, per-group velocity assigns a separate exponent to each output neuron or channel, which improves precision when gradient magnitudes differ across channels (Figure 1b and Section 3.3). 3.3 Integer-only weight update with scale alignment 

Our target update is SGD with momentum applied to each weight tensor W , using learning rate η,momentum coefficient β ∈ [0 , 1) , a persistent velocity (momentum buffer) V of the same shape as 

W , and the loss gradient ∇W produced by backpropagation. Each step applies 

V ← βV + ∇W, W ← W − ηV. (9) Realizing Equation (9) entirely on integer mantissa-exponent pairs exposes two scale-alignment problems that are much less pronounced in the forward and backward passes. First, gradient magnitudes can vary substantially across output channels within the same weight tensor; with a single shared exponent, large-gradient channels dominate the available dynamic range and updates for smaller channels are heavily quantized or vanish entirely. Second, after many updates the integer weight mantissa drifts: ∥W ∥∞ may overflow INT8, or shrink so that most of the available range goes unused, in either case losing precision at the next subtraction. We address the first with per-group velocity (Section 3.3.1) and the second with reactive rescaling (Section 3.3.3). 

3.3.1 Per-group velocity 

Rather than storing the velocity V as one tensor with a single shared exponent, we partition it along the output-neurons/channels axis of the weights W into Ng groups, each with its own exponent:

## 5 Conclusion 

We demonstrated that a full SGD-with-momentum training pipeline can be realized end to end on INT8 mantissa-exponent tensors with no floating-point fallback at any stage. The resulting algorithm is, to our knowledge, the first integer-only training framework that scales to standard computer-vision benchmarks at modern depth: it trains ResNet-18/34/50/101 and MobileNetV2 to within a few percent of FP32 across CIFAR-10, CIFAR-100, and ImageNet, fine-tunes ViT-B integer-only, and trains ViT-Ti from scratch. Methodologically, per-output-channel velocity exponents are essential for integer optimizer state. Because every persistent tensor is INT8, training memory is roughly 4×

smaller than FP32 across every configuration we tested. This shifts the accuracy-memory frontier: at a fixed memory budget, the integer pipeline trains substantially deeper and more accurate architectures than FP32 can admit; at a fixed architecture, it trains within a few percent of the FP32 baseline at a quarter of the memory. The same pipeline can in principle run on hardware without floating-point support (e.g. microcontrollers, in-memory compute substrates) opening on-device training of ResNet-and ViT-scale models. 

Limitations and future work. Extending the framework to adaptive optimizers (an integer Adam, requiring integer square roots, integer division, and exponent bookkeeping for the bias-correction terms) is the most substantive open direction for moving to architectures that are typically trained with Adam or AdamW. Replacing power-of-two exponents with richer scaling schemes (learned scales, non-power-of-two block exponents) would use dynamic range more efficiently and likely narrow the remaining accuracy gap on MobileNetV2 and ViT. Operator fusion (e.g. reducing the 9requantization points per ViT block) is a possible engineering direction for requantization-heavy architectures. Finally, bringing the framework to LLM training and parameter-efficient fine-tuning (LoRA) is a natural next step, with the same structural advantage of keeping the optimizer state at INT8 rather than FP32.

Per-tensor storage format. The per-tensor formats assumed for each method are listed in Table 2 (main text). Summing the weight, gradient, and velocity widths gives the persistent state in bytes per parameter; the activation format scales with batch size and input resolution. Two points deserve comment. Hardware and software. All training runs use a single NVIDIA A100-SXM4 (40 GB) GPU. The integer pipeline is implemented on top of PyTorch 2.10 with CUDA 12.8 and cuDNN 9.1.

## E Additional experiments 

This appendix supplements Section 4 with: (i) training dynamics for ResNet-18/CIFAR-10 (Figure 3); (ii) an operator-level breakdown of the ViT-B accuracy gap on CIFAR-10 (Table 11); and (iii) a layer-by-layer cosine-similarity analysis of the INT8 ViT that motivates the need for fine-tuning. 

Training dynamics. Figure 3 shows that INT8 training tracks the FP32 baseline on ResNet-18/CIFAR-10 over 200 epochs; step-LR drops produce clean discrete transitions with no instability or divergence. 

Where the INT8 ViT drifts. Table 11 attributes the entire 3.2% pre-finetuning gap to the Linear projections: replacing only the Linear layers with INT8 (keeping LayerNorm, GELU, and softmax in float) costs 3.2%, while subsequently moving LayerNorm, GELU, and softmax into the integer pipeline costs nothing further (LayerNorm −0.1, GELU +0 .1, softmax +0 .2). In other words, the integer attention machinery introduced in this paper – LUT-based softmax, 1/√dh as a rational shift, and the cached-p Jacobian backward – is essentially free in accuracy terms; the residual gap is the generic Linear-quantization error that any INT8 fwd/bwd method incurs. 18 Figure 3: ResNet-18 / CIFAR-10: integer-only (INT8) vs. FP32 baseline, 200 epochs, step LR at 60/120/180. A complementary cosine-similarity analysis of the INT8 ViT without fine-tuning shows where this Linear-quantization error compounds. The match to the float model degrades through the stack from 0.977 after the patch embedding to 0.83 after block 0, 0.64 after block 2, and below 0.01 after block 5. The drop is concentrated inside the attention sublayer – 0.95 → 0.64 in a single block – but, consistent with Table 11, the source is not the attention-specific operators: it is the four Linear projections per block ( QW Q, KW K , V W V , and the output projection), whose ∼72 INT8 compaction points compound multiplicatively across the 12-block stack. Fine-tuning then closes the gap as the weights adapt to the INT8 quantizer. 19
