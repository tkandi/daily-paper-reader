## Abstract 

Large language models (LLMs) trained on vast amounts of data have demonstrated im-pressive performance over a wide range of tasks, but future performance gains will require increasing amounts of data and compute. Federated Learning (FL) can facilitate training on decentralized private data, but client resource limitations can make FL training impractical, particularly as model sizes continue to expand. These resource constraints make the widely accepted assumption of a shared server–client architecture impractical. Model heterogeneous FL offers a promising alternative by adapting model size to client capabilities; however, previous work is limited to the pre-training setting, and these conclusions on sub-network selection do not necessarily hold for fine-tuning. Furthermore, prior approaches have created client sub-networks without considering their unique data distributions, thereby overlooking the opportunity to tailor sub-network architecture to local data. Motivated by evidence that attention heads exhibit task dependency within layer defined feature spaces, we propose Op-timal Hydra, a transformer based heterogeneous FL framework for fine-tuning that combines global layer selection with client specific attention head selection aligned with local data distributions. Optimal Hydra makes explicit use of client data to produce client-specific sub-networks we call Hydra-nets. To our knowledge, this is the first model heterogeneous FL approach tailored to LLM fine-tuning. Experiments on three datasets show significant improvements over a number of baseline methods while also reducing local resource demands. 

## 1 Introduction 

Foundation models have demonstrated advanced abilities on diverse tasks and have been widely adopted in domains including healthcare, education and autonomous vehicles (Bommasani et al., 2021; Thirunavukarasu et al., 2023; Brown et al., 2020; Hu et al., 2023). Established scaling laws indicate that continued performance gains will require increasingly large amounts of data (Kaplan et al., 2020), a requirement at odds with the growing awareness of digital privacy. Federated learning (FL) (McMahan et al., 2017) offers a privacy preserving alternative that may create a greater willingness to share data Sani et al. (2024); however, the application of FL to foundation models is challenging due to large parameter counts that considerably exceed the capabilities of a typical edge device (Yao et al., 2024). Previous attempts to bridge this gap have largely relied on Parameter-Efficient Fine-Tuning (PEFT) (Babakniya et al., 2023a; Tian et al., 2024; Bai et al., 2024), but these methods can struggle to match the performance of full fine-tuning and are prone to overfitting when parameters are strictly limited (Chen et al., 2022). Furthermore, these methods still require homogeneous model architectures, so client nodes must have adequate resources to store and train the entire large model, which is often not the case (Yao et al., 2024). This creates a dilemma: either we limit model complexity to accommodate the most under resourced client or we exclude low resource clients from training. Neither of these options is ideal, since limiting model complexity eliminates our ability to train large SoTA models and excluding low resource clients from training creates a bias towards the data of those with better resources (Kairouz et al., 2021). Model heterogeneous FL relaxes the assumption that server and client model architectures must be identical, it allows for federated training with heterogeneous models better suited to unique client capabilities. Subsequent work improved stability by training model parameters more equally (Alam et al., 2022; Liao et al., 2025); however, all of these approaches overlook the fact that optimal sub-networks are intrinsically linked to the data they encounter (Frankle & Carbin, 2018). For FL in particular, client data distributions are often highly heterogeneous and tailoring sub-network architectures to local data presents an important opportunity to capture distribution-specific features. Notably, while prior work focuses exclusively on the pre-training setting, our work addresses fine-tuning and we will show that previous conclusions regarding strictly equal parameter training in the pre-training setting do not necessarily hold in the context of fine-tuning. We propose Optimal Hydra , a method that adapts model-heterogeneous FL for LLM fine-tuning. Prior research has focused exclusively on pre-training (Alam et al., 2022; Diao et al., 2020; Liao et al., 2025; Wen et al., 2022; Horvath et al., 2021); we address this gap in the literature by being the first to investigate model-heterogeneous FL in the fine-tuning setting, a setting that has become increasingly relevant as large foundation models become the norm. We also take the opportunity to improve upon the random sub-network selection used in previous works by creating data-informed sub-networks we call Hydra-Nets that are tailored to local client data to better capture distribution-specific features. Fig. 1 provides a high level overview of our Hydra-Net selection method that proceeds in two steps; common layer selection and individual head selection . Common layer selection is motivated by the fact that transformer layers govern macro level feature transformations (Voita et al., 2019) and that in FL a lack of consensus amongst client sub-networks can negatively affect global model convergence (Diao et al., 2020; Horvath et al., 2021; Babakniya et al., 2023b). We adapt a metric used to determine the relative importance of transformer layers by He et al. (2024) to enable clients to collaboratively identify a small subset of “important" layers, establishing a shared structural backbone across clients from which no attention heads may be removed. Subsequently, each individual client will select a unique subset of attention heads to keep from each “unimportant" layer via a gradient based scoring metric, the rest will be discarded. Ultimately, Hydra-Net selection is motivated by the task dependent nature of attention heads to create targeted sub-networks, informed by each client’s unique local 2data distributions. Our key contributions are as follows: • We propose Optimal Hydra for model heterogeneous federated fine-tuning using data-informed LLM sub-networks.As the first model-heterogeneous FL method to tailor sub-network architectures to local client data, Optimal Hydra achieves up to a 42 .5% improvement over the best performing baseline in challenging model heterogeneous conditions and achieves reductions in computational and memory resource utilization up to 32 .0% and 29 .3%, respectively. • We demonstrate that assumptions from the pre-training about evenly distributing parameters can be relaxed in the fine-tuning regime due to the strong initial representations provided by the pre-trained model. • Based on representation dynamics and prior work suggesting that attention heads may be linked to specific tasks within layer feature spaces, we combine common layer selection to create Hydra-Nets, data-informed sub-networks that are better able to capture distribution specific features. 

## 2 Background and Related Work 

Federated learning (FL) (McMahan et al., 2017) is a privacy preserving, distributed, machine learning paradigm that allows collaborative model training without data sharing. Resource limitations on federated edge devices can make FL challenging to execute using current SotA foundation models, examples in the literature (Babakniya et al., 2023a; Bai et al., 2024; Tian et al., 2024; Wu et al., 2024) have relied heavily on parameter efficient fine-tuning (PEFT) which significantly reduces communication costs but remains highly burdensome in terms of local computation and memory requirements. While communication constraints have historically been considered the most significant federated resource bottleneck (McMahan et al., 2017; Kairouz et al., 2021), the large parameter counts of modern foundation models have increased the significance of memory and computational resource bottlenecks (Yao et al., 2024; Sani et al., 2024) since the common assumption in FL, including the aforementioned PEFT methods, is that server and client models possess the same architecture. This underlying assumption either limits the choice of global model so that the most resource limited client is able to participate; or selects a more complex global model and excludes resource limited clients altogether. A natural solution is to distribute sub-networks according to client capabilities, but stable aggregation of heterogeneous local architectures into a single global model is challenging and requires careful consideration of base and sub-network model architectures in order to be successful (Alam et al., 2022). HeteroFL (Diao et al., 2020), widely regarded as the first example of model heterogeneous FL in the literature, creates static sub-models that train model parameters unequally. FedDrop (Wen et al., 2022) randomly drops a fraction of neurons in each layer, which can lead to inconsistent updates and convergence instability, particularly when data are not independently and identically distributed (i.i.d.). FedRolex (Alam et al., 2022) and FedBRB (Liao et al., 2025) improve upon these methods by iterating through model parameters so they are all trained evenly, yet these approaches fail to consider the quality of the sub-models and how differences in local data distributions can affect the suitability of the selected sub-network from round to round. These approaches are designed for pre-training, use relatively small models and focus primarily on computer vision tasks. Due to the learning differences that exist between federated pre-training and fine-tuning (Nguyen et al., 2022; Legate et al., 2023), we cannot assume their suitability for large model fine-tuning. Additionally, pervious model heterogeneous FL experiments have used convolutional neural networks (CNNs), none of them have been adapted for the transformer architecture, ubiquitous in modern machine learning. The opacity of an LLMs internal mechanisms makes improving the efficiency of supervised fine-tuning (SFT) methods challenging. Previous attention head focused interpretability studies suggest that they exhibit a high degree of task specificity (Zhao et al., 2024; Yin et al., 2024) and redundancy (Michel et al., 2019; Diao et al., 2020) during the SFT process. Zhao et al. (2024) observe rapid task adaptation of attention heads during SFT, they also show task dependence by targeting specific heads, which consistently provides 3better performance. LoFiT (Yin et al., 2024) trains small localized adapters to fine tune attention heads. The authors find that identifying and targeting task heads most important for a task is more effective than standard fine-tuning, random head selection or targeting specific layers identified by probing. Michel et al. (2019) find that while many heads are task dependent, some are universally important regardless of task. 

## 3 Optimal Hydra 

Optimal Hydra adapts model heterogeneous FL for transformer models using heterogeneous client sub-networks called Hydra-Nets, clients train their Hydra-Nets locally and send updates back to the server for aggregation. While aggregating updates from heterogeneous local sub-networks into a single global model has presented a significant challenge for model heterogeneous FL, our approach overcomes this difficulty through direct index-mapping. During the aggregation phase, local updates are mapped back to their original coordinates in the global transformer architecture and parameter averaging is scaled by the total number of data samples residing in clients contributing to the update of a given parameter instead of the typical FL approach of scaling updates according to the number of data samples residing in all clients participating in the federated round (McMahan et al., 2017). This approach lets the global model accumulate specialized, data-informed gradients from specific heads without the gradient interference present from full parameter federated averaging.

3.2 Hydra-Net Selection 

Model heterogeneity creates particular challenges for model aggregation in FL and these systems are particularly sensitive to client drift (Karimireddy et al., 2020) because of their un-equal parameter training 4(Alam et al., 2022). One of our key contributions is the use of data-informed sub-networks to stabilize aggregation and improve training outcomes. To this end, we combine a number of previous insights to optimize our Hydra-Net selection. Attention heads have been shown to exhibit a high degree of task specificity (Zhao et al., 2024; Yin et al., 2024) and certain attention heads exhibit universal importance across domains Michel et al. (2019). Because these critical heads concentrate within key functional layers that drive core feature transformations (Voita et al., 2019) we establish a two stage Hydra-Net selection strategy: common layer selection and individual head selection. As shown in Fig. 1, clients first work collaboratively to select a small number of layers from which no attention heads are dropped. They subsequently select attention heads with high sensitivity to their own local data distributions, which they keep from the remaining layers while dropping the rest. In what follows, we present a detailed discussion of the procedures for common and individual head selection.

When developing sub-network selection strategies, it is tempting to consider individual client properties in isolation; however, this approach over simplifies the problem and a lack of consensus between client sub-networks can inhibit global model convergence and cause serious performance degradation (Babakniya et al., 2023b; Alam et al., 2022). To prevent this from occurring in Hydra-Nets, all clients retain a common subset of parameters. Instead of attempting to negotiate consensus on individual attention heads which risks over-specializing or missing critical head interactions, we select common parameters by layer instead of opting for fine grained head-selection. He et al. (2024) use a metric based on cosine similarity to score the relative importance of transformer layers, where layers with high similarity between inputs and outputs are deemed redundant. This provides a computationally efficient proxy to identify layers that drive critical representation shifts. We evaluate layer redundancy locally at each client using activation similarity statistics of each transformer block (Alg. 1, lns. 5–8), and apply a mixture model (Dempster et al., 1977) (Alg. 1, ln. 12) to separate them into three distinct categories. 1. low redundancy i.e. cosine similarity near zero 2. medium redundancy i.e. cosine similarity ≈ 0.5 − 0.83. high redundancy i.e. cosine similarity > 0.9Very few layers (empirically, < 15%) fall into the the low and medium redundancy categories, so we select all of these layers and for simplicity, and retain all of their attention heads. The remaining layers will be used for personalized attention head selection via Algo. 2. These two disjoint subsets are the end goal of Algo. 1 where they are defined as Lkeep and Ldrop , respectively. Common layers serve two purposes: preserve the 5functionality of the most important layers, and provide a shared structural backbone between all clients to defend against client drift.

## 4 Experiments 

While Optimal Hydra is feasible for any number of attention heads dropped, we study model heterogeneity by focusing on four discreet sizes; all attention heads kept, or full ( f), three quarters of attention heads kept (3q ), half of attention heads kept ( h), one quarter of attention heads kept ( q). Heterogeneous configurations are indicated by f, 3q , h, q, followed by the number of clients assigned that model size in brackets. 

Datasets We consider three datasets, Natural Instructions (NI) (nat, 2022), Alpaca (Taori et al., 2023) and Dolly-15K (Conover et al., 2023). Dolly-15K samples are distributed between clients using a Dirichlet distribution with α = 0 .5. The creative writing task of Dolly-15K is not included in any of the local client data distributions and is instead used for global evaluation. The Alpaca dataset does not include pre-determined task categories, so we define six categories, summarization, reasoning, classification, mathematics, QA and other, and assign each sample a classification by searching for keywords in the input. As with Dolly-15K, samples are distributed between clients using a Dirichlet distribution with α = 0 .5. The“other" task is selected for global evaluation. For NI, which has 857 unique tasks, 119 test tasks are held-out and used for global evaluation, while each of the remaining 738 tasks is uniquely assigned to one client. This isolated task distribution makes the NI dataset particularly challenging.

Improved Resource Utilization: Tab. 1 provides memory and compute comparisons for the resource allocation configurations in each of our experiments. These values are also expressed as the percentage improvement each configuration provides over the full FedAvg baseline. In the most extreme case where each client selects only 25% of the attention heads, we achieve savings of 32 .0% for computation and 29 .3% for memory. Values provided in Tab. 1 are calculated using a sequence length of 2048; however, since computational requirements depend heavily on sequence length, we note that an increase in sequence length to ≈ 12000 achieves 50% savings in computational resources. This computational sequence length dependence theoretically makes Optimal Hydra an attractive prospect for FL with long context LLMs. 

Results: The results in Tab. 2 consider a homogeneous resource setting in which all clients retain the same number of attention heads, but may retain different subsets of heads. In this scenario, clients have equal resource capabilities, although these budgets may still be substantially smaller than those required for full-model fine-tuning. We also report the zero-shot performance of the pre-trained model and the performance of full-model FedAvg. In this setting, the relative performance of the methods is mixed. Optimal Hydra achieves the best score in the majority of configurations and is consistently the best-performing method or competitive with the strongest baseline. The adapted HeteroFL and FedRolex baselines exhibit less consistency; HeteroFL performs substantially worse than the other methods for H(20) on Alpaca, and FedRolex exhibits a similar sub-standard performance for Q(20) on Dolly-15K. These results suggest that, when all clients have the same resource budget, the benefit of data-informed head selection is present but relatively modest, and the choice of which heads are retained becomes increasingly important as the available model capacity decreases. Fig. 2 considers a more challenging setting in which clients have substantially different resource constraints. We assign different fractions of clients different model sizes, with particular emphasis on configurations where most 9clients are under-resourced. Unlike the homogeneous-resource setting, Optimal Hydra consistently outperforms both baselines across all heterogeneous resource configurations. The advantage is most pronounced for the NI dataset, which has the most heterogeneous task distribution and supports our hypothesis that sub-network selection should account for both the resource constraints and client data distributions. Optimal Hydra also performs particularly well in the aforementioned low-resource setting where it achieves a 42 .5% improvement over FedRolex, the next highest scoring baseline, on the 3Q(2)H(2)Q(16) configuration for the NI dataset. Finally, model heterogeneity does not necessarily result in a performance degradation relative to full-model FedAvg. We observe that Optimal Hydra and the adapted baselines sometimes outperform the full-model FedAvg baseline, one possible explanation is that removing attention heads acts as a form of regularization during fine-tuning. This effect is most pronounced on Dolly-15K where the pre-trained model already provides strong initial representations. 

Equal Parameter Training in the Fine-Tuning Setting: Previous work in the pre-training setting has motivated the importance of evenly training model parameters (Alam et al., 2022; Liao et al., 2025); however, application of this principle to fine-tuning has not been closely examined, despite indications of behavioural differences between these two regimes (Legate et al., 2023; Nguyen et al., 2022). The objective in Eq. 6 introduces an additional hyperparameter, λ, which controls how strictly equal parameter training is enforced. When λ = 0, the balance penalty vanishes and head assignments are determined solely by client-specific importance scores. As λ increases, the objective increasingly favors equal head distribution across clients, 

λ ≈ 1 × 10 6 effectively recovers the hard-bounded objective in Eq. 5, enforcing an even distribution of heads between clients for each layer. Dolly-15K has the lowest attention head score diversity between clients, with clients selecting similar subsets of heads in each layer. The best Rouge-L scores were often achieved when λ = 0 or λ = 1, indicating that the strict requirement for equal parameter training established in federated pre-training may be greatly relaxed during fine-tuning. NI displayed the greatest attention head score diversity, with clients naturally gravitating towards different attention heads. NI generally achieved better Rouge-L scores with larger values of λ, sometimes as high as 500 or 1000, indicating a greater need for enforced attention head diversity. To account for the difference in optimal λ values, we turn to the zero-shot performance strength of the pretrained representations. Dolly-15K had zero-shot performance of 26 .694, significantly higher than the 5.698 achieved on NI. The strong initial representations available to Dolly-15K make strictly even parameter fine-tuning unnecessary. The weaker initial representations available for NI may make greater participation of all parameters more important, resulting in behavior more similar to the equal parameter training observed in the pre-training regime. 

## 5 Conclusion 

We introduced Optimal Hydra, a model-heterogeneous federated learning framework for fine-tuning large language models under heterogeneous client resource constraints. Optimal Hydra creates data-informed Hydra-Nets that permit resource limited clients to adapt to their available resources, by retaining model parameters best suited to their own local data. Across three datasets and multiple resource configurations, Optimal Hydra consistently outperformed adapted model-heterogeneous FL baselines in the most resource-heterogeneous settings while reducing local computational and memory requirements by up to 32 .0% and 29 .3%, respectively. Our results also challenge the assumption that model parameters must be trained equally across clients. In the fine-tuning setting, the necessary degree of parameter balancing depends on the strength of the pretrained representations and the heterogeneity of the local data distributions.
