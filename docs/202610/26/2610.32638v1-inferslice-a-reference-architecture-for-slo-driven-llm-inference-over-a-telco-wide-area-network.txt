## ABSTRACT 

LLM inference is increasingly deployed as a core service for enterprise and interactive applications, but sovereignty, compliance, latency and resource-locality requirements can limit the suitability of centralised cloud deployments. Telco operators offer geographically distributed compute and network infrastructure for addressing these con-straints. However, existing LLM serving frameworks are designed for tightly coupled data centres and provide limited support for coordinating geographically distributed and heterogeneous resources. We present InferSlice ,a reference architecture for Service-Level Objective (SLO)-driven LLM inference over a telco infrastructure, integrating network slicing, GPU partitioning, elastic serving and observability. Led by BT in collaboration with the University of Bristol and NVIDIA, and realised on the JOINER UK national-scale wide-area network (WAN), InferSlice is evaluated on service differentiation across tiers, elastic scale-out, client-edge bandwidth as a bottleneck, and the reach of tensor, pipeline, data parallelism, and prefill/decode disaggregation over the WAN. Our results show that, on a general-purpose telco network, tiers yield distinct latency-throughput regimes, and weight streaming shortens scale-out time. Replication is placement-insensitive but memory-bound; pipeline parallelism tolerates near- and mid-cross-site hops, while tensor parallelism and disaggregation fail beyond a single subnet hop. We share practical insights from operating SLO-driven inference on a telco network, lessons learned and open questions, and hope to encourage further research on distributed and heterogeneous LLM inference systems.

InferSlice is realised as a cloud-native platform that integrates distributed LLM serving, resource orches-tration, network management, and end-to-end observability across multiple sites, extending service differentiation from network connectivity to AI inference itself. Our experiments examine whether resource and scaling policies can enforce differen-tiated SLO outcomes across service tiers under increasing load, whether inference scaling mechanisms can reduce scale-out latency, and how wide-area network latency and bandwidth constrain distributed model parallelism and dis-aggregation across sites (Figure 1). All experiments are conducted over a shared WAN, without dedicated AI in-terconnects or RDMA between sites, reflecting real-world conditions in general-purpose telco infrastructure rather than specialised AI-cluster fabrics. Together, these evaluations characterise the feasibility and operating boundaries of SLO-driven LLM inference over geographically distributed telco infrastructure and inform the design principles presented in this work. 

## 2 BACKGROUND AND RELATED WORK 

2.1 Distributed LLM Inference Fundamentals 

Serving LLMs at scale relies on distribution strategies that can be broadly categorised into three largely composable dimensions: model parallelism , replication , and functional disaggregation . While these dimensions are not fully in-dependent, disaggregation in particular alters the internal structure of what would otherwise constitute a single replica. Each addresses a distinct scaling question: model paral-lelism scales a single model instance across accelerators; replication scales the number of independent model in-stances; and functional disaggregation scales distinct stages of the inference lifecycle independently of one another. 

2.1.1 Model parallelism. 

When a model does not fit, or does not perform adequately, on a single accelerator, its computation may be partitioned across devices. Tensor Parallelism (TP) partitions individ-ual layers’ weight matrices across accelerators, requiring frequent collective communication, such as all-reduce, all-gather, or reduce-scatter, between participating accelerators. Pipeline Parallelism (PP) instead partitions the model by layer across devices, exchanging intermediate activations be-tween pipeline stages and trading reduced synchronisation frequency for pipeline bubble overhead. Expert parallelism (EP) shards mixture-of-experts layers across devices, rout-ing tokens to the accelerators holding the relevant experts. Each of these strategies imposes synchronisation between participating devices at a frequency that typically confines

2.1.2 Replication. 

Often referred to as Data Parallelism (DP) in inference serv-ing, replication deploys complete, independent copies of a model and distributes incoming requests across them, rather than partitioning a single model instance. Because repli-cas require no inference-time synchronisation with one an-other, replication tolerates substantially higher latency and lower-bandwidth interconnects than TP, PP, or EP, and is in principle viable across racks, sites, or wide-area network links. Replication therefore provides a natural mechanism for scaling capacity and elasticity: replicas can be added or removed independently as demand changes, while requests can be routed according to load, locality, or SLO require-ments. This independence also provides fault isolation, as congestion or failure affecting one replica need not directly stall others. 

2.1.3 Functional disaggregation. 

A further scaling dimension separates distinct functions of the inference service into independently provisioned com-ponents. The most common example is prefill-decode disag-gregation, where the compute-bound prefill phase, respon-sible for processing the input prompt, is separated from the memory-bandwidth-bound decode phase that generates output tokens autoregressively. These services are con-nected through a Key-Value (KV) cache transfer mecha-nism (Zhong et al., 2024). A related form of functional disaggregation externalises KV-cache management itself, allowing KV states to be offloaded, persisted, shared, or reused across inference workers. Such decoupling enables resources for different functions to be scaled according to their distinct computational characteristics, at the expense of additional coordination overhead and network dependencies whose feasibility depends on KV-cache size and available interconnect bandwidth. These dimensions compose: a deployment may apply TP within a node, PP across nodes within a site, replication across sites, and, orthogonally, disaggregate prefill from decode within each replica. 2.2 SLO-Aware LLM Serving 

As LLM inference has moved from research prototypes to production services, SLOs have become a primary means of expressing serving quality from the user’s perspective. Unlike traditional request response services, LLM requests are generated incrementally: a prompt is first processed to produce the initial output token (i.e., Prefill), after which subsequent tokens are generated autoregressively (i.e., De-code). This two phase structure gives rise to distinct latency characteristics and consequently to two commonly used serving metrics. TTFT measures the elapsed time from re-quest arrival until the first output token is generated, and is strongly influenced by queueing, scheduling, and the com-pute intensive prefill phase. TPOT measures the latency between subsequent output tokens and is strongly influ-enced by the memory bandwidth and batch characteristics of the decode phase (Huang et al., 2025). An interactive application such as a chatbot is sensitive to both: a high TTFT is perceived as an unresponsive system, while a high TPOT reduces the rate at which generated content becomes available to the user. Other workloads may weight these objectives differently or instead impose end to end latency or throughput targets where token level responsiveness is less important, such as batch or offline processing. An SLO specifies a target on one or more such indicators, and SLO attainment , the proportion of requests meeting their assigned target, is related to but distinct from good-put , the rate of requests completed within their SLO per unit time (Wang et al., 2024): maximising throughput or minimising average latency does not necessarily maximise the number of requests that satisfy their individual SLOs, particularly once queueing is taken into account. This dis-tinction is sharpened under heterogeneous SLOs, where a single deployment serves multiple applications or service classes concurrently, each with different TTFT, TPOT, or latency requirements, and a request with a relaxed target can absorb delay that would cause a stricter request to violate its own; recent SLO-aware serving systems therefore treat SLO requirements as explicit inputs to scheduling rather than treating all requests as interchangeable (Hong et al., 2025; Tang et al., 2025). For geographically distributed inference, this introduces a further dimension: where a re-quest is served becomes part of the scheduling decision, since network latency, queueing conditions, and available capacity all vary by site. For InferSlice, SLO-awareness therefore provides the basis for treating a geographically distributed infrastructure as a shared resource pool, where placement is driven not only by proximity or utilisation but by which candidate site can satisfy a request’s SLO. 2.3 Telco Infrastructure as an AI Platform 

Telecommunications networks provide a mature foundation for differentiated service delivery. Network slicing enables multiple logical services to share physical infrastructure while receiving differentiated resource allocations and per-formance objectives (Alam et al., 2025). However, these objectives have historically focused primarily on network-level characteristics such as bandwidth, latency, reliability, and isolation, rather than the application-level compute re-quirements of AI services. Liu et al. extend this principle directly to LLM workloads through LLM-Slice, which cre-ates dedicated wireless network slices for LLM services and binds communication resources to service requests (Liu et al., 2024). This demonstrates how service differentiation can be applied to the network delivery of LLM services, although the work focuses on communication resource con-trol rather than the placement and orchestration of infer-ence computation. The evolution toward Multi-access Edge Computing (MEC) further extended the role of telcos from connectivity toward distributed compute, placing resources closer to users and network access points (Taleb et al., 2017). While the broader MEC vision faced challenges including fragmented infrastructure and limited demand for generic edge workloads, the emergence of telco cloud and AI-RAN, wherein AI and RAN workloads can run concurrently on shared, GPU-accelerated infrastructure, is renewing inter-est in operator infrastructure as a platform for AI (Kundu et al., 2025). AI-RAN primarily targets the co-location of AI and RAN workloads at the radio edge, with its broader commercial opportunity depending on the emergence of latency-critical, edge-native applications such as physical AI, robotics, and autonomous systems. LLM inference presents a complementary opportunity that does not require compute to be placed at the radio edge. Its service quality depends jointly on compute capacity, net-work conditions, queueing, and workload characteristics, while the geographical distribution and operational control of telco infrastructure can provide properties that are diffi-cult to obtain from centralised cloud platforms: data and service sovereignty, stronger security and governance, lower latency through proximity to users, and greater predictabil-ity and reliability through controlled network and compute resources. This creates an opportunity to use existing telco compute sites, including central offices, regional data cen-tres, and core network locations, as a distributed substrate for AI serving. In this setting, the service-differentiation principles long applied to network connectivity can be ex-tended to AI services themselves, with application-level SLOs guiding the allocation and placement of distributed inference resources. 

2.4 Related Work 

Recent production systems have demonstrated increasingly sophisticated approaches to LLM serving under heteroge-neous workloads and SLO requirements. Meta’s large-scale study of Llama inference explores millions of deployment configurations spanning heterogeneous accelerators, tensor, pipeline, expert, context, and data parallelism, as well as continuous batching and prefill-decode disaggregation, to maximise throughput while meeting latency SLOs (Cho et al., 2026). Similarly, Mitra et al. systematically evaluate disaggregated inference across hundreds of thousands of configurations, showing that its benefits depend strongly on workload characteristics, model size, dynamic rate match-InferSlice: SLO-driven LLM Inference over a Telco WAN 

ing, and elastic scaling (Mitra et al., 2026). Behdin et al. further demonstrate the scale and workload-specific opti-misation required for industrial LLM serving, achieving substantial throughput improvements through the joint op-timisation of models and serving infrastructure (Behdin et al., 2026). These works demonstrate the importance of jointly considering inference configuration, workload char-acteristics, and SLOs, but primarily target tightly coupled compute infrastructures. Meta’s study is particularly rep-resentative of this setting: although it considers diverse hardware and deployment configurations, the optimisation problem is formulated within the infrastructure available to a large-scale serving platform rather than across geographi-cally distributed telco sites (Cho et al., 2026). More closely related to telecommunications, Yet et al. eval-uate SLA-aware LLM and VLM inference across device, RAN-edge, and cloud tiers using a 5G Standalone AI-RAN testbed (Yet et al., 2026). Their experiments demonstrate the impact of execution location, model configuration, queue-ing, and network delay on SLA attainment, but use a fixed baseline policy and a hierarchical device-to-RAN-to-cloud topology rather than a distributed pool of independently provisioned serving sites. Wang et al. propose a wide-area distributed inference network that separates prefill at edge nodes from decode in a central cloud, with the two stages coordinated over a wide-area network (Wang et al., 2026). This work demonstrates the feasibility of LLM inference across wide-area telco infrastructure and is particularly rel-evant to the present setting, but its deployment follows a predetermined functional placement rather than dynamically selecting among multiple sites and inference configurations according to application-level SLOs. GORGO further con-siders network-aware LLM serving across geographically distributed inference replicas, jointly accounting for network latency, queueing, and KV-cache locality when routing re-quests to minimise TTFT and end-to-end latency (Toniolo et al., 2026). While this work demonstrates the importance of network conditions in cross-region inference decisions, it operates primarily at the request-routing layer and assumes an existing set of serving replicas, rather than jointly orches-trating compute resources, inference configurations, serving locations, and network resources across distributed sites. Petals demonstrates that very large LLMs can be exe-cuted across Internet-connected, heterogeneous, volunteer resources using fault-tolerant layer partitioning, routing, and dynamic load balancing across unreliable peers (Borzunov et al., 2023). While this work establishes the feasibility of geographically distributed inference over the public Inter-net, its objective is collaborative execution across unman-aged resources. In contrast, InferSlice considers operator-controlled telco infrastructure and investigates how network-aware service differentiation, resource slicing, and SLO-driven placement interact with multiple inference strategies (TP, PP, DP and P/D-D) over a WAN. To the best of our knowledge, there is no existing reference architecture that brings these dimensions together by treating geographically distributed telco compute sites as a shared substrate for SLO-driven LLM inference, with serving location and infer-ence configuration jointly selected according to application requirements, site-level resource availability, and network conditions. This motivates the reference architecture devel-oped in this work. 

## 3 SYSTEM DESIGN 

3.1 Physical Infrastructure Layer 

For the development of InferSlice, we assume a telecom-native infrastructure as a set of geographically distributed edge computing sites, as discussed in Section 2.3. The physical infrastructure layer, represented at the bottom of Figure 2, consists of a set of compute hosts, each providing CPU, memory, storage, and hardware acceleration, both GPU and Network Interface Card (NIC). These hosts are in-terconnected by a WAN and together form edge computing Points of Presence (PoPs), which allow InferSlice to opti-mise the placement of the inference workload and reduce latency and network overhead (for example, serving a user from the PoP nearest to them). In the deployed system, the physical layer is modelled after the JOINER federated testbed where each PoP comprises Dell R660/R760 host machines with up to 64 physical CPU cores, 512 GB of RAM and an NVIDIA L40S GPU accel-erator. Each host’s NIC is connected directly to the PoP’s edge router (Juniper ACX7100) via 10 Gbps SFP optics. Since the JOINER platform itself is multi-tenant, compute resources are exposed as Virtual Machines (VMs) to Infer-Slice. InferSlice’s storage is based on the general-purpose disks of the compute nodes, which the virtualisation layer pools into shared volumes. Further details of the testbed, including the host hardware each node carries, are given in the Annex A.

To enable scalability and configurability for modern AI workloads, a virtualisation layer is necessary. The resources of the testbed’s tenants are isolated from one another using JOINER’s virtualisation capabilities, implemented on top of VMware’s hypervisor and automation platform. 3.3 Network Isolation and Slicing Layer 

Network slicing logically partitions a shared physical net-work into multiple isolated networks (i.e., network slices), each of which can be associated with its own connectiv-ity, resource allocation, and Quality of Service (QoS) poli-cies (Wu et al., 2025a). Such isolated network slices enable service differentiation by allowing network and compute resources to be allocated and enforced independently across service tiers. This capability is particularly important for telecom operators deploying LLM inference over existing distributed infrastructure: heterogeneous inference services with different latency and throughput requirements (Wu et al., 2025b) must coexist across the same PoPs and over the shared WAN fabric, while retaining differentiated QoS performance. Rather than provisioning separate physical network infrastructure for each inference service with a specific QoS requirement, slicing allows an operator to ex-pose differentiated service tiers over a common infrastruc-ture with service-specific QoS differentiations in a dynamic, on-demand manner. 3.4 Compute Orchestration and Resource Scheduling Layer 

The compute orchestration and resource scheduling layer manages how the shared accelerator pool is partitioned and allocated across service tiers and distributed inference work-loads. Network isolation alone cannot guarantee service differentiation, since inference QoS ultimately depends on the compute capacity available to each workload. The layer therefore provides compute isolation and resource guaran-tees by assigning each tier a defined share of GPU and CPU capacity, together with quotas and scheduling priori-ties, while allowing resources to be shared across tiers. This is particularly important for heterogeneous service require-ments: lower-priority workloads may benefit from fractional GPU allocation, whereas higher-priority tiers require suffi-cient reserved capacity to maintain their SLOs under con-tention. Together with the network management layer, the compute layer establishes a coordinated resource envelope in which GPU, CPU and network capacity are allocated according to the requirements of each service tier. In InferSlice, this functionality is implemented using NVIDIA Run:ai (Run:ai) and KubeRay (Ray Project), which provide complementary resource management and distributed workload orchestration capabilities within Ku-bernetes. Each service tier is mapped to a Run:ai project defining its GPU and CPU resource allocations, quotas and scheduling priorities, as shown in Fig. 2. Run:ai schedules workloads against these resource constraints and supports fractional GPU allocation, allowing multiple inference work-loads to share the available accelerator pool while enforcing the resource boundaries of each tier. This enables, for exam-ple, the Free, Standard and Premium slices to use different GPU shares without requiring dedicated hardware for each tier. KubeRay manages the lifecycle of the distributed inference deployments that consume these resources. Declarative Ray-based deployment specifications are reconciled into the corresponding Kubernetes pods, which are subsequently scheduled within the compute resources assigned to their service tier. Replica counts are constrained by the available slice capacity, preventing scaling in one tier from consum-ing resources guaranteed to another. GPU and compute utilisation, scheduling state and workload status are exposed through the observability layer, providing the measurements required by the InferSlice control plane to monitor resource availability and, ultimately, support higher-level planning, optimisation and SLO-aware orchestration. 

3.5 Distributed Serving and Replica Management Layer 

The distributed serving and replica management layer acts as middleware between the inference workload and the vir-tualised resources managed beneath it. Where the orches-tration layer manages and schedules resources, this layer exposes them to the application through a distributed com-puting abstraction, so that the workload is expressed in application terms (e.g., models, replicas, and per-replica re-source requirements) rather than in terms of nodes, devices, and pods. It translates these application-level requirements into resource requests the orchestration layer can satisfy and coordinates the resulting distributed components at runtime. This separation is needed because inference workloads must scale and recover independently of where their resources are physically located. Without such a layer, each workload would need to be tied to specific infrastructure, preventing the same resource pool from being shared across tiers and applications. The layer therefore provides a distributed run-time for the inference workload and manages the number and lifecycle of its replicas, while remaining agnostic to the underlying infrastructure. In InferSlice, this functionality is implemented using Ray (Moritz et al., 2018), deployed on Kubernetes through KubeRay as described in Section 3.4. Ray provides a dis-tributed runtime in which a head node coordinates the clus-ter, worker nodes host the workload, and a shared object store supports data exchange between distributed compo-nents, as shown in Fig. 2. Each model deployment is de-scribed in a deployment graph and tracked in a model reg-istry, together with the resources each of its replicas requires. These application-level specifications are mapped to re-source requests submitted to the underlying Kubernetes and Run:ai layers, which place the corresponding pods within the compute slice of the tier. The Ray autoscaler adjusts the number of replicas of each deployment in response to serving demand, subject to minimum and maximum bounds and to the capacity of the tier’s compute slice, and issues the corresponding resource requests when replicas are added or removed. 

3.6 Inference Optimisation Layer 

The inference optimisation layer manages the lifecycle and execution of the model within each serving replica, from model loading and initialisation to GPU-resident execution and exposure through the inference API. Whereas the dis-tributed serving layer manages replicas as application-level units, this layer determines how the model is instantiated and operated within each replica, including model loading, GPU memory allocation, request scheduling, batching, and KV-cache management. It therefore provides the abstraction between a model deployment and the underlying accelerator, hiding device-level execution details from the serving and orchestration layers. This separation is important because the same model can require different execution strategies depending on available GPU memory, workload charac-teristics, concurrency, and SLO requirements. The layer InferSlice: SLO-driven LLM Inference over a Telco WAN 

converts a model from a deployment artefact into a running inference service while exposing a consistent interface to the distributed serving layer. In InferSlice, this functionality is implemented using vLLM (Kwon et al., 2023) as the inference runtime. For each replica, vLLM loads the model weights, initialises the model on the allocated GPU resources, manages GPU mem-ory during execution, and exposes an OpenAI-compatible inference interface to the serving layer. The runtime sched-uler coordinates concurrent requests through continuous batching, while the KV-cache manager allocates and reuses attention states during autoregressive generation. These mechanisms allow multiple requests to share the resources of a replica while efficiently managing GPU memory and compute across concurrent requests. The layer also provides the mechanisms required for distributed inference, includ-ing tensor, pipeline, and data parallelism and prefill/decode disaggregation (Zhong et al., 2024), allowing the model to be partitioned or replicated according to the configuration specified in its deployment. 

3.7 Control Plane 

The InferSlice control plane provides the decision-making layer that coordinates compute, network, serving, and in-ference resources to meet the SLOs of each service tier. Whereas the underlying layers execute resource allocation, replica management, and model inference, the control plane determines how these resources and execution mechanisms should be configured and where workloads should be served. It maintains a global view of workload demand, service re-quirements, resource availability, network conditions, and observed system performance, and uses this information to determine the serving configuration for each tier. This separation is essential in a distributed operator environ-ment, where compute and network resources span multiple sites and the configuration that satisfies an SLO can change as demand and infrastructure conditions evolve. The con-trol plane therefore provides the coordination mechanism through which local resource management is transformed into end-to-end, SLO-aware service orchestration. In InferSlice, the control plane comprises planning and optimisation, traffic forecasting, semantic routing, prompt caching, and distributed KV-cache management functions, as shown in Fig. 2. The planning and optimisation compo-nent determines the required serving configuration based on the SLOs and available resources, including replica counts, serving locations, resource allocations, and inference config-urations. Traffic forecasting provides estimates of future de-mand to support proactive scaling and placement decisions, while semantic routing directs requests towards replicas ac-cording to application and service requirements. Prompt caching and distributed KV-cache management further ex-ploit reuse across requests and replicas, reducing redundant computation and the cost of transferring state across the net-work. Together, these components provide the control logic required to coordinate the underlying compute, network, serving, and inference layers. The control plane operates over the state exposed by the observability layer and translates this state into configura-tion changes applied to the underlying infrastructure. Re-source utilisation, request rates, queueing behaviour, latency, throughput, network conditions, replica state, and KV-cache residency provide the feedback required to identify SLO violations and available capacity. The resulting decisions are then enforced through the compute orchestration, net-work slicing, distributed serving, and inference optimisation layers, closing the loop between observed system state and service configuration. This architecture separates policy and optimisation from resource enforcement, allowing the same control plane to reason over heterogeneous infrastructure and different inference configurations while leaving the un-derlying layers responsible for their respective execution mechanisms. 

3.8 Observability Layer 

The observability layer provides the measurements required to understand the state and performance of the distributed inference system across all infrastructure and service lay-ers. Whereas the control plane determines how the system should be configured, the observability layer provides the telemetry required to make those decisions based on cur-rent and historical system state. It therefore collects and aggregates metrics across compute, network, serving, and in-ference components, providing a consistent view of resource utilisation, workload behaviour, and SLO attainment. This cross-layer visibility is particularly important in a distributed environment, where performance degradation may originate from compute contention, network congestion, replica satu-ration, or inference-level effects such as KV-cache pressure. The layer consequently provides the measurement and feed-back mechanism that connects the execution layers with the control plane. In InferSlice, observability spans resource, network, serving, and inference telemetry, including GPU and CPU utilisa-tion, allocated resources, network throughput and latency, request rates, queueing, TTFT, TPOT, token throughput, replica state, and KV-cache utilisation and hit rate, as shown in Fig. 2. Telemetry is gathered by a combination of stan-dard and custom collectors. Prometheus scrapers collect metrics exposed by the Kubernetes, serving, and inference components, the Run:ai API provides GPU allocation and scheduling state for each tier, and custom scrapers collect measurements not available from standard exporters, such as network statistics and the request-level metrics reported by InferSlice: SLO-driven LLM Inference over a Telco WAN 

the traffic generator. Prometheus stores and aggregates these measurements as time series, and Grafana and a React fron-tend provide the interfaces through which they are queried and visualised. InferSlice’s React frontend is described in the Appendix Section B. The resulting telemetry supports both runtime monitoring and offline evaluation: current measurements allow resource pressure and SLO violations to be identified, while historical measurements support traffic forecasting, capacity planning, and analysis of the operating boundaries of different infer-ence configurations. A common observation layer across geographically distributed resources allows InferSlice to analyse end-to-end service behaviour, rather than treating compute, network, and inference performance as indepen-dent systems.

## 5 EVALUATION 

5.1 Service differentiation across slices 

We first evaluate whether InferSlice can expose distinct ser-vice tiers from a shared infrastructure by differentiating the resources allocated to each slice. The Free, Standard, and Premium slices are allocated a maximum of 1, 3, and 6 repli-cas respectively. We sweep the client max concurrency 

from 2 to 32 and measure P99 TTFT and SLO-compliant goodput, defined as the rate of requests whose TTFT meets the 500 ms SLO. A complementary experiment in Ap-pendix C.1 instead holds the resource allocation aside and differentiates slices through their concurrency policy. At low load (up to concurrency 12) the three slices are indis-tinguishable (Figure 5). P99 TTFT remains well below the SLO and goodput grows linearly with load to about 2.5 req/s, because every slice has sufficient capacity for the offered load. The slices diverge once the load exceeds their capacity. Free, limited to a single replica, breaches the SLO at concur-rency 16: P99 TTFT jumps from about 0.1 s to about 5 s and reaches about 13 s at concurrency 32. Its goodput peaks at 2.49 req/s (462 tok/s) and then collapses to below 0.1 req/s as requests queue behind the saturated replica. Standard, with up to three replicas, sustains the SLO for longer and breaches at concurrency 20, where P99 TTFT reaches about 2.8 s and rises to about 8 s at concurrency 32. Its goodput peaks at 3.18 req/s (840 tok/s) at the breach point and falls to about 0.1 req/s at concurrencies 28 and 32. Premium, with up to six replicas, keeps P99 TTFT below the SLO across the sweep, apart from a transient excursion at con-currency 16 (about 0.8 s) that we attribute to a replica cold start. Its goodput grows with load to 5.36 req/s (1,026 tok/s) at concurrency 32, where the sweep ends before saturation, so this value is a lower bound. At their peaks, Premium therefore delivers about 2.2 × the compliant goodput of Free and about 1.7 × that of Standard, while Standard delivers about 1.3 × that of Free. Beyond its capacity a slice does not degrade gracefully: queueing inflates TTFT for most requests and goodput falls sharply, whereas a slice with sufficient resources continues to scale. These breach points define the compute-limited operating regimes that Experiment N1 builds on (Section 4.3.3). 

5.2 Cold-Start Reduction 

During elastic scale-out, weight acquisition dominates the time required to deploy the pod. With the default loader, a cold deployment of the premium service takes approxi-mately 950 s from manifest apply to serve readiness, and pods added under load serve their first response after a median of roughly 560 s, yielding an end-to-end scale-out time of approximately 1,500 s. Replacing the default loader with the runai-streamer parallel filesystem reader over the same PVC cache reduces the cold deploy time to approx-imately 330 s, a reduction of about 65%, and the median per-pod time-to-first-response to roughly 285 s, approxi-mately halving the end-to-end scale-out time to approxi-mately 610 s. The improvement cannot be attributed to storage-cache warmth: hot-phase medians match the cold ones in both variants (roughly 580 s versus 560 s with the default loader, and approximately 290 s versus 285 s with runai-streamer), indicating that the loader, not the cache state, bounds weight acquisition. Newly added pods in fact reach readiness within approximately two minutes in both variants, so the observed differences arise almost entirely during weight loading and first-token production. These measurements explain the cold-start transient observed in the service-differentiation sweep (Section 5.1) and suggest that weight streaming can substantially shorten scale-out for latency-sensitive slices. Figure 7 also separates network-limited from compute- or queue-limited behaviour. At concurrency 16 and 32, Stan-dard and Free already violate the TTFT SLO with an effec-tively unconstrained 100 Gbps link (marginally for Standard at 16, by a wide margin otherwise), while their TPOT re-mains close to the Premium baseline. Their high TTFT therefore cannot be attributed primarily to the network and instead reflects queueing or compute-side saturation, which the 0.5 Mbps cap then compounds. Bandwidth effects can appear before the 0.5 Mbps floor when demand approaches the cap: Standard at concurrency 32 with a 1.5 Mbps cap (demand 1.44 Mbps) shows P99 TPOT rising from 41 to 136 ms and its transmit rate roughly halving, even though its TTFT is already dominated by queueing. Taken together, these results distinguish the network bottleneck from the previously observed queue boundary: queue saturation pre-dominantly increases TTFT, whereas severe bandwidth sat-uration reduces measured link and token throughput and increases both TTFT and TPOT by an order of magnitude.

## 6 DISCUSSION AND OPEN QUESTIONS 

6.1 Key Lessons 

6.1.1 L1: Communication Patterns Define the Operating Boundaries of Distributed Inference 

Table 7 summarises the resulting operating boundaries: which distributed inference techniques remain feasible as compute is spread across increasingly distant sites. We deem a technique feasible at a tier if it meets the 500 ms P99 TTFT SLO at a queue-free offered load that retains at least 50% of the co-located maximum queue-free rate. The first condition captures latency compliance and the second captures sustainable capacity. The reach of each technique is determined by its communication pattern rather than by available bandwidth alone. Tensor parallelism, which places an all-reduce on the critical path at every layer, does not remain feasible beyond a single subnet. Pipeline parallelism and prefill–decode disaggregation, which exchange activa-tions and KV-cache state respectively, avoid synchronous all-reduce semantics and therefore tolerate wider-area place-ment in principle. Within our measurements, pipeline par-allelism meets the latency SLO on every measured cross-PoP pair (median TTFT 85–364 ms, P99 <0.5 s, Table 5), but the load it can sustain falls with RTT: the maximum queue-free rate is 2.60, 1.55 and 0.85 requests/s at 9, 18 and 33 ms, which is 59%, 35% and 19% of the co-located 4.40 requests/s. Under the criterion above, PP is therefore feasible at the Mid tier but not at the Far and Distant tiers, where it satisfies the latency SLO only at a fraction of the co-located capacity. The picture is also load-regime de-pendent: under saturation, client-side queueing inflates PP TTFT to 7.2–23.1 s on the same pairs. Configurations span-ning more than one hop could not be initialised within our deployment budget. Prefill–decode disaggregation does not meet the SLO at any cross-PoP tier: its penalty is set by the KV-cache transfer and handover rather than by propaga-tion delay, and median TTFT stays within 4.2–4.4 s across 9–21 ms. Data parallelism, which introduces no inter-node communication on the critical path, is insensitive to place-ment, but requires the model to fit within a single node’s GPU memory and therefore does not generalise to models exceeding that bound. 

6.1.2 L2: Bottlenecks Shift with Load 

The resource that limits SLO attainment is not fixed: it changes with offered load, and the three regimes we ob-served leave distinct signatures in the latency metrics. At low load, the three tiers are indistinguishable and inference and scheduling dominate latency (Section 5). As load grows, each slice reaches the capacity set by its replica ceiling and becomes queue-limited: TTFT rises by an order of magni-tude while TPOT remains close to its unconstrained value (Tables 9 to 11). When the client-edge bandwidth cap is tightened, the slice becomes network-limited instead: mea-sured link and token throughput clamp, and both TTFT and TPOT increase by an order of magnitude. 6.1.3 L3: Cold Start Limits Elastic SLO Enforcement 

Elastic scale-out only protects an SLO if new replicas be-come ready before the queue builds. In our deployment, weight acquisition rather than scheduling bounds this delay: pods reach readiness within about two minutes under both loaders, so the remaining time is spent loading weights and producing the first token. Even with parallel weight stream-ing, which reduces end-to-end scale-out from approximately 1,500 s to 610 s, a replica added under load takes minutes to serve its first response, whereas the SLO breaches we measured develop within a single step of the concurrency sweep. The transient TTFT excursion of the Premium tier at concurrency 16 (Section 5) shows the same effect at small scale. Hot-phase scale-out matches the cold phase, so the delay is set by the loader and not by cache warmth, and keeping weights cached on the node does not remove it. Reactive autoscaling alone therefore cannot enforce latency SLOs under load growth, even when it is configured with generous replica ceilings. Capacity has to be provisioned ahead of demand, through traffic forecasting, warm standby replicas, or faster weight distribution, and the cold-start time of the largest model a tier serves sets the lead time that forecasting must achieve. Our measurements use an 8B model; the lead time will grow with model size, which makes this constraint more severe for the models that most need distributed serving. 

6.2 Scope and Limitations 

Our experiments use independent, short-lived, stateless chat requests drawn from ShareGPT, served on a homogeneous pool of NVIDIA L40S GPUs. Compute heterogeneity is emulated through Run:ai’s fractional GPU scheduler. We use Llama-3.1-8B-Instruct for service differentiation ex-periments and GPT-OSS-20B for distributed-inference ex-periments. The operating boundaries reported in Table 7 therefore characterise a specific workload class on a single accelerator generation, under the evaluated resource configu-rations. Agentic workflows introduce chained, stateful calls with growing contexts, shifting the optimisation objective towards task-level latency and making KV-cache locality an additional placement constraint. Longer-context work-loads may consequently alter these operating boundaries, particularly for prefill/decode disaggregation, where KV-cache transfer costs increase with the size of the context to be transferred. These scenarios, together with closed-loop control-plane adaptation to workload dynamics, remain out-side the scope of the present evaluation. Service tiers were realised through statically configured per-slice policies, so the evaluation shows that coordinated policy enforcement yields distinct and predictable regimes, but not that the control plane can select configuration and serving location dynamically under changing load; the plan-ning, forecasting and routing components of the architec-ture (Figure 2) were not evaluated in closed loop. Each distributed-inference technique was likewise evaluated in isolation, with network and compute dimensions varied one at a time (for example, rate-shaping inter-shard bandwidth at a fixed RTT), so interactions between techniques and be-tween simultaneous network and compute pressure are not captured. Finally, model-parallel results cover at most two shards, since four-shard configurations failed to initialise within our time budget. These limitations motivate the open questions that follow. 6.3 Open Questions 

6.3.1 Heterogeneity in compute and network 

Operator infrastructure is inherently heterogeneous: sites differ in accelerator generation, memory capacity and avail-able headroom, while WAN paths span more than an order of magnitude in latency and vary nearly as much in through-put (Table 4). Our results show that serving location is a first-order determinant of SLO attainment, making place-ment across heterogeneous sites a problem that existing serving systems have yet to address. We encourage the com-munity to explore placement and routing policies that treat compute capability and network conditions jointly: when a nearby but less capable site should be preferred over a dis-tant, better-provisioned one, how inference configurations and replica counts should be matched to mixed accelera-tors, and how heterogeneous replicas should interact with SLO-aware routing and autoscaling. Benchmarks that re-flect heterogeneous, operator-controlled infrastructure rather than uniform clusters would make this research comparable and reproducible. 

6.3.2 Composability 

Our results establish distinct operating boundaries for dis-tributed inference techniques over a WAN (Table 7): repli-cation is insensitive to placement, pipeline parallelism is feasible up to mid-tier latencies, and tensor parallelism is confined to a subnet. These boundaries suggest a hierarchy that matches each technique to the network tier it tolerates: tensor parallelism within a node, pipeline parallelism across nearby nodes, replication across sites, and prefill/decode disaggregation within each replica (Section 2). However, the feasibility of individual techniques does not establish the feasibility of their composition. How far such compo-sitions extend the feasible region, and how they interact, is a rich space for the community to explore. For exam-ple, replica-level autoscaling may amplify pipeline bubbles, while KV-cache transfer layered onto pipelined stages may introduce additional communication dependencies. A con-trol plane that selects compositions according to site capa-bilities, network conditions and SLOs, accounting for the interactions between techniques rather than optimising each independently, is a natural next step. 

6.3.3 Serving models that exceed single-node memory over the WAN 

Our measurements identify communication on the critical path as the principal barrier to scaling out over the WAN. Replication is insensitive to placement but requires the model to fit on each serving node. Tensor parallelism is confined to a single subnet by per-layer all-reduce, while pipeline parallelism meets the latency SLO across sites but sustains less load as RTT increases, and is limited by the burst latency of activation transfers rather than by aver-age bandwidth. Prefill/decode disaggregation encounters a different bottleneck: transferring 15–35 MB of KV-cache per request adds seconds to TTFT even though the transfer occupies only a small fraction of the available bandwidth, in-dicating that message-level latency, rather than throughput, constrains the handover. These findings point to concrete directions for inference systems research: overlapping com-munication with computation, compressing and shaping activation bursts, batching and pipelining KV-cache transfer descriptors, and relaxing synchrony through asynchronous or speculative execution. Progress here would allow opera-tors to serve models that exceed the memory of any single site, which is currently out of reach over a WAN. InferSlice: SLO-driven LLM Inference over a Telco WAN 

6.3.4 Stateful and agentic workloads 

Our experiments use stateless chat requests, whereas agen-tic applications chain stateful calls with growing contexts, shifting the service objective from per-request latency to task-level latency. In this setting, KV-cache locality be-comes a placement constraint: routing a follow-up call to a site that already holds the relevant cache may be prefer-able to selecting the site with the most spare capacity. Our disaggregation results show that moving 15–35 MB of KV-cache per request over the WAN adds seconds to TTFT (Section 5.2.1), so placement must account for cache resi-dency and transfer cost as well as network conditions. Open questions include how SLOs should capture task comple-tion time and tail latency across multi-step workflows, how routing should balance cache affinity against load and fail-ure recovery, and how distributed KV-cache management should support reuse, migration and eviction across sites as contexts grow. Exploring evaluation methodologies and serving policies designed for stateful, agentic workloads over geographically distributed, operator-controlled infras-tructure are key aspects for broader impact and adoption. 

6.3.5 SLO-aware placement and control 

The control plane of InferSlice includes planning and optimi-sation, traffic forecasting, semantic routing, prompt caching and distributed KV-cache components (Figure 2), whereas our evaluation exercises statically configured per-slice poli-cies. Our results show that admission limits, replica ceilings and serving location each shape SLO attainment, and that the binding resource changes with load. The central open problem is closing the loop between observation, prediction and resource control, which requires forecasting demand accurately enough to scale ahead of cold-start delays, identi-fying the binding resource online, and jointly selecting serv-ing locations, inference configurations and replica counts as compute and network conditions evolve, while preserving differentiated SLOs under load without over-provisioning. Energy, carbon and power availability add further objectives and constraints to this decision, as discussed next. We en-courage the community to explore closed-loop, cross-layer control that integrates these decisions, since it is the point at which the other open questions come together. 

6.3.6 Energy-aware AI grid 

The geographic distribution of inference infrastructure also raises fundamental questions about the energy efficiency of AI serving. Rather than concentrating compute in purpose-built, gigawatt-scale AI factories, operators could aggregate existing capacity across data centres, edge sites and other infrastructure into a geographically distributed AI grid, im-proving the utilisation of underused resources while placing workloads where compute capacity, network conditions and energy availability are favourable. However, the energy implications cannot be inferred from accelerator utilisation alone, since distributed inference introduces network energy costs, replication increases the number of active accelera-tors, and the communication and synchronisation overheads characterised in this work can offset the gains from better utilisation. The same SLO can therefore be met at very dif-ferent energy cost depending on the inference configuration and placement chosen, and powering down idle replicas must be weighed against the cold-start lead times that fore-casting has to achieve. This motivates extending SLO-aware placement to jointly optimise compute, network and energy, including energy-aware routing, workload migration and consolidation, and the trade-off between local utilisation and WAN communication. Carbon-aware orchestration adds a further dimension, be-cause sites draw on grids with different and time-varying carbon intensity, so flexible, throughput-oriented tiers could be deferred or shifted towards cleaner sites and periods, within the limits set by sovereignty, latency and the het-erogeneity of sites discussed above. Finally, power supply must be treated as a first-class resource alongside compute and network capacity. Telco sites were provisioned for net-work equipment rather than dense accelerators, so available power, cooling and backup capacity may bound the GPU capacity each site can host and sustain, and sites with lim-ited headroom may be less suitable than better-provisioned but more distant ones. Exploiting this requires power and energy telemetry to be exposed through the observability layer, so that the control plane can reason over latency, en-ergy, carbon and power availability when selecting serving locations, configurations and replica counts. We encourage the community to explore such energy- and carbon-aware or-chestration, and to report energy alongside SLO attainment in future evaluations. 

## 7 CONCLUSION 

This paper presented InferSlice, a reference architecture for SLO-driven LLM inference over a telco WAN. InferSlice integrates network slicing, GPU partitioning, elastic serving and end-to-end observability, extending service differentia-tion from network connectivity to AI inference itself. De-ployed across six PoPs of the JOINER testbed, on a shared network not dedicated to AI and without RDMA or spe-cialised interconnects between sites, it shows that operator-controlled infrastructure can meet sovereignty, latency, and resource-locality requirements. Service tiers yield distinct latency-throughput regimes over the same GPU pool, with peak SLO-compliant goodput of 2.2 × between the Premium and Free tiers, and parallel weight streaming reduces end-to-end scale-out time from approximately 1,500 s to 610 s. Our experiments also show that the binding resource shifts InferSlice: SLO-driven LLM Inference over a Telco WAN 

with load, with queue saturation and bandwidth saturation leaving distinct signatures in TTFT and TPOT, so that cross-layer observability is a prerequisite for SLO enforcement. Over the WAN, the reach of each distributed inference tech-nique is set by its communication pattern rather than by bandwidth alone. Replication is insensitive to placement but bounded by single-node memory, pipeline parallelism meets the 500 ms SLO on every measured cross-site link while sustaining progressively less load as RTT grows, and tensor parallelism and prefill/decode disaggregation are not feasible beyond a single subnet. In the case of disaggre-gation, the penalty is set by the KV-cache handover rather than by propagation delay or available bandwidth. These boundaries suggest that distributed and heterogeneous infer-ence systems should match each technique to the network tier it tolerates, and that serving models beyond the memory of a single site over a WAN remains an open problem. Our evaluation used statically configured policies, stateless chat workloads and a single accelerator generation, so closed-loop control, stateful and agentic workloads, heterogeneous accelerators and energy-aware placement are part of our fu-ture direction. We hope that the lessons and open questions presented here encourage further research on SLO-driven in-ference over geographically distributed, operator-controlled infrastructure.

## C EXTENDED EXPERIMENTAL EVALUATION 

C.1 Concurrency-policy differentiation 

Section 5.1 differentiates the slices through the resources allocated to each. This experiment complements it by dif-ferentiating configurations through their concurrency policy, that is, how many requests each configuration admits for execution at once, as shown by the target and maximum concurrency settings in Table 8. The three configurations, C1, C2, and C3, produced clearly separated operating points in both throughput and latency. C1 sustained approximately 698–699 tokens/s across all tested loads, while maintaining mean TTFT close to 100 ms and P99 TTFT below 140 ms. C2 increased throughput to 883–902 tokens/s, representing a 26–29% improvement over C1, while maintaining P99 TTFT below the 500 ms interactive serving threshold at all tested request rates. C3 prioritised capacity, sustaining 2,117–2,130 tokens/s, approximately 2.4 × higher than C2 and 3.0 × higher than C1. Importantly, throughput remained stable once each configuration reached saturation, indicat-ing that the available GPU resources were fully utilised and that additional load was absorbed through queuing rather than causing execution throughput degradation. Further-more, no request failures were observed in any experiment, demonstrating stable operation across all configurations. A clear latency-throughput trade-off emerges across the three configurations. C1 consistently delivered the lowest latency, with mean TTFT remaining between 99 and 101 ms and P99 TTFT between 130 and 138 ms regardless of load. C2 accepted a moderate increase in latency, with mean TTFT ranging from 176 to 184 ms and P99 TTFT between 313 and 472 ms, while still satisfying interactive-service re-quirements. In contrast, C3 achieved the highest throughput but exhibited significant tail-latency degradation under in-creasing load. While P99 TTFT was only 308 ms at 8 RPS, it increased to 1,305 ms at 12 RPS and reached 2,001 ms at 32 RPS, about four times the interactive-service thresh-old. Notably, TPOT remained largely constant within each configuration, varying by less than 4% across the evaluated load range. This indicates that model execution performance remained stable and that the observed latency degradation originated primarily from request admission and queuing effects rather than from reduced decoding efficiency. The observed behaviours reveal three practical service classes. C1 provides a latency-first regime suitable for highly interactive applications where responsiveness is the dominant requirement. More broadly, the results demonstrate that AI inference services can be differentiated in a manner analogous to telecom network services, allowing operators to offer multiple SLO-driven service tiers from the same pool of compute and network resources. This capability is fundamental for future telco AI platforms, where heterogeneous applications must coexist while receiving performance characteristics aligned with their specific operational requirements. 

C.2 Network Bottleneck Identification 

C.2.1 Experiment N1 results: End client bandwidth limiting 

At moderate load (concurrency 10), all three service tiers remain largely insensitive to bandwidth while the available link capacity exceeds approximately 1 Mbps (Table 9). Re-ducing the cap from 100,Gbps to 1 Mbps produces little change in throughput or TPOT: measured receive rates re-main around 0.9–0.94 Mbps and throughput around 370– 380 tok/s, while P99 TTFT remains below the 500 ms SLO. A clear network bottleneck emerges when the cap is reduced to 0.5 Mbps. At this point, the measured receive rate sat-urates at approximately 0.46 Mbps across all tiers, token throughput falls to 216–224 tok/s, and P99 TTFT increases from hundreds of milliseconds to 1.9–3.3 s. TPOT simulta-neously rises from approximately 26–38 ms to 150–276 ms. This sharp transition indicates a client-edge bandwidth floor between 0.5 and 1 Mbps for this workload: above the floor, inference and scheduling dominate latency; below it, net-work throughput becomes the binding resource and affects both first-token delivery and subsequent token generation. As concurrency increases, the bottleneck shifts, as shown in Tables 10 and 11. For Premium, the unconstrained traffic demand rises from approximately 0.91 Mbps at concurrency 10 to 1.32 Mbps at 16 and 2.45 Mbps at 32, and correspond-ingly the tier remains close to its unconstrained operating point under caps of 1, 1.5, and 2.5 Mbps, respectively. In every case, constraining the link to 0.5 Mbps forces the measured receive rate to approximately 0.45–0.47 Mbps and collapses throughput to roughly 221–231 tok/s; at con-currency 32, Premium P99 TTFT reaches 12.8 s and P99 TPOT 1.19 s (Table 11). Standard and Free, however, al-ready violate the TTFT SLO at higher concurrency even with an effectively unconstrained 100 Gbps link (Tables 10 and 11), while TPOT remains comparatively stable. Their high TTFT at these operating points therefore cannot be at-tributed primarily to network bandwidth and instead reflects queueing or compute-side saturation. Bandwidth restric-tion compounds this degradation once the 0.5 Mbps floor is reached. These results distinguish the network bottle-neck from the previously observed queue boundary: queue saturation predominantly increases TTFT, whereas severe bandwidth saturation simultaneously reduces measured net-work throughput and token throughput and substantially increases both TTFT and TPOT.
