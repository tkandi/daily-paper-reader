# Sustainable Processing-in-DRAM Integration and Replacement for Transformer Inference Servers 

Bipin Thapa Magar 

Department of Electrical and Computer Engineering University of Kentucky , Lexington, KY, USA bipin.thapa@uky.edu 

Ishan Thakkar 

Department of Electrical and Computer Engineering University of Kentucky , Lexington, KY, USA igthakkar@uky.edu 

Abstract —Processing-in-DRAM (PID) accelerators are emerg-ing as a sustainable alternative to GPUs for transformer model inference. They bring compute to data, mitigate the memory bandwidth limitation, and reduce the overall energy footprint. Yet, carbon analysis of PID accelerators is covered for only a limited set of hardware and workloads. In this work, we present a carbon modeling framework for PID-enabled inference servers that captures embodied carbon along with idle and active operational carbon. Our evaluation spans three GPU base-lines, six PID architectures, and eight transformer workloads. Additionally, we propose an aging-aware model that finds the carbon-optimal replacement period for PID accelerators over a 10-year horizon. Across evaluated configurations, our analysis shows that including PIDs in AI inference servers increases the total embodied carbon by at most 2.5%, while the total carbon is reduced by up to 1.14 × for encoder and 1.04 × for decoder transformer workloads. The results also depend on GPU selection for pairing, where introducing PID modules in high-end HGX-H100 servers incurs 5.4–5.9 × higher total carbon compared to A100 and H100 servers, due to their larger idle and embodied carbon. Introducing PID modules in higher-end GPU servers, compared to the A100-only baseline, increases (decreases) the to-tal carbon-delay product (CDP) for encoder workloads (decoder workloads). The aging-induced carbon-optimal PID replacement varies greatly with PID design and workload. Therefore, the sustainable deployment of PIDs requires a co-design of PID architecture, GPU pairing, and replacement policy. 

Index Terms —Carbon Analysis, Processing-in-DRAM, Sustain-able Computing, Aging Analysis 

I. I NTRODUCTION 

Transformer models have revolutionized the field of artifi-cial intelligence (AI) by achieving state-of-the-art performance across workloads, including text-only large language models, vision transformers, and multimodal encoders [1], [2]. As these models are actively deployed in data centers, the requirements go beyond computation alone. Recent reports estimate that data centers consume around 4.4% of total electricity in the U.S., which is forecasted to reach 6.7–12% by 2028 [3], [4]. As data centers grow, global CO 2 emissions from them are estimated to reach 720 million tons by 2030 [5]. Hence, minimizing carbon emissions from AI inference in data centers is urgent for sustainability [6]. Current AI inference largely runs on graphics processing unit (GPU) based servers. Although GPUs have growing compute density, they are limited by the memory wall [7], especially during the memory-bound autoregressive decod-ing phase of transformer inference [8]. Processing-in-DRAM (PID) accelerators address this problem by placing compute logic inside or near the DRAM array [9]–[18]. This results in lower data movement, which is a dominant factor in energy consumption during memory-bound execution. Prior PID works have proposed designs for both the compute-bound encoder [10], [11], [16] and the memory-bound decoder [12]– [14] phase of transformer inference. However, few studies have explored how deploying such PID accelerators in cloud-to-edge settings impacts sustainabil-ity and carbon efficiency. Current carbon modeling mainly focuses on CPU- and GPU-based systems [19]–[22], with only a few focusing on edge PID applications [23], [24]. Recent work has extended this modeling to PID-integrated servers [25], but it limits the study to a single GPU and a single workload, limiting the generalization of its findings. This leaves an open problem: whether the carbon benefits of PID integration hold across multiple GPUs and varying workloads with different parameter counts and modalities. To address this gap, this paper models embodied and operational carbon for six PID designs and three GPU base-lines, where the GPUs span single-GPU and multi-GPU server configurations. These PID–GPU server configurations are evaluated against eight transformer workloads, covering both encoder and decoder classes with language and vision modalities. We also perform aging-aware PID replacement analysis that evaluates the carbon-optimal PID replacement interval for the various PID architectures, GPU baselines, and workloads over a ten-year deployment horizon. To enable our analysis to reflect realistic upgrades rather than hypothetical designs, we added PID modules to GPU inference servers as drop-in installations of compute-enabled DRAM devices. Our contributions are as follows:  

> •

We present a unified carbon modeling framework for PID-enabled transformer inference servers that spans six PID designs, three GPU baselines, and eight representa-tive encoder and decoder workloads.  

> •

We quantify how PID integration changes total carbon and carbon-delay product relative to GPU-only servers, and show that GPU baseline choice dominates absolute emissions even when PID reduces operational carbon.  

> •

We evaluate the carbon-optimal replacement period for aging-aware analysis of PID subsystems and show how arXiv:2610.27345v1 [cs.AR] 19 Oct 2026 the optimal period depends on co-design of PID choices, GPU baselines, and workloads. II. B ACKGROUND 

A. Carbon Modeling for Computing Systems 

The carbon footprint of a system can be modeled in terms of two components: embodied and operational carbon. Embodied carbon captures the carbon emissions from the extraction of raw materials, device manufacturing, and transport, whereas operational carbon captures the carbon cost of operating the device. As technology advances, the carbon cost of fabrication is rising, causing embodied carbon to rival or even exceed operational carbon [26], [27]. This makes it necessary to look beyond energy efficiency toward overall carbon efficiency. Existing architectural carbon models estimate embodied carbon from device properties, including die area and process node, and combine them with fabrication carbon intensity and industry data. Operational carbon, on the other hand, is derived from application energy and the deployment grid’s carbon intensity [19]. These models have been further extended by introducing fixed-time and fixed-work assumptions [20], mod-eling 3D and 2.5D integration [28], and including accelerator subsystems [21], [22]. The operational carbon of AI inference servers is evaluated based on application energy, and these servers rarely run at peak utilization. During idle periods, the servers consume idle power, which is often comparable to active power for low-utilization workloads. Recent studies have therefore included idle carbon as a first-order term [4], [25], while others pri-oritize extending hardware lifetime over early replacement to amortize embodied carbon [29]. This study evaluates the effects of idle carbon and analyzes how to optimize the replacement period of the low-embodied-carbon PID devices .The performance drop of devices such as processors and DRAM as they age has been extensively studied [30], [31]. This aging-induced degradation has been shown to increase operational carbon in servers, as application time grows with degrading performance [23]–[25]. This analysis has also been extended to PID inference servers in both edge and cloud configurations, but these studies are largely limited to fixed hardware and workload configurations [23]–[25]. This mo-tivates examining the generalizability of the results across broader hardware and workload scenarios. 

B. Processing-in-DRAM for Transformer Inference 

Transformer inference has two phases with distinct hard-ware demands: encoder (prefill) and decoder (autoregressive). The encoder phase processes the entire input sequence at once as general matrix-matrix multiplication (GEMM) and is mainly compute-bound. GPUs efficiently handle these GEMM operations unless the sequence length is long enough to require multiple off-chip transfers [8]. The decoder phase, by contrast, generates one output token at a time and requires multiple weight and KV-cache fetches, making it memory-bound [8]. PID accelerators reduce this data movement by embedding compute logic within or near the DRAM array [9]–[18]. Encoder-oriented PID designs [9]–[11] compute the prefill phase directly inside DRAM. TransPIM [10] implements a token-based dataflow on high-bandwidth memory (HBM) by using bank-level units that handle reduction and softmax. PRIMATE [11] builds on TransPIM’s [10] dataflow and adds in-memory dynamic token pruning that discards low-relevance tokens by using HBM2-based top-k engines. ATRIA [9] is a bit-parallel stochastic accelerator for convolutional neural net-works, later adapted in [16] to support transformer encoders. Decoder-oriented PID designs focus on the memory-bound decoding phase. NeuPIMs [12] introduces co-execution be-tween a neural processing unit (NPU) and a PID, offload-ing attention to the PID subsystem while the NPU handles GEMM layers. AttAcc [13] implements general matrix-vector multiplication (GEMV) engines across the HBM hierarchy and performs softmax on the buffer die. AttenPIM [14] introduces configurable bank-level units to exploit multi-level parallelism. These PID architectures differ in terms of DRAM technol-ogy, compute logic, and GPU involvement. As these factors jointly affect the carbon footprint, the carbon profile of a PID-enabled server depends on the joint configuration of PID design, GPU, and workload. This motivates the evaluation space in Section IV. III. C ARBON MODELING FRAMEWORK

B. Server Configurations: Encoder and Decoder Topologies 

We now describe how PID and GPU accelerators are inte-grated into servers for encoder and decoder workloads. Fig. 1 summarizes a typical datacenter and the scope of our analysis. Each AI server includes the green components in Fig. 1, and we add a PID accelerator (blue) that enables efficient transformer inference alongside the GPU. In our setting, PID subsystems are compute-logic-modified DRAM modules that are installed in the memory slots of standard CPU/GPU servers, providing an upgrade solution without requiring a full system redesign. Datacenter-level infrastructure highlighted in orange is excluded from our carbon model. Each server executes one of two classes of transformer workloads: encoder or decoder. GPUs are well suited to the compute-bound encoder phase, but as sequence length increases, attention activations exceed on-chip memory capac-ity, forcing repeated transfers to and from off-chip DRAM. Several PID accelerators [9]–[11] have therefore been designed to accelerate encoder workloads directly in memory. In our configurations, encoder workloads are fully executed by the PID accelerator and the GPU remains idle throughout. Decoder workloads, by contrast, are memory bound at each autoregressive step. PID accelerators are well suited to this regime, but decoding still requires an initial encoder, or prefill, pass where GPU execution is beneficial. Accordingly, the GPU and PID accelerator [12]–[14] co-execute the decoder workload in sequence. The GPU completes the compute-bound prefill pass, after which the PID accelerator performs memory-bound autoregressive decoding. The two components are never active at the same time; co-execution here denotes this division of labor across phases rather than concurrent operation. The CPU plays the same role in encoder and decoder configurations. It does not run transformer computations, but instead handles orchestration, tokenization, and data schedul-ing between the GPU and PID subsystem. We therefore model the CPU as active with U = 0 .06 in all configurations.

E VALUATION AND RESULTS 

A. Evaluation Setup 

Baselines: We evaluate three GPU baselines: NVIDIA A100 SXM4 [35], NVIDIA H100 SXM5 [36], and NVIDIA HGX-H100 8 × [32], each paired with an Intel Xeon Max 9480 CPU [37] and 220 GB of main memory as the fixed host configuration. Against these baselines, we evaluate six PID accelerators: three encoder-oriented designs (ATRIA [9], Tran-sPIM [10], and PRIMATE [11]) and three decoder-oriented designs (NeuPIMs [12], AttAcc [13], and AttenPIM [14]). Each PID design is configured according to the specifications reported in its original publication, so that differences in our results reflect architectural choices made by the original authors rather than adjustments we introduced. However, we modify ATRIA [9] since it was originally designed for convo-lutional neural networks, and we scale it to a 64 GB configu-ration with an added softmax unit for supporting transformer inference. For NeuPIMs [12], we substitute the GPU baseline for the NPU used in its original design. 

Workloads: We evaluate eight transformer workloads: four encoder and four decoder. The workload set is chosen to span multiple modalities (language and vision) and a range of pa-rameter counts within each class. Encoder workloads include BERT, RoBERTa-PubMed, ViT-Base, and SigLIP-So400M, while decoder workloads include GPT-3-7B, Gemma-2-9B, Mistral-7B, and LLaMA-3-8B. All workloads use a batch size of 16 and are executed the same number of times for a fair comparison across architectures. C. Aging Analysis 

Fig. 5 examines total carbon as a function of PID re-placement period LT pid for encoder and decoder workloads. For each PID, the top row uses the highest-carbon work-load (RoBERTa-PubMed for encoder and Gemma-2-9B for decoder) and the bottom row uses the lowest-carbon workload (BERT for encoder and GPT-3-7B for decoder). The optimal replacement period differs across PID designs and GPU base-lines. For encoder-oriented PIDs under RoBERTa-PubMed, the optimal LT pid is 2, 4, and 10 years for ATRIA, TransPIM, and PRIMATE, respectively. For the lower-carbon BERT work-load, the optimal replacement period for all three designs is 10 years because reduced operational carbon incentivizes longer deployment periods. Across server configurations, the GPU choice has no impact on the optimal interval because GPUs are inactive in encoder servers and their total carbon contribution remains constant with LT pid .Decoder-oriented PIDs show similar trends, with GPU effects most visible for NeuPIMs. Under Gemma-2-9B, the optimal replacement period for NeuPIMs is 4 years with A100 and H100 and 10 years with HGX-H100. AttAcc and AttenPIM have a 10-year replacement period across all GPU baselines because their operational carbon is low relative to the embodied overhead. GPT-3-7B exhibits a pattern similar to that of encoder servers, where all replacement periods move to 10 years due to the reduced operational carbon. Most replacement periods remain long because the PIDs in the decoder phase share work with GPUs. Since only the PIDs are replaced, replacement is preferred only when the PID’s operational carbon is large relative to its embodied cost, as in NeuPIMs on Gemma-2-9B. Across all designs, HGX-H100 servers have the longest replacement intervals. This is because their large embodied and idle contributions dominate the total carbon and diminish the benefit of frequent PID replacement. This highlights the need to choose GPU baselines carefully for inference servers with longer lifetimes. V. C ONCLUSION 

This paper presented a carbon footprint analysis of PID-integrated transformer inference servers across three GPU baselines, six PID architectures, and eight workloads. PID subsystems add at most 2.5% embodied overhead yet reduce total carbon by up to 1.14 × compared with GPU-only config-urations. GPU choice is the dominant factor for total carbon, with HGX-H100 systems incurring up to 4.9 × higher CDP and 5.9 × higher total carbon than A100 or H100 systems. As GPUs become more powerful, the carbon-delay product advantage from encoder-oriented PIDs diminishes, whereas decoder-oriented PIDs still retain up to 6.5 × lower carbon-delay product than the GPU-only baseline under A100. Aging-aware analysis shows that the carbon-optimal PID replacement interval is shorter for PIDs with higher operational carbon, and longer for low-footprint designs such as PRIMATE. For decoder-oriented PIDs, the carbon-optimal replacement period is longer because the PIDs have lower operational carbon, as they split the work with GPUs. These patterns remain consistent across workloads and GPU generations. Sustainable PID deployment therefore requires a joint selection of PID architecture, GPU baseline, and replacement policy, where GPU choice remains the most important factor, followed by PID choice and lifetime tuning for carbon savings.
