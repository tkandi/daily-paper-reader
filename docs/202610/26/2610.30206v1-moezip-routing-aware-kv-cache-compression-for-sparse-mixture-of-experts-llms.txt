## Abstract 

Mixture-of-Experts (MoE) language models scale model capacity efficiently by activating a small subset of parameters per token. However, in long-context infer-ence, memory remains a major bottleneck because the KV cache grows linearly with sequence length. Existing KV cache compression methods reduce this cost for dense transformers, but overlook a problem specific to MoE: KV eviction can change which experts are selected during answer generation, degrading the generation quality. We propose MoEZip, a routing-aware KV cache compression for sparse MoE language models. To estimate the sensitivity of expert routing to perturbation, we propose a novel metric based on the Fisher information matrix of routing distributions. MoEZip combines this metric with attention weights for routing-aware scoring of contexts. Finally, we compose a complementary scoring system that combines eviction methods for dense and sparse-MoE architectures. Ex-periments show that MoEZip achieves robust performance under aggressive cache compression on various benchmarks. At a KV retention ratio of 10%, MoEZip improves over the SoTA baseline by +24 and +20 points on average on LongBench and RULER, corresponding to 2.3 × and 4.9 × higher scores, respectively. The source code is available at https://github.com/kmswin1/MoEZip. 

## 1 Introduction 

Large language models (LLMs) [5, 13] have enabled substantial advances across a wide range of real-world applications, including chatbot assistants [1, 3, 7], coding agents [22, 41], robot agents [17, 20], and broader LLM-based agent systems [30, 26, 37]. Mixture-of-Experts (MoE) architectures have emerged as an effective solution for scaling LLM capacity while controlling inference cost [10, 9, 24]. By activating only a small subset of parameters for each token, MoE models can scale total capacity without incurring the full computation cost of dense models. This favorable scaling property has made MoE a promising architecture for large-scale deployment [8, 31]. However, efficient inference remains challenging in long-context and agentic settings. Many real-world applications require processing long interaction histories [18, 45], retrieved documents [21], tool outputs [29], or multimodal observations [25, 44]. In these scenarios, the key bottleneck is often memory rather than activated parameter count: the KV cache grows linearly with sequence length and quickly becomes a major obstacle for scalable inference. To address this issue, a body of work recently studied KV cache compression and eviction methods, which reduce memory usage while preserving generation quality [23, 6, 43, 19, 40]. Existing approaches span diverse strategies, from attention-based token selection methods such as SnapKV [23] to methods based on context reconstruction such as KVZip [19]. Although these methods have shown

promising results on dense transformers, applying them to MoE models can be problematic. In sparse-MoE architectures, changes in KV representation due to eviction can affect router decisions that determine which experts are activated. This has been shown to aggravate performance degradation under high compression. KV compression can therefore incur MoE-specific problems, which are not captured by eviction methods designed for dense architectures. 

Contributions. In this paper, we consider KV cache compression for sparse MoE models from the perspective of routing fragility : it is defined as the sensitivity of routers due to perturbation induced by KV eviction. To estimate fragility, we propose a novel metric based on the Fisher information matrix of routing distributions. We derive the metric for two popular types of MoE routing: softmax-type and sigmoid-type routing. The metric is combined with the attention weights of instruction tokens to produce the fragility score that estimates the importance of context positions. Finally, we propose a scoring system that combines the importance scores from compression methods for dense and sparse-MoE architectures. Our complementary approach enables compression robust to perturbation on both context saliency and routing stability. Experiments show that MoEZip achieves robust performance on various benchmarks such as LongBench, NIAH, and RULER. MoEZip is especially strong at aggressive compression, e.g., at a KV retention ratio of 10%, MoEZip outperforms the current SoTA method, KVZip [19], by +24 and +20 points on average on LongBench and RULER, corresponding to 2.3 × and 4.9 × higher scores, respectively. Further analysis shows that MoEZip achieves good performance for both softmax-type and sigmoid-type routing models and is compatible with diverse compression methods originally developed for dense Transformer LLMs.

## 3 Method: Routing-Aware Prefix KV Compression 

Overview. The expert selection of future answer tokens is unavailable at KV eviction time. Thus, MoEZip uses instruction-token router states as a proxy for estimating routing fragility. Specifically, we measure the routing fragility of instruction tokens via the Fisher trace of the router distribution, and combine this fragility with instruction-to-context attention to score the routing-aware importance of each context position. Context positions are ranked by this score, and the top-scoring positions are reserved as routing-aware anchors . Within the per-(layer, head) KV retention budget B, MoEZip first allocates a portion to anchors and then lets a base scorer (e.g., KVZip [19], SnapKV [23]) select the remaining positions by contextual saliency. Thus, MoEZip changes only the composition of the retained cache while keeping the total cache size and decoding procedure unchanged. 

Router deviation under logit perturbations. To quantify the sensitivity of routing to eviction-induced perturbations, we consider the KL divergence 

D(p(·| z) ∥ p(·| z + δz )) (8) for perturbation δz on router logit z. The divergence captures how much the router distribution changes under a perturbation to its input logits. However, the perturbation δz is unavailable before KV selection, since it depends on which KV entries are removed. δz depends on which context positions are removed and how those removals cause representation shift propagating into the input router logits. Thus, (8) is not directly usable as a pre-selection criterion. Instead, we use a tractable direction-agnostic proxy based on the current router distribution. Query-agnostic variant. MoEZip-Recon replaces the downstream instruction with the same fixed reconstruction query used by KVZip and computes routing-aware scores from the router and attention statistics of that reconstruction pass. It requires no additional model forward pass beyond KVZip’s reconstruction pass, and the resulting compressed cache can be reused across downstream requests. The original MoEZip instead conditions anchor selection on the downstream instruction. 

Complexity. MoEZip adds only a lightweight selection-time computation over the base scorer, while preserving the same retained-cache size and decoding procedure after eviction. Its wall-clock overhead is small, adding 0.88s on Qasper and 0.86s on average across 4K–32K NIAH contexts; details are in Appendix D. 

## 4 Experiment 

4.1 Experimental Settings Baselines and Models. We compare MoEZip with KVZip [19], SnapKV [23], and PyramidKV [6]. We evaluate on Qwen3-30B-A3B-Instruct [34] and GLM-4.7-Flash [42], which use softmax and sigmoid routers, respectively. 

Evaluation Datasets. We evaluate on 12 LongBench tasks [4], RULER [15], and Needle in a Haystack (NIAH) [12] with context lengths from 4K to 32K. These benchmarks cover question answering, summarization, few-shot learning, synthetic retrieval, code completion, and diverse long-context tasks. We follow the official evaluation protocol for each benchmark and use the ROUGE-L F1 score for NIAH. 

KV Compression Setup. We evaluate keep ratios from 0.1 to 0.9, matching retained-KV budgets, model checkpoints, prompt templates, and decoding settings across methods. ## 5 Related Work 

KV Cache Compressions. Recent studies have proposed a variety of KV cache eviction and compression methods to reduce the memory and latency cost of long-context LLM inference. StreamingLLM [33] retains attention sinks and recent tokens to enable stable streaming inference, while H 2O [43] formulates KV eviction around heavy hitters that dominate attention. SnapKV [23] identifies prompt positions likely to be important for future decoding from a short observation window, and PyramidKV [6] exploits layer-wise differences in information concentration to assign non-uniform cache budgets across layers. AdaKV [11] extends this line of work by adaptively reallocating budgets across attention heads, and DuoAttention [32] distinguishes retrieval heads from streaming heads to combine full and lightweight KV retention. KVZip [19] departs from query-aware scoring and proposes query-agnostic compression via context reconstruction, improving cache reuse across multiple queries. However, these methods are primarily developed for dense LLMs. In MoE LLMs, KV eviction can change router outputs and destabilize expert assignments. MoEZip addresses this issue by explicitly preserving routing behavior during KV compression. 

Efficient LLM Architectures. Recent work has increasingly emphasized efficiency as a central design goal for long-context language models [22, 41]. Prior studies have explored this direction through alternative sequence modeling and efficient attention architectures, including linear attention methods such as DeltaNet [36], Gated DeltaNet [35], and Lightning Attention [27], as well as sparse attention methods such as BigBird [39], Native Sparse Attention [38], and DSA [8]. These methods improve efficiency by modifying the underlying architecture or attention pattern, reflecting a broader shift toward scalable long-context modeling. In contrast, our work is complementary: rather than redesigning the model architecture, we focus on KV cache compression at inference time, with particular emphasis on preserving routing behavior in sparse MoE LLMs. 

## 6 Conclusion, Limitations, and Broader Impacts 

In this paper, we revisited KV cache compression for MoE language models from the perspective of routing fragility. We showed that KV eviction can perturb expert routing and induce a MoE-specific source of quality degradation under aggressive compression. We proposed MoEZip, a routing-aware KV compression method that protects context positions important for stable expert routing. Across LongBench, NIAH, and RULER, MoEZip consistently outperforms strong baselines, especially in the high-compression regime. A limitation of MoEZip is that it requires access to router outputs and instruction-to-context attention statistics, which may not be exposed in black-box inference settings. As with other LLM efficiency methods, MoEZip may lower deployment costs for both beneficial applications and potential misuse. Overall, our results highlight routing fragility as an underexplored principle for KV compression in MoE models and suggest future opportunities for routing-aware compression in broader sparse architectures. 10 Acknowledgement

A.4 Instruction-token surrogate validation. 

The central design choice of MoEZip is to use instruction-token router states as a pre-generation surrogate for future answer-token routing fragility, since answer-token states are unavailable at eviction time. Table 5 validates this choice. We separate the load-bearing surrogate claim from supporting end-to-end evidence, since the two play different roles in justifying MoEZip.

## D Complexity 

Our method introduces only a modest additional overhead over the base scoring procedure. E.3 Layer-Wise Router Instability under KV Compression 

We further examine where routing instability emerges across MoE layers. For each MoE layer, we compare the top-1 expert selected with the full KV cache and with the compressed KV cache, and report the fraction of tokens whose expert assignment changes. As shown in Fig. 15 and Fig. 16, both KVZip and SnapKV exhibit a clear increase in router flip rate as compression becomes more aggressive. In addition, the instability is not uniform across depth: the flip rate is relatively low in early layers but becomes substantially higher in deeper layers, especially in the middle-to-late MoE layers. This suggests that routing perturbations induced by KV eviction are not limited to isolated shallow routing decisions, but can affect expert selection across a broad range of MoE layers. These results motivate incorporating routing-aware protection into KV compression. However, layer-wise flip variation alone does not imply that the routing-aware score should replace fine-grained layer/head-specific KV scoring. Instead, MoEZip uses the routing-aware score as a conservative position-level anchor signal: it selects a small set of context positions that are likely to be important for routing stability and protects them across layers. The remaining KV budget is still allocated by the layer/head-specific base scorer, allowing MoEZip to combine coarse routing-aware protection with fine-grained KV allocation. 

E.4 Router Flip Rate across Different Inputs for the Same Context 

We further analyze router instability across different inputs that share the same context. For each input, we compare the top-1 expert selected with the full KV cache and with the compressed KV cache, and report the input-wise router flip rate under the same compressed context. As shown in Fig. 17 and Fig. 18, both KVZip and SnapKV exhibit substantial within-context variation in router flip rates: some inputs remain relatively stable, while others induce much larger routing disruption under the same context and compression ratio. This indicates that routing instability is input-dependent and cannot be fully explained by context-side saliency alone. This supports the need for instruction-conditioned refinement. A context-only score assigns the same importance to context positions regardless of the downstream input, but the routing-critical positions to preserve may vary across inputs. MoEZip therefore estimates routing fragility from the instruction tokens and uses instruction-to-context attention to identify input-specific routing-aware anchors.

The original MoEZip instead conditions anchors on the downstream instruction. At keep ratio 0.1, MoEZip-Recon improves over KVZip by 22 .44 points and remains 2.94 points below instruction-conditioned MoEZip. It retains approximately 88% of MoEZip’s improvement over KVZip; the corresponding fraction at keep ratio 0.2 is 78% . Thus, substantial routing-aware gains persist without downstream query conditioning, while instruction conditioning provides an additional quality benefit. The reuse comparison concerns the final compressed context cache; it does not claim identical end-to-end costs for the two deployment modes. 

E.7 Global versus Fine-Grained Anchor Selection 

We compare global anchors with per-layer and per-GQA selection on Qwen3-30B-A3B-Instruct-2507 at keep ratio 0.1. For the fine-grained variants, local budgets are normalized so that the total number of retained KV elements matches the global method. All variants use the same retention budget B

and anchor budget Bf for each layer–head cache, differing only in how anchor positions are scored and selected. Global selection exceeds per-layer and per-GQA selection by 8.6 and 5.6 macro points, respectively. It wins on all 12 tasks against the per-layer variant and on 11 of 12 tasks against the per-GQA variant. These results support global sharing among the evaluated alternatives, but do not establish optimality among all joint cross-layer strategies. MoEZip shares only routing-aware anchor positions; the remaining budget is filled independently by the base compressor at each layer and head.
