Abstract 

Micro-expert pruning reduces the capacity of mixture-of-experts (MoE) models by removing coupled channels within routed experts. How-ever, finer pruning granularity alone does not guarantee the preservation of downstream capa-bilities, as similar language-modeling perplex-ity can accompany substantially different task performance. We revisit micro-expert mask construction as two distinct decisions: allocat-ing retained capacity across experts and select-ing channels within that capacity. We propose 

UC-RAMP , a two-stage post-training prun-ing framework that explicitly separates these decisions. CAPE uses cross-domain calibra-tion evidence to redistribute expert widths un-der an exact layer-wise budget, while RAMP refines channel composition at fixed widths through one-out, one-in exchanges guided by a channel-interaction surrogate. Across three MoE models at 25% and 50% routed-expert channel pruning, UC-RAMP achieves the high-est ten-task mean accuracy among evaluated pruning methods in five of six settings. At 50% pruning, it outperforms the strongest eval-uated baseline by 8.19, 3.96, and 6.14 per-centage points across the three models, respec-tively. In a single-A100 deployment at 25% pruning, UC-RAMP reduces measured time to first token by 19.7–23.9% and time per out-put token by 20.2–22.3% relative to the un-pruned model. The code is available at https: //github.com/xzktx003/UC_RAMP .

1 Introduction 

Sparse mixture-of-experts (MoE) models reduce per-token computation by routing each input to only a subset of experts while retaining a substan-tially larger parameter pool (Shazeer et al., 2017; Fedus et al., 2022). However, sparse activation does not eliminate the storage and deployment

costs of the full expert pool. Post-training prun-ing can reduce these costs without retraining the model from scratch. Existing pruning methods operate at different granularities, ranging from in-dividual weights (Frantar and Alistarh, 2023; Sun et al., 2024) and coupled model dimensions (Ma et al., 2023; Ashkboos et al., 2024) to entire experts (Lu et al., 2024; Lasby et al., 2026). While whole-expert pruning removes all channels of a selected expert, micro-expert pruning instead removes cor-responding gate and up-projection rows together with a down-projection column (Xu et al., 2026), allowing expert capacity to be reduced without nec-essarily removing the entire expert. Channel-level pruning offers greater flexibility in constructing structural masks, but finer granu-larity alone does not guarantee the preservation of downstream capabilities. Figure 1 illustrates this limitation on Qwen3-30B-A3B-Instruct-2507. At 50% routed-expert intermediate-channel pruning, CAMERA’s WikiText-2 perplexity remains close to that of the dense model (7.61 versus 7.26), whereas MATH500 accuracy drops from 80.6% to 10.2% and HumanEval pass@1 from 95.7% to 0%. In contrast, UC-RAMP achieves substantially higher task accuracy under the same pruning budget, while exhibiting similarly low perplexity. These obser-vations highlight the importance of mask construc-tion beyond pruning granularity alone, motivating a closer examination of how individual channel scores are translated into a structural pruning mask. A micro-expert pruning mask encodes two dis-tinct structural decisions: capacity allocation ,which determines the number of channels retained in each expert, and channel composition , which specifies the identities of those channels. Cross-domain response statistics can guide expert-capacity allocation, whereas interactions among channels provide complementary information for evaluating channel sets at fixed widths. While prior methods such as MoE-Slimming consider nonuni-form expert widths (Ding et al., 2026), our focus is on explicitly separating capacity allocation from channel composition, allowing each decision to be guided by its corresponding evidence. To operationalize this separation, we intro-duce UC-RAMP, a two-stage micro-expert pruning framework consisting of Capability-Aware Pruning via Envelopes (CAPE) and Reconstruction-Aware Micro-expert Pruning (RAMP). In the first stage, CAPE establishes a shared channel ranking from cross-domain calibration statistics and evaluates each expert on a common reference channel set. An expert-level multiplier then adjusts the competition for retained capacity, enabling width redistribution under an exact layer-wise budget while preserving the channel ranking within each expert. In the sec-ond stage, RAMP freezes the allocated widths and incorporates pairwise channel interactions into a reconstruction-based surrogate. It then refines the retained channel sets through one-out, one-in ex-changes that reduce the surrogate without changing expert widths. Both stages operate without gradient updates or modifications to the router. Our main contributions are summarized as fol-lows: • We formulate micro-expert pruning as two distinct structural decisions: allocating re-tained capacity across experts and selecting channels within the allocated capacity. This formulation makes explicit the two decisions jointly determined by conventional global channel ranking. • We propose UC-RAMP, a two-stage post-training pruning framework. CAPE uses cross-domain calibration evidence to redis-tribute expert capacity under an exact channel budget, while RAMP incorporates channel in-teractions to refine retained channel sets with-out changing expert widths. • We evaluate UC-RAMP across three MoE models and two pruning ratios. UC-RAMP achieves the highest mean accuracy among the evaluated pruning methods in five of six settings, including all three models at 50% pruning. Component ablations characterize the contributions of the two stages, and de-ployment measurements demonstrate infer-ence latency reductions with nonuniform ex-pert widths. 

2 Related Work 

Post-training and whole-expert pruning. Post-training pruning has been widely studied for reduc-ing the storage and computational costs of large language models. SparseGPT (Frantar and Al-istarh, 2023) and Wanda (Sun et al., 2024) per-form weight-level pruning using reconstruction in-formation and weight-activation saliency, respec-tively, while LLM-Pruner (Ma et al., 2023) and SliceGPT (Ashkboos et al., 2024) remove coupled model dimensions. For MoE models, another line of work focuses on expert-level compression. Lu 2et al. (2024) investigate expert pruning and skip-ping, REAP (Lasby et al., 2026) evaluates routed expert contributions, and DiEP (Bai et al., 2025) optimizes nonuniform expert pruning across lay-ers. More recently, AIMER (Liu et al., 2026a) employs calibration-free weight statistics, whereas MAN/MSAN (Liu et al., 2026b) leverages routed-token activation statistics. These approaches pri-marily operate at the granularity of complete ex-perts, in contrast to micro-expert pruning, which allows capacity to be reduced within individual experts. 

Expert-internal pruning and dependent chan-nel selection. Finer-grained MoE compression re-tains greater flexibility in selecting expert-internal structures. MoE-Pruner (Xie et al., 2024) incor-porates routing information into expert-internal parameter pruning, while CAMERA (Xu et al., 2026) introduces micro-experts as coupled chan-nels across the gate, up, and down projections of SwiGLU experts. MoE-Slimming (Ding et al., 2026) further considers nonuniform expert widths through attribution-guided and coverage-based structural compression. Beyond independent im-portance estimates, The Sparsity Whisperer (Kong et al., 2026) highlights the importance of depen-dencies among jointly pruned units. Building on this line of research, UC-RAMP explicitly sepa-rates expert-capacity allocation from fixed-width channel composition. CAPE uses cross-domain re-sponses evaluated on shared reference channel sets to redistribute expert widths under an exact layer-wise budget, whereas RAMP incorporates pairwise channel interactions to refine channel membership without changing the allocated widths.

4 Experiments 

4.1 Models, Budgets, and Evaluation 

We evaluate UC-RAMP on Qwen3-30B-A3B-Instruct-2507, Gemma-4-26B-A4B-it, and Qwen3.6-35B-A3B at pruning ratios of 25% and 50%. These ratios refer exclusively to the intermediate channels of routed experts, excluding attention, embeddings, routers, shared experts, Gemma’s parallel dense MLP, and multimodal modules. We distinguish channel-level and expert-level pruning baselines in Table 1, as identical nominal pruning ratios can lead to different structural configurations and runtime characteristics. UC-RAMP uses four calibration domains, each consisting of sequences of length 2048: 128 WikiText-2 training examples for general language (Merity et al., 2017); 64 GSM8K and 64 MathQA training examples for general reasoning (Cobbe et al., 2021; Amini et al., 2019); 128 competition-MATH training examples for advanced mathemat-ics (Hendrycks et al., 2021); and 64 MBPP training examples for code (Austin et al., 2021). Calibration requires only forward-pass statistics, without gradi-ent updates. We set the general-domain weight to 

λ = 0 .9, distributing the remaining weight equally among the three specialized domains. The other hyperparameters are fixed at α = 0 .9 in Eq. (4) and β = 0 .2 in Eq. (12). Details of the width trust-region constraints are provided in Appendix A. Our channel-level baselines include CAMERA (Xu et al., 2026) and MoE-Slimming (Ding et al., 2026), while the whole-expert baselines comprise AIMER (Liu et al., 2026a), REAP (Lasby et al., 2026), and MAN/MSAN (Liu et al., 2026b). We also report results for the unpruned models. The baseline results retain the respective scoring pro-cedures and calibration settings of each method. Table 2 further disentangles the contributions of CAPE’s capacity redistribution and RAMP’s fixed-width channel refinement. All evaluations are conducted using EvalScope 1.4.1 with temperature set to 0, a maximum gen-eration length of 2048 tokens, and a fixed ran-dom seed of 1234. We report WikiText-2 per-plexity separately from accuracy on ten down-stream benchmarks: PIQA (Bisk et al., 2020), ARC-Easy and ARC-Challenge (Clark et al., 2018), GSM8K (Cobbe et al., 2021), HellaSwag (Zellers et al., 2019), WinoGrande (Sakaguchi et al., 2020), RACE (Lai et al., 2017), GPQA-Diamond (Rein et al., 2024), MATH500 (Hendrycks et al., 2021; Lightman et al., 2024), and HumanEval pass@1 (Chen et al., 2021). The average accuracy reported in Tables 1 and 2 is the unweighted mean across the ten benchmarks, excluding perplexity. Detailed per-task results are provided in Appendix C. 

4.2 Results across models and budgets Overall Performance. UC-RAMP achieves the highest ten-task mean accuracy among pruned models in five of the six settings in Table 1, in-cluding all three models at 50% pruning. At this ratio, it reaches 75.43% on Qwen3-30B-A3B, 67.88% on Gemma-4-26B, and 74.23% on Qwen3.6-35B-A3B. These scores exceed the re-spective strongest baselines, MSAN (67.24%), MoE-Slimming (63.92%), and MAN (68.09%), by 8.19, 3.96, and 6.14 percentage points.

WikiText-2 per-plexities remain similar: 7.68 for UC-RAMP and 7.61 for CAMERA, compared with 7.26 for the unpruned model. These task-level gains at similar perplexity address the capability loss highlighted in Figure 1. 

Performance Across Architectures. The advan-tages at 50% pruning extend to Gemma-4 and Qwen3.6, although recovery varies across indi-vidual tasks and architectures. 4.3 Ablation Studies 4.3.1 Component Ablation 

Table 2 isolates expert-capacity redistribution and fixed-width channel refinement while retaining multi-domain scoring. At 50% pruning, adding CAPE’s expert multipliers and capacity redistribu-tion raises mean accuracy from 73.11% to 75.19% on Qwen3 and from 65.51% to 67.60% on Gemma, gains of 2.08 and 2.09 percentage points. RAMP then improves the means to 75.43% and 67.88% with these expert widths held fixed. Appendix D reports the other four CAPE–UC-RAMP compar-isons. These results distinguish the contributions of the two structural decisions. The multi-domain Top-K baseline already provides a channel rank-ing, while CAPE additionally changes how the retained-channel budget is distributed across ex-perts. RAMP operates within these allocated widths and provides a smaller incremental gain through interaction-aware set refinement. The larger improvement from capacity redistribution in both settings suggests that expert width alloca-tion is an important factor in constructing effective micro-expert masks. 

4.3.2 Hyperparameter Sensitivity 

We examine the three hyperparameters on Qwen3-30B-A3B-Instruct-2507 at 50% pruning (Figure 3). The α and λ sweeps evaluate CAPE only, while the 

β sweep adds RAMP to the CAPE checkpoint. The main experiments use the fixed defaults α = 0 .9,

λ = 0 .9, and β = 0 .2.Balancing activation energy and peak response yields similar mean accuracy across α ∈ [0 .6, 0.9] ,ranging from 75.178% to 75.268%. Latency measurement. The five input-length/batch-size pairs are 256/1, 512/1, 1024/1, 1024/2, and 1024/4. Each request generates exactly 32 tokens with temperature 0 and EOS ignored. Each configuration is tested in two rounds, with one warmup request followed by three timed requests per model in each round. The second round reverses model startup order; the reported values are medians pooled across both rounds. 

B Properties of the two stages 

Exact solution of the capacity proxy. For a fixed width ke and positive ρe, a channel set omitting a higher-scoring channel in favor of a lower-scoring one cannot maximize Eq. (7). Hence an optimum exists in which each expert retains an anchor-score prefix. The lower-bound prefixes are mandatory. After retaining them, each eligible additional po-sition has marginal value ρeAe, (j). Choosing the globally largest remaining marginals until the exact layer budget is reached maximizes the objective; upper-bound positions are excluded from the can-didate pool. A zero multiplier gives equal marginal values for that expert, resolved by a fixed tie-break.
