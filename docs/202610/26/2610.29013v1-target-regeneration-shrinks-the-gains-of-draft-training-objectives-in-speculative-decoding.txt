Abstract 

Speculative decoding accelerates large lan-guage models by verifying drafted tokens in parallel. New draft-training objectives are usu-ally credited with the acceptance they add over cross-entropy on externally written responses, yet many pipelines let the target rewrite them. We ask how much of that gain survives. With a vision-language target and a block drafter, we train three objectives on four versions of one corpus: cross-entropy on corpus tokens, token-level distillation from the target, and distillation gated to positions that would sur-vive verification. Distillation and the gate add large gains on external responses, but after greedy regeneration, distillation adds a smaller gain and the gate little or none. The shrinkage persists across data orders, posi-tion weights, verifiers, decoding temperatures and, for distillation, initializations and learn-ing rates. Most of it remains at matched su-pervised length and at each objective’s best measured training length. A second vision cor-pus shows the shrinkage, as does a text-only replication, for distillation also with an autore-gressive drafter. Objective gains should there-fore be measured against a target-regenerated baseline and across training lengths. Our code is available at https://github.com/ js-lee-AI/TargetRegeneration .

1 Introduction 

Large language models (LLMs) and the vision-language models built on them (Yang et al., 2025; Bai et al., 2025) generate text one token at a time (Kang et al., 2026; Lee et al., 2026f,c), and each token takes a forward pass through the full model (Lee et al., 2026e). Speculative decoding reduces the number of these sequential passes by letting a small draft model propose several tokens that the target model verifies in one parallel pass (Leviathan et al., 2023; Chen et al., 2023). Recent drafters condition on the target’s hidden states, pre-dict several future tokens at once, or both (Cai et al., 2024; Li et al., 2025; An et al., 2026a; Chen et al., 2026). Beyond the architecture, other work designs the training objective, either distilling the target’s token distributions into the drafter or giving more weight to positions that are more likely to survive verification (Zhou et al., 2024; An et al., 2026b; Chen et al., 2026). A new objective is often credited with the ac-ceptance it adds over cross-entropy on a fixed cor-pus whose responses were written by other mod-els (Li et al., 2024b; Zhang et al., 2025). How-ever, many draft-training pipelines replace those responses with responses generated by the target 1arXiv:2610.29013v1 [cs.CL] 21 Oct 2026 itself (Cai et al., 2024; Goel et al., 2024; Zafrir et al., 2025; Ganesan et al., 2025; Chen et al., 2026). Such regeneration is sequence-level dis-tillation from the target (Kim and Rush, 2016). As Figure 1 illustrates, the corpus token matches the target’s own greedy prediction far more often in regenerated responses than in external ones. Where the two match, cross-entropy on the corpus already trains the drafter toward the target’s prediction, and token-level distillation can add only the probability the target assigns to other tokens. Consequently, we expect part of an objective’s gain to disappear after regeneration. If most of it disappears, a gain measured on external responses overstates what the objective adds in a pipeline that regenerates its responses. To test this expectation, we measure how an objective’s gain depends on who wrote the train-ing responses. With Qwen3-VL-8B as the tar-get and a DFlash-style block drafter, we compare cross-entropy, token-level distillation and the same distillation behind a gate that keeps only posi-tions whose preceding corpus tokens would sur-vive greedy verification. Each objective is trained on one image-grounded corpus with its original external responses and with responses the target regenerates greedily, and on a second corpus in the same two versions. Because the regenerated responses are also longer on average, we add two regenerated versions of the first corpus matched to the original supervised length, one that cuts each response and one that masks the loss beyond that length. Controls on the first corpus vary the initial-ization, the data order, the position weight, the form of the gate, the learning rate, the verifier and the decoding temperature. A horizon study varies the training length, and a text-only replication changes the target, the corpus and the verifier and adds an autoregressive drafter. We find that the response source changes how much each objective appears to add. Distillation and the gate add large gains on external responses, but greedy regeneration removes most of both gains, leaving distillation a small gain and the gate little or none. The controls change the size of this shrinkage but not its direction. Most of the distil-lation shrinkage remains in both matched-length versions. Only the cut version leaves the gate a small gain, because cutting the responses instead of masking their loss lowers acceptance under the ungated objectives but not under the gated one. On external responses, cross-entropy loses more ac-ceptance than distillation as training lengthens, so the distillation gain grows. At each objective’s best measured training length, however, distillation adds much less on regenerated responses than on exter-nal ones. A second vision corpus shows the same shrinkage, and the text replication shows it for dis-tillation and the gate under greedy and sampled decoding and for distillation with an autoregressive drafter under greedy decoding. A label control supports the reasoning behind our expectation. On external responses, cross-entropy on the target’s greedy tokens in place of the cor-pus tokens already yields most of the distillation gain. Our results suggest two practices for report-ing objective gains. First, the gain of a new ob-jective meant for pipelines that regenerate their responses should also be measured against cross-entropy on regenerated responses. Second, gains on external responses should be compared across training lengths.

A slot that follows a corpus token other than the target’s greedy token trains a predic-tion that greedy verification never uses, because the target does not produce that prefix. The gate is a hard counterpart of the survival weighting in PARD-2, which multiplies the target’s confidence in the preceding corpus tokens (An et al., 2026b). 

2.2 Response treatments 

All four treatments keep the images and prompts of the training corpus and change only the responses, as Figure 2 illustrates for one example. They are (i) External , the original corpus responses, written by a model other than the target; (ii) Full regen-eration , the target’s greedy response to the same image and prompt, with at most 768 new tokens; (iii) Truncated , the regenerated response cut to the token count of the external response, after which the assistant turn is closed; and (iv) Loss window ,the full regenerated response as input, with the loss restricted to the same number of leading response tokens as the external response. Because regenerated responses are longer on av-erage, both matched-length treatments supervise only as many response tokens as the external cor-pus. They differ at the cut, where truncation closes the turn and the loss window continues the response without supervision. The two therefore also differ in the anchors available near the cut and in batch padding. Neither isolates the effect of length, and we report both. For the analysis of Section 5, a fifth version replaces greedy regeneration with sampling at temperature 1, and a sixth samples at temperature 0.7, the default of the target’s generation configura-tion. 

2.3 Measurement 

We report mean accepted tokens ( MAT ), the number of tokens a verification round advances, counting the accepted draft tokens and the to-ken the target adds itself, averaged over rounds. The increment of one objective over another is the ratio of their MATs minus one, for example 

MAT( KD )/MAT( CE ) − 1 on the same responses. To compare an increment across treatments, we use the ratio of ratios 

θ = MAT r(KD )/MAT r(CE )MAT e(KD )/MAT e(CE ) − 1, (3) where r is a regenerated treatment and e the ex-ternal one. A negative θ means that KD adds less after regeneration. For the gate, KD + gate over KD replaces KD over CE.

5 Why the Increments Shrink 

The corpus already carries the target’s greedy token. The corpus columns of Table 1a mea-sure how often the corpus token equals the target’s greedy token. On ALLaVA, this agreement rises from 58.0% on external responses to 95.2% after full regeneration, and on ShareGPT4V from 64.7% to 94.9%. With greedily regenerated responses, CE already trains toward the target’s greedy prediction at nearly every slot, KD can add only the probabil-ity mass away from that token, and the gate has few continuations left to remove. Agreement separates the external versions from the target-written ones, but it does not order the versions within each group by their KD gains. Training CE and KD on three target samples of every prompt at temperature 1 for the same number of updates removes most of KD’s gain on sampled responses, so more target samples also supply most of that gain. Appendix E defines agreement exactly and compares it across versions, and Appendix C reports the three-sample cells. 

The gains fall with the share the target wrote. 

We also train each objective, twice, on mixtures that replace a random quarter, half or three quarters of the external ALLaVA responses with their greedy regenerations, keeping the prompts, their order and the training length of the external cells. As Figure 5 shows, both gains decline as the regenerated share grows, while agreement rises. Most of the KD decline comes with the first quarter: regenerating a quarter of the responses already covers about two thirds of the drop from external to full regeneration, so a KD gain measured on such a mixture is closer to the fully regenerated gain than to the external one. Appendix D lists every mixture. 

Greedy labels carry the part of the external gain that regeneration removes. A label control on ALLaVA separates two parts of what KD adds. Training on the target’s greedy token in place of the corpus token at every position, with the corpus prefix unchanged, raises MAT by 9.3% over CE on external responses, and KD on the same pre-fixes adds a further 4.7%. On fully regenerated responses the same control adds no measurable gain over CE, and KD adds 3.9% over it, close to the 3.7% that KD keeps over CE. In the vision study, regeneration thus removes the greedy-label part of the external KD gain and leaves most of what KD adds beyond the target’s greedy token. In the text replication under greedy decoding, the same control matches KD on external responses and adds only 0.3% over CE on regenerated ones. Appendix B lists the control for each domain and Appendix G for each task. 

The remaining KD gain lies beyond the drafter’s first choice. On external ALLaVA responses KD mostly corrects the drafter’s first choice, whereas after greedy regeneration most of its gain lies in how the other tree candidates are ranked. Ap-pendix D shows this by scoring each drafter on the target’s greedy text. 

Full regeneration outweighs the objective set-tings we tried. From the released drafter’s 2.835, three epochs of CE on external ALLaVA responses end 1.4% higher, whereas every regenerated cell of Table 1a ends at least 22.9% higher. Fully regen-erated CE also exceeds every objective setting we trained on external ALLaVA responses. It is 8.5% above the best of these, KD with the soft survival weighting of PARD-2 and γ = 2 . Table 2 and Appendix F list the settings. 

6 Related Work 

Drafters and objectives. Work on speculative decoding (Leviathan et al., 2023; Chen et al., 2023) has proposed both drafter architectures and training objectives. Autoregressive drafters predict one to-ken at a time (Li et al., 2024a,b, 2025), and parallel drafters predict several future tokens in one for-ward pass (Cai et al., 2024; An et al., 2026a; Chen et al., 2026; Lee and Eo, 2026; Lee et al., 2026d). Distillation objectives train the drafter to match the target’s token distributions (Liu et al., 2024; Zhou et al., 2024; Goel et al., 2024; Zhang et al., 2025). Other objectives give less weight to the positions that follow a corpus token the target or the drafter is unlikely to produce. PARD-2 weights tokens by 7the cumulative target confidence of the preceding corpus tokens (An et al., 2026b), and GRIFFIN masks the loss after the first corpus token outside the drafter’s own top-k predictions (Hu et al., 2025). Our gate likewise masks the loss after the first cor-pus token that is not the target’s greedy token, so it is a hard counterpart of the PARD-2 weighting. We also test the PARD-2 weighting itself in the control with γ = 2 .

Who writes the training responses. Training drafters on target-generated responses is estab-lished practice (Cai et al., 2024; Goel et al., 2024; Zafrir et al., 2025; Ganesan et al., 2025), and DFlash, the drafter family used in both of our stud-ies, regenerates its training responses with the tar-get (Chen et al., 2026). On such responses, Hong et al. (2025) find that distilling the target’s token dis-tributions gives drafters for domain-specific targets a 2% to 10% higher acceptance rate than cross-entropy on the same responses. Other drafters are trained and compared on fixed responses. EA-GLE uses a fixed corpus to avoid the cost of gen-eration (Li et al., 2024b), and HASS compares its distillation loss with six alternative losses on that corpus (Zhang et al., 2025). Two concurrent works propose a draft-training method that rela-bels an external corpus with greedy target rollouts (Lee et al., 2026h) and a vision-language block drafter trained on the target’s greedy generations (Lee et al., 2026b). We propose neither and instead treat the response source as an experimental factor, train the same objectives on both sources, and mea-sure how regeneration changes what each objective adds. Student-generated data with target feedback (Agarwal et al., 2024; Zhou et al., 2024) is a dif-ferent factor, because it trains on the drafter’s own outputs. 

Distillation data beyond drafting. Sequence-level distillation trains a student on teacher-generated sequences instead of matching token dis-tributions, and in translation, adding word-level dis-tillation on top of it changed BLEU by less than one point for every student (Kim and Rush, 2016). Dis-tilled corpora differ in complexity in ways whose value depends on student capacity (Zhou et al., 2020). In language model distillation, how the generated training data are constructed can decide whether distillation helps (Lee et al., 2026g,a), and the effect of a training objective can depend on the amount of available data (Lee et al., 2025). Draft training on regenerated responses is sequence-level distillation from the target, with KD as the word-level term. 

7 Discussion 

We find that the gain of a draft-training objective depends on who wrote the training responses. In our vision-language study, distillation and the sur-vival gate add large gains on external responses of both corpora, but once the target regenerates them greedily, distillation adds a small gain and the gate little or none. Even at matched supervised length on ALLaVA or at each objective’s best measured training length, distillation adds less than half of its external gain, and a text-only replication shows the shrinkage too, for distillation also with an autore-gressive drafter. Because regeneration is already common in draft training, the external-corpus gain of a new objective can overstate what it adds in such a pipeline. 

Which gains survive regeneration. We expect objectives that repair a mismatch between corpus and target to lose most of their value in pipelines that regenerate. The gate and the soft survival weighting discount corpus continuations that the target would not produce, and greedy regenera-tion leaves few such continuations. KD instead adds the target’s probability mass beyond its greedy token, which regenerated responses do not con-tain, and keeps a small gain. HASS, which adds a top-K distillation loss and trains the drafter on its own predicted features, likewise keeps its gain over EAGLE-2 (Li et al., 2024a) when both are trained on target-generated rather than fixed re-sponses (Zhang et al., 2025). 

Reporting objective gains. For an objective meant for pipelines that regenerate their responses, we suggest also measuring its gain over CE on responses that the target regenerates with the pipeline’s own decoding. Regenerating the ALLaVA and text corpora cost less than one train-ing run, as Appendix A shows. KD already com-putes the target’s distribution at every corpus posi-tion, so the agreement between corpus tokens and the target’s greedy tokens comes at no extra cost wherever KD is trained. Reporting this agreement beside an objective gain shows how far the training responses are from the target’s own greedy pre-dictions. Gains on external responses should be compared across training lengths or at each objec-tive’s best schedule, because the external KD gain 8roughly doubled from the shortest to the longest schedule we trained. 

Limitations 

Drafters and targets. We study two drafter fam-ilies with two Qwen3 targets: a DFlash-style paral-lel block drafter in both studies and an autoregres-sive EAGLE-3 drafter in the text study, where it is trained only with CE and KD and evaluated only under greedy decoding. Other drafter families, the gate on an autoregressive drafter and targets from other families may respond differently. Apart from one control, every vision drafter continues training a released drafter. That control shows the distil-lation shrinkage from random initialization at a smaller magnitude after three epochs, so the magni-tudes we report describe continued training. When the drafters of both objectives are anchored to the released drafter, most of the distillation shrinkage remains. 

Corpora and training lengths. Limited com-pute kept each training corpus to a fixed subset of at most 26,196 examples, and we did not extend the study to the full corpora. The second corpus, ShareGPT4V, has only external and fully regener-ated responses, and the matched-length versions and the controls cover ALLaVA alone. The horizon study measures the gate only on ALLaVA. Its best schedules are selected on the evaluation prompts, although the best-schedule ratio of ratios stays be-low zero on both corpora when the schedules are chosen on one half of the prompts and the ratio is estimated on the other. 

Measurement. MAT depends on the verification decoder. We measure it with a tree and a chain verifier in the vision study and, in the text study, with a block verifier for the DFlash drafter and the EAGLE-3 tree verifier for the EAGLE-3 drafter, and we do not measure wall-clock speed or answer quality. The vision verifiers accept drafts against the target’s greedy token computed within each batched bf16 verification pass, so MAT describes acceptance under that execution. 

Runs and intervals. Each DFlash cell has two training runs with one data order and each EAGLE-3 cell two runs in two data orders, six vision cells add one run in each of two further data orders, and each domain has 99 scored prompts. The bootstrap intervals cover prompt variation for the trained checkpoints, and the data orders vary only for those six cells and the EAGLE-3 cells. Appendix B also resamples the two runs of each cell, and the full-regeneration intervals of both corpora stay below zero. Two runs give only a coarse account of run variation, and no interval covers variation across corpora. 

Objectives and treatments. The factorial uses a hard survival gate, one distillation divergence, 

γ = 7 and one learning rate. The soft survival weighting and γ = 2 are tested only on external and fully regenerated responses, and the lower learn-ing rate only with CE and KD on those responses. Other divergences and position weights may shrink differently after regeneration. The response treat-ments change content, length, turn boundaries and anchor support together, so the matched-length con-trols bound the role of length without isolating it. 

Ethical Considerations 

This work trains and evaluates draft models with public datasets and released models within their licenses. It involves no human subjects and col-lects no data from people. A draft model changes how quickly its target generates, not what the tar-get can produce, so the behavior and risks of the target carry over unchanged. Appendix A.5 lists the licenses of all artifacts.

A Protocol Details 

A.1 Vision training 

Examples come from the ALLaVA LAION instruc-tion file in a fixed random order. We keep the first 26,196 rows whose conversation opens with a user turn and an assistant turn of at least 40 characters and whose image belongs to the first released image archive, and we train on that first exchange. The ShareGPT4V subset takes the 30,000 GPT-4V cap-tions of its 100K caption file whose images come from LAION, CC and SBU, sorts them by identifier, shuffles them with a fixed seed and keeps the first 26,196. On ShareGPT4V, the three objectives are trained on external and fully regenerated responses, both of which also enter the horizon study, and KD and KD + gate with γ = 2 are trained once on the external responses, as Appendix F lists. Examples with fewer than 32 supervised assistant tokens are removed. On ALLaVA this leaves 26,101 external, truncated and loss-window examples and 26,193 fully regenerated examples, so three epochs take 3,261 or 3,273 updates. No ShareGPT4V example falls below the threshold, so both ShareGPT4V ver-sions keep all 26,196 examples and three epochs take 3,273 updates. The drafter has five layers and reads the hidden states of target layers 1, 9, 17, 25 and 33. Train-ing uses four GPUs with an effective batch of 24, AdamW with 4% warmup and cosine decay, bf16, SDPA attention, gradient clipping at norm 1 and a maximum length of 4,096 tokens, and images keep between 50,176 and 200,704 pixels. The learning rate, 1.47 × 10 −3, is the SpecForge (Li et al., 2026) default for DFlash training, 6 × 10 −4, scaled by the square root of our six times larger batch on each GPU. The KD loss uses the target distribution at temperature 1 and carries a fixed scale of 1.013, the ratio of the median gradient norms of CE and KD on identical batches before training. Regeneration uses the target in vLLM (Kwon et al., 2023) with greedy decoding and the im-age bounds and chat template of training, and the 3.8% of regenerated ALLaVA responses that reach the 768-token limit are closed there. Regenerating the ALLaVA corpus took about 5 minutes on four H100 GPUs, on which a three-epoch ALLaVA run trains for 29 to 46 minutes. Truncation cuts the regenerated content to the token count of the exter-nal response and closes the assistant turn. The loss window keeps the full regenerated input and masks the loss after that count. In the 60 retained exam-ples whose regenerated response is shorter than the external one, both treatments keep the whole response. On ALLaVA, regenerated responses average 395 tokens against 112 for external ones, and a proxy for the anchors available in each example, the mean of min(128 , L ) over response lengths L, is 106.7 for external and 127.3 for fully regenerated re-sponses. Fully regenerated ShareGPT4V responses keep the first 768 tokens of the target’s greedy re-sponse, and the 3.9% that are longer are closed there. They average 380 tokens against 203 for the external captions, and the anchor proxy is 127.5 against 126.7. 

A.2 Vision evaluation 

The captioning prompts are rows 100 to 199 of COCO val2017, the TextVQA prompts rows 0 to 99 of the public training split and the DocVQA prompts rows 400 to 499 of the validation split, each in release order. The first prompt of each do-main is a warm-up that is not scored. No evaluation image occurs byte for byte in the training corpus. Images are resized to at most 896 pixels on the longer side. In each round the drafter predicts the 15 slots in one forward pass, and the verifier builds a new tree from these predictions. The candidates at a slot are the drafter’s eight most probable tokens there, a path is scored by the sum of the drafter’s log-probabilities of its tokens, and the tree keeps the 63 best nodes, each added only after its parent. The target scores the whole tree in one forward pass with a tree attention mask and accepts the path that follows its own greedy tokens as far as the tree allows. All vision MATs come from one GPU model, the RTX A6000, in bf16. Within each bootstrap resample, every run’s MAT is recomputed from the drawn prompts, and the runs of a cell are averaged before increments 12 and ratios are formed. MAT counts the accepted path and the target’s own token in each round before the final round is clipped at the end-of-sequence token or the length limit, so it can differ from delivered tokens in that last round. 

A.3 Vision controls 

Every control uses external and fully regenerated ALLaVA responses. The controls with random initialization, one third of the learning rate and the L2 anchor train CE and KD, and the random-initialization control trains them for three and for six epochs. The control with γ = 2 trains CE, KD, KD + gate and KD with the soft weighting, the control with two further data orders retrains the six factorial cells of the two versions once in each or-der, and the 64-token control trains KD. Apart from the data orders, control cells are trained twice. The chain verifier re-evaluates the 24 checkpoints of the external, fully regenerated and matched-length versions. The sampled evaluation re-evaluates the 12 checkpoints of the external and fully regenerated versions with the tree verifier while the target sam-ples at temperature 1, with random numbers fixed for each prompt and output position and shared by all drafters. The random-initialization control keeps the drafter’s architecture and starts its five layers and feature projection from random weights, while the embeddings and output head come from the target as in every run. Each six-epoch run starts from the random weights and data order of its three-epoch counterpart and changes only the number of epochs, so its warmup and cosine decay span 6,522 updates on external and 6,546 on fully regenerated responses. The two further data orders shuffle the training examples into two other fixed random or-ders, and each of their six cells is trained once with the anchor sampling of the first factorial run, which represents the main order. The control with γ = 2 

keeps the rest of the recipe, and its soft weighting multiplies the target’s probabilities of the preceding corpus tokens, as in PARD-2. The chain verifier runs the tree verifier’s routine with one candidate at each slot and seven slots in each round, on the same prompts and GPU model. After every optimizer step, the L2 anchor moves the drafter’s weights toward those of the released drafter by the current learning rate times their difference, in both objec-tives. The control with 64 distilled tokens keeps the rest of the KD recipe and reuses the factorial CE cells. To separate turn closure from the other differ-ences at the cut, we also train KD and KD + gate twice on the truncated examples with the loss masked on the tokens that close the assistant turn after the cut, keeping the examples, their order and the training length. The loss on these tokens stays in the examples that the cut leaves whole, whose closure is the target’s own, and in 15 cut exam-ples that masking would leave with fewer than 32 supervised tokens. 

A.4 Text study 

The text corpus starts from the 26,196 conversa-tions whose messages have the lowest SHA-256 hashes in the first of the three supervised fine-tuning files of UltraChat 200k. Their first turns are kept when both responses leave at least 32 super-vised tokens under the 1,024-token limit. Regener-ated responses are the target’s greedy responses of at most 768 new tokens. They are 1.7 times as long as the original ones, 594 against 344 supervised tokens on average, and 56.6% reach the 768-token limit. Regenerating the text corpus took 2.9 GPU-hours on one RTX A6000, against 7.2 to 8.9 hours for each three-epoch DFlash text run on the same GPU model. Each of the eight DFlash cells, CE, KD, KD + gate and the label control of Section 5 on each version, is trained twice for three epochs on one RTX A6000 with the learning rate, effective batch and γ of the vision study, using micro-batches of 3, eight accumulation steps, 3,069 updates, a KD scale of 1 and a maximum length of 1,024 tokens. Evaluation covers MT-Bench (Zheng et al., 2023), Alpaca (Taori et al., 2023), GSM8K (Cobbe et al., 2021), AIME 2024 and 2025, HumanEval (Chen et al., 2021) and LiveCodeBench (Jain et al., 2025). The seven tasks contribute 80, 80, 1,319, 30, 30, 164 and 400 prompts, with up to 256 new tokens. At T = 1 the target samples and the drafter pro-poses its greedy tokens, and each prompt uses a fixed random seed. Tasks take the place of domains in the aggregation and the bootstrap. 

EAGLE-3 drafter. The second text drafter starts from a publicly released EAGLE-3 drafter for Qwen3-4B, one decoder layer that reads the fused hidden states of target layers 1, 17 and 32 and pre-dicts over a fixed 32,000-token subset of the target vocabulary, whose mapping we keep. Each of its four cells is trained twice for three epochs with the micro-batches, accumulation steps, updates, 13 warmup, gradient clipping and maximum length of the DFlash text runs, with seven simulated test-time steps and a learning rate of 2.45 × 10 −4, the SpecForge default for EAGLE-3 training, 10 −4,scaled by the same factor. This drafter samples no anchors, so the two runs of a cell use two data or-ders. CE and KD train the positions whose corpus token lies in the drafter’s vocabulary, and KD keeps the target’s eight most probable tokens within that vocabulary and merges the rest, including all proba-bility outside the vocabulary, into a ninth outcome. The fine-tuned checkpoints and the released drafter are evaluated at temperature 0 on the same 2,103 prompts with the same token limit, using the pub-lic EAGLE-3 decoder, which verifies a tree of 59 drafted tokens, at most eight deep, grown from the drafter’s ten most probable tokens at each step, so its MATs are compared only within this family.
