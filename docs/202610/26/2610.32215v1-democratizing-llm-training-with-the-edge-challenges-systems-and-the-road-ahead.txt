# Democratizing LLM Training with the Edge: Challenges, Systems, and the Road Ahead 

Ziyue Luo 1, Jiaxuan Cai 1* , Cedric Le Denmat 1* , Srijith Nair 1* , Fatemeh Nourzad 1* , Rohith Krishnan Sudha 1* ,Qinhang Wu 1* , Jifan Zhang 2* , Sungjae Lee 1, Zhe Li 3, Peiwen Qiu 1, Siddharth Shah 4, Rishabh Sharma 2,Sundararajan Srinivasan 5, Yinglun Xia 1, Xue Zheng 1, Zidong Liu 6, Bicheng Ying 7, Kaushik Chowdhury 5,Gauri Joshi 4, Yingbin Liang 1, Robert Nowak 2, Srinivasan Parthasarathy 1, Saurav Prakash 8,Balaraman Ravindran 8, Sanjay Shakkottai 5, Ness B. Shroff 1, Haibo Yang 3, Aylin Yener 1, and Jia Liu 1

Abstract —The growing computational power of modern GPU data centers has let a few industrial giants train and deploy large language models (LLMs) on massive amounts of data. Yet the sheer scale of these models and their training data makes centralized development increasingly hard to sustain, and at the same time shuts out smaller institutions that hold relevant private data but lack comparable infrastructure. In this paper, we organize our positions on distributed LLM training around the pretraining and fine-tuning paradigms, covering federated learning, split learning, and decentralized approaches, and we assess their readiness for geo-distributed settings with heterogeneous hardware and unreliable connectivity. We then present a unified system architecture built around a cloud-hosted parameter server that coordinates heterogeneous clients over outbound gRPC connections, reusing one communication, compression, and fault-tolerance stack for both pretraining and a family of fine-tuning methods. We validate the design with real geo-distributed experiments on GPT-2 Medium and Llama3-1B pretraining across multiple university sites, showing stable convergence under realistic conditions. Finally, we chart the road ahead, identifying open problems that span frontier model support, elastic fault tolerance, adaptive compression, federated alignment, privacy and trust, incentive design, and system– algorithm co-design, all of which must be addressed to make democratized LLM training a reality. 

Index Terms —Distributed machine learning systems, large language models, federated learning, geo-distributed training, parameter server, fault tolerance. 

I. I NTRODUCTION 

Recent progress in Large Language Models (LLMs) [1]– [4] and foundation models [5], [6] has been driven almost entirely by a few large technology companies operating vast, centralized computing centers. This concentration of compute carries three costs. First, it limits transparency , since the most capable models are developed behind closed doors, which constrains independent scientific review and replication. Second, it raises trust and security concerns, because closed and proprietary training pipelines are difficult to audit. Third, it imposes environmental costs, as hyperscale data centers con-sume enormous amounts of energy and contribute substantially to carbon emissions.

Scaling laws indicate that model quality keeps improving with more data and more parameters [7], [8], which only sharpens the incentive to train ever larger models. Yet two bottlenecks increasingly restrict who can participate. The first is data availability and governance : much of the world’s useful data is private, and centralizing it raises privacy, band-width, and storage costs that many data owners cannot or will not pay. The second is computational resources : a single training run often demands hundreds of high-end GPUs joined by high-throughput interconnects, an infrastructure that lies well beyond the reach of most universities and smaller firms. Distributed and privacy-preserving paradigms such as feder-ated learning [9] and split learning [10] offer a natural starting point for loosening these constraints. Extending LLM training 

to geo-distributed clusters of heterogeneous GPUs, however, exposes a harder problem: real wide-area networks are slow and unreliable, and the institutions that own the GPUs sit behind firewalls, NATs, and incompatible software stacks that no single operator controls. This tension motivates the central question of this paper. 

Key Question: Can we make LLM training broadly acces-sible by distributing it across a network, so that training can happen “anywhere and everywhere”? To answer this question, we observe that LLM development decomposes into two phases, pretraining and fine-tuning , each with its own objectives and constraints.  

> •

Pretraining across Geo-distributed GPU Clusters: Pre-training optimizes a foundation model [1], [2] from scratch, or from a weak initialization, over massive text corpora. We aim to run this phase on underutilized GPUs spread across many institutions. Each site runs a client process that trains locally, for example with Fully Sharded Data Parallelism (FSDP) [11], while coordination across sites takes place over wide-area networks.  

> •

Fine-tuning across Geo-distributed GPU Clusters: Fine-tuning adapts a foundation model to downstream tasks and adds constraints related to data governance, privacy, and site-specific objectives. Here, each institution fine-tunes on its own data while a central service coordinates the updates, with no need for direct peer-to-peer connectivity between sites. arXiv:2610.32215v1 [cs.DC] 23 Oct 2026 2

These two phases differ in their learning objectives, but we argue that a single system structure can serve both. That structure is a cloud-hosted parameter server (PS) that holds an authoritative global snapshot, together with a set of heterogeneous clients that perform local updates, periodically push parameter deltas to the PS, and pull refreshed snapshots in return. This abstraction lets us reuse one connectivity model, one fault-tolerance and rejoin path, and one set of communication optimizations for both pretraining and fine-tuning. The two phases differ not in the machinery but in the demands they place on it. Pretraining is dominated by throughput and stability: the system must maximize token throughput under stragglers and WAN delay while maintain-ing a steady trajectory over very long runs. Fine-tuning is dominated by governance and personalization: updates come from non-i.i.d. data, must respect institutional boundaries, and are often parameter-efficient (for example, LoRA) to reduce communication and memory cost. Despite these differences, both phases run into the same 

systems obstacles once training crosses institutional boundaries over the public Internet. We group them into three challenges that any practical cross-institution training system must con-front. 

Challenge 1: Computation Heterogeneity. The first chal-lenge is orchestrating heterogeneous resources, GPUs above all. Even inside a single production cluster, GPU fleets are rarely uniform: new generations arrive quickly, and replacing a cluster wholesale is prohibitively expensive, so operators add newer hardware while keeping older devices in service. The Ohio Supercomputer Center (OSC) [12], for example, runs H100s, A100s, and V100s side by side. Using such mixed fleets well already improves training efficiency [13], and col-laboration across institutions only amplifies the problem, since sites differ in GPU architecture, memory capacity, and soft-ware environment. We separate this challenge into hardware and software heterogeneity, then summarize its algorithmic consequences. 

Hardware Heterogeneity. Consider one site with a pair of 32 GB V100 GPUs, a second contributing a single 24 GB RTX 4090, and a third offering a bank of A100s with native BF16 support. Running identical work on every machine turns the slower GPUs into stragglers that stretch the wall-clock training time and leave faster devices idle. Mixing numerical formats such as FP32, FP16, and BF16 can also produce silent errors when gradients are combined, which in turn risks model divergence. 

Software Heterogeneity. Software stacks diverge as well. Driver versions, CUDA toolkits, and NCCL libraries vary across administrative domains; an optimizer built for CUDA 12.2 may fail on CUDA 11.7, and certain vendor BF16 optimizations are simply absent on older cards. Coordinating upgrades across many universities is slow and error-prone, es-pecially when each cluster serves dozens of unrelated research groups. 

Algorithmic Implications. Synchronous data-parallel train-ing assumes every step finishes at roughly the same time. Heterogeneous compute breaks that assumption and pushes the system toward asynchronous aggregation. FSDP, designed for homogeneous clusters, does not readily support different shard sizes per device; in practice we instead vary the per-device batch size so that faster GPUs do more work, all while preserving the conditions needed for stable convergence. 

Challenge 2: Latency and Bandwidth Variability. The second challenge is the unpredictable latency and fluctuating bandwidth of a geo-distributed deployment. 

Variability of WAN links. Wide-area links between distant institutions and cloud infrastructure are variable and prone to disruption. Packet loss and intermittent connectivity break transfers mid-flight, leaving model synchronization incomplete and training iterations inconsistent. The result is delayed convergence, longer runs, and a less effective system overall. 

Staleness versus Compression. Aggressive compression shrinks the bytes on the wire but can introduce bias that slows or destabilizes convergence. Waiting for every worker, on the other hand, lets the slowest link dictate throughput. Prior work on gradient staleness shows that accuracy degrades quickly once delays exceed a few steps for large transformers, yet WAN jitter alone can introduce delays of several seconds. The system must therefore navigate the space between these two failure modes rather than commit to either. 

Challenge 3: Heterogeneous Network Configurations. 

The third challenge is the diversity of institutional network policies, which we group into three forms. 

Security Middleboxes and Policy Heterogeneity. Many in-stitutional clusters sit behind enterprise firewalls or Network Address Translation (NAT) devices that block inbound connec-tions and restrict outbound ports. Edge servers on 5G or LTE change IP addresses often, and campus Wi-Fi may rewrite or cap long-lived TCP connections. Under such varied policies, a simple peer-to-peer overlay is not realistic. 

Unreliable and Asymmetric Paths. WAN paths fail for mun-dane reasons, from fiber cuts to scheduled maintenance, and wireless links fade. Strongly asymmetric bandwidth, where upload and download speeds differ sharply, complicates any protocol that leans on acknowledgments. A reset connection can break a standard gRPC stream and force a costly re-dial that risks dropping in-flight data. 

Operational Complexity. Contributors should not have to configure port forwarding or stand up VPN tunnels to partic-ipate. Truly democratized participation across institutions and edge sites requires a simple “click-to-join” experience. Together, these three challenges define the systems problem of democratized LLM training. Addressing them requires three design principles. First, heterogeneity awareness : the system uses asynchronous aggregation that decouples local training from global synchronization, so that stragglers do not stall faster nodes. Second, asynchronous robustness : the system bounds staleness and uses fault-tolerant communication, on the assumption that WAN links fail by default rather than as the exception. Third, network compatibility : the system relies on a cloud-hosted rendezvous with outbound-only connectiv-ity, which removes the need for peer-to-peer overlays, port 3

forwarding, or VPN setup, and thereby lowers the barrier to entry. Guided by these principles, this paper makes the following contributions.  

> •

We identify and characterize the systems challenges that arise when LLM pretraining and fine-tuning move beyond centralized data centers, namely computation heterogeneity, WAN latency and bandwidth variability, and diverse net-work configurations, and we argue that these challenges remain unsolved at the scale that democratized training requires.  

> •

We survey the landscape of distributed LLM training along two axes, training paradigm (pretraining versus fine-tuning) and coordination style (synchronous, asynchronous, and infrequent-synchronization). We examine how existing methods handle compute, network, and statistical hetero-geneity, and where they fall short once deployed in geo-distributed, WAN-constrained settings with diverse institu-tional networks.  

> •

We propose a unified two-level system architecture in which a cloud-hosted parameter server, talking to heterogeneous clients only over outbound gRPC connections, supplies shared building blocks (periodic delta synchronization, quantization-based compression, and fault-tolerant rejoin) that a federated pretraining paradigm and a federated fine-tuning paradigm reuse without modification. Preliminary results on GPT-2 Medium and Llama3-1B pretraining show stable convergence and competitive wall-clock efficiency under realistic WAN conditions.  

> •

We describe a set of open problems for the community. These span scaling to frontier and multimodal models, fault tolerance under elastic membership, adaptive communica-tion compression, federated alignment, privacy and trust at LLM scale, incentive design for sustained participation, and system-algorithm co-design for dynamic WAN environ-ments. To facilitate reproducibility and future research, we make the implementation of our unified distributed LLM training framework publicly available at https://github.com/ai-edge-i nstitute/geo-distributed-llm-training. The rest of the paper is organized as follows. Section II surveys distributed learning and existing systems for dis-tributed LLM pretraining and fine-tuning. Section III presents our unified architecture, describes the shared infrastructure and the two paradigms it supports, and reports preliminary experiments. Section IV discusses open problems and the research agenda ahead. II. B ACKGROUND : D ISTRIBUTED LLM T RAINING 

In this section, we review existing methods for distributed LLM training, grouped into two broad categories: pretrain-ing and fine-tuning. The underlying distributed optimization techniques, such as federated learning and split learning, are shared across both categories, but the optimization process and the computation and communication requirements differ markedly between them. The training category also shapes specific system choices, including the use of compression, whether the model is pipelined or split, and the kind of network messages exchanged. 

A. Preliminaries a) Distributed learning vs. federated learning: what changes when the boundary is institutional.: Distributed learning trains a shared model across multiple workers, tradi-tionally in data-center settings with centralized coordination, fast intra-cluster communication, and increasingly sophisti-cated sharding and parallelism stacks for large models [11], [14]–[18]. Federated learning (FL) , by contrast, is defined by organizational boundaries: data remain local to multiple owners, communication is constrained, and training proceeds through exchanged model updates rather than centralized data collection [9], [19]. As a result, FL must treat participation, availability, heterogeneity, and system-level issues such as scheduling, fault tolerance, and secure aggregation as first-class concerns [20], [21], making the optimization method inseparable from the communication protocol that governs it. 

b) Parameter servers: a foundational abstraction for many-to-one coordination.: A natural abstraction for large-scale training is the parameter server (PS): a logically cen-tralized service maintains model parameters, while workers pull parameters, compute updates, and push them back for aggregation. Early systems such as DistBelief already followed this many-to-one pattern in practice [14], and later work formalized PS as a general framework for distributed learning with flexible consistency models, elastic scaling, and fault tolerance [15]. PS-style coordination is especially attractive when direct peer-to-peer communication is difficult or undesirable. Be-cause workers communicate primarily with a coordinator rather than a dense all-to-all group, this abstraction fits dynamic membership, heterogeneous workers, and settings with asymmetric or policy-constrained connectivity. It also provides a natural control point for enforcing synchronous, asynchronous, or bounded-staleness update rules [15], [22]. Although modern data-center training often emphasizes col-lective communication, systems such as BytePS show that PS-style and collective designs can be viewed within a unified design space [23]. For WAN-scale and cross-domain learning, the PS abstraction remains especially appealing because it aligns with restricted connectivity, variable worker availability, and explicit server-side control over synchronization. 

c) Synchronous, asynchronous, and infrequent-synchronization training: consistency models as the hidden design choice.: A central axis in both distributed learning and FL is the synchronization model, since it determines both when workers communicate and which version of the model they optimize against. In synchronous training, workers compute in parallel and then coordinate a global update at a barrier, as in data-parallel SGD or one FedAvg communication round [9]. This yields clean semantics because all workers update from the same parameter snapshot, but wall-clock progress is limited by the slowest worker and communication path. 4

In asynchronous training, workers instead push updates whenever they finish local computation. The idea predates modern deep learning: early work on asynchronous itera-tive methods and distributed optimization established conver-gence under delayed information [24]–[26]. Later, H OGWILD !showed that lock-free asynchronous stochastic updates can work well in suitable regimes [27], while systems such as Dist-Belief and the parameter-server line brought asynchronous and partially synchronous execution to large-scale learning [14], [15]. The main benefit is higher utilization under stragglers and heterogeneity; the main cost is staleness , since an update may be computed from parameters that are already outdated when applied. This tradeoff motivates bounded-staleness models that lie between strict synchronization and unconstrained asynchrony. Stale Synchronous Parallel (SSP) is the canonical exam-ple: workers may proceed asynchronously, but only up to a bounded lag, making staleness an explicit system knob [22]. More recent analyses continue to study asynchronous SGD under heterogeneous computation and communication pat-terns [28]. In practice, similar ideas also appear through buffered aggregation, time-based synchronization, and stale-update filtering. The same spectrum appears in FL. Standard FL is usu-ally organized into synchronous rounds , where clients train locally and then send updates to a coordinator; FedAvg is the canonical example and already reflects the idea of amortizing communication through multiple local steps [9]. Asynchronous FL removes this round barrier and allows clients to contribute whenever they finish local work. Asynchronous Federated Optimization explicitly incorporates stale client updates into FL [29], while buffered asynchronous aggregation provides a practical middle ground [30]. A closely related design choice is infrequent synchroniza-tion : instead of communicating often, workers perform more local computation between exchanges. This includes local SGD / periodic averaging, with FedAvg as the FL counter-part [9], [31]. The systems advantage is lower coordination cost; the optimization risk is greater drift across replicas, especially under non-IID data, heterogeneous local work, or delayed communication. Recent large-model training systems revisit this idea explicitly in low-communication regimes, with DiLoCo as a representative example [32]. Overall, syn-chronous execution, asynchronous execution, bounded stale-ness, and infrequent synchronization are best viewed as points along a broader consistency–communication tradeoff : tighter synchronization reduces staleness but amplifies waiting, while looser or less frequent synchronization improves utilization at the cost of noisier and more drift-prone updates. 

d) Heterogeneity is the default: compute, network, and statistical heterogeneity.: Heterogeneity is a defining feature of federated and WAN-scale learning. It appears at least in three forms: compute heterogeneity , where clients differ in hardware, memory, workload, and availability; network hetero-geneity , where bandwidth, latency, jitter, and reliability vary across links; and statistical heterogeneity , where clients hold non-IID data with different sample sizes or label distributions. These forms are tightly coupled: slower devices and weaker links create stragglers and stale updates, while non-IID data amplifies the optimization error caused by delayed or uneven participation. One line of work addresses systems heterogeneity . Fed-Prox is a canonical example because it explicitly models FL under both systems and statistical heterogeneity, allowing variable local work while stabilizing training with a proximal term [21]. TiFL mitigates stragglers through tier-based client grouping [33], while Oort improves time-to-accuracy by se-lecting clients that are both system-efficient and statistically useful [34]. FedScale complements these methods by exposing such effects through realistic large-scale benchmarking [35]. A second line of work targets statistical heterogeneity ,especially client drift under non-IID data. FedProx partially addresses this through regularization [21]; SCAFFOLD uses control variates to correct drift [36]; FedNova addresses ob-jective inconsistency caused by unequal local work [37]; and MIME reduces the gap between federated and centralized optimization under heterogeneous data [38]. For WAN-scale cross-silo training, the key lesson is that these heterogeneities should not be treated in isolation. Sys-tems heterogeneity affects who participates, network hetero-geneity affects how delayed updates become, and statistical heterogeneity determines how harmful those delays are after aggregation. The relevant question is therefore not simply how to “handle heterogeneity,” but which form of heterogeneity a method addresses, through what mechanism, and under what communication and consistency assumptions.

a) Key Challenges with Centralized Pretraining: While pretraining is a straightforward application of supervised learn-ing from an optimization perspective, the main challenge with LLM pretraining is that the training data and the model parameter sizes are unprecedentedly large when compared to the ML models and datasets used just a decade ago [8], [40], [42], [43]. In fact, the current state-of-the-art (SOTA) LLMs simply cannot be pretrained on single hardware accelerators (e.g., GPU, TPU or HPU) or a single stand-alone machine, and instead require several hours (often, days) of distributed 5

training across powerful data centers with hundreds of high-performance devices. Besides the optimization process, an of-ten ignored overhead in the centralized pretraining pipeline, is data collection and curation [44]. For these reasons, centralized LLM pretraining has driven a sharp rise in energy expenditure and is affordable only to the largest technology firms. Almost all SOTA LLMs are therefore proprietary and maintained by a handful of corporations, which leaves LLM pretraining research dominated by industry and public academic work well behind. Centralized training faces a further limit: scaling laws [7] imply that the supply of publicly available data will be insufficient to meet LLM training demands by 2028 [45]. 

b) Distributed Systems: These concerns together call for a collaborative effort to build genuinely distributed and decentralized systems for training LLMs at scale. Towards this end, academics and startups [32], [46]–[50], and large corporations [11], [32], [51] have proposed to leverage existing distributed ML systems as a means to facilitate a more 

democratic approach to LLM training. Federated Learning 

(FL) [9], [21], [36], [52] and Decentralized FL (DFL) [53]– [56] democratize LLM pretraining on edge devices by forming a loose federation of participating devices, which in turn train the LLM locally, in parallel , while aggregating models trained by others, either via a centralized parameter server , or in a decentralized manner. DiLoCo [32] is the closest to the system we propose here. Its two defining choices are the use of Nesterov momentum as the outer optimizer and time-based synchronization in place of the step-based synchronization common in conventional FL. We refer the reader to the surveys [57], [58] for comprehensive coverage of FL and DFL. There are several key benefits to using such collaborative and decentralized training paradigms:  

> •

From a systems perspective, FL and DFL avoid the cost of collecting and centralizing data, since they operate on data held locally by design.  

> •

By avoiding data sharing, these methods are intrinsically 

privacy-preserving , enabling training on the vast pool of private and sensitive data without compromising privacy [59].  

> •

Many FL algorithms enjoy linear speedup [60], meaning that their theoretical convergence rate improves as more devices, or clients, participate.  

> •

Combined with Split Learning (SL) [10], [61], smaller clients with much lesser compute and memory, can still participate in the training process where they train only the initial few layers of the model, leaving the bulk of processing to the server. 

c) Open-source Systems: Several projects such as Ray Anyscale [62], Amazon SageMaker [63], Google Vertex AI [64], NVIDIA NeMo [65], and several others, offer commer-cial plans or solutions for distributed LLM training, but are proprietary and run on private and paid clusters. Recently, various open-source systems have been designed with support for LLM pretraining and fine-tuning, such as PrimeIntellect [66]. Flower [48], [49], is perhaps the largest such open-source endeavor, with a large community of contributors, but many parallel projects have also been initiated in recent years like [50], [51], [67]. Some other projects offer decentralized FL systems [68], [69], and with support for model pipelining and split learning [70]. Most of these systems address a single concern, such as federated or distributed training, or pipelining. What is increas-ingly needed, however, is a system that adapts dynamically to system and data heterogeneity and to the geographical layout of the federation. An ideal system would automatically split or pipeline models that exceed a device’s capacity, while performing system-aware and network-aware load balancing and straggler mitigation.

a) FL for LoRA fine-tuning: Fine-tuning under the FL framework has gained substantial traction lately, largely be-cause training large models on edge or IoT devices with scarce communication and computation budgets is increas-ingly relevant [78]–[80]. Distributed pretraining and fine-tuning share several properties, including local and private data and the absence of a central authority. Fine-tuning in constrained networks, however, calls for additional system-level support such as model splitting [10], [61] or pipeline parallelism [81], [82], since many edge devices cannot store or process an entire LLM and must instead spread it across the 6

network. Robustness to system, data, and model heterogeneity, along with tolerance for random client dropouts, are further considerations when designing an FL fine-tuning system for such settings. 

b) RL-based fine-tuning: Fine-tuning methods requiring multi-turn generation are often formulated as reinforcement learning (RL) problems, like alignment to human values [83]– [87], reasoning for mathematics and coding [88], [89], long-horizon and tool-calling agentic systems using MCP [90], retrieval-augmented generation (RAG) [91], etc. Such systems often require distributed RL across multiple devices [92] for the same reasons as pretraining and fine-tuning. III. A U NIFIED SYSTEM FOR GEO -DISTRIBUTED LLM TRAINING 

In this section, we describe how the vision of Section I is realized as a practical, fault-tolerant training system. The sys-tem has two levels. The unified building blocks (Section III-B) provide the shared infrastructure for communication, synchro-nization, compression, and fault tolerance. The two applica-tion paradigms , pretraining and fine-tuning (Section III-C), build on this infrastructure to meet their respective objectives. Throughout the section, we also present preliminary evaluation results that validate the design under realistic geo-distributed conditions. 

A. Design Goals 

Our system design is guided by three main goals to address the challenges of distributed LLM training: 

a) G1: Use heterogeneous GPUs without stragglers: The system must keep all participating clusters actively working at the same time, even if they have different computing power (FLOPS), memory capacity, or support for mixed-precision calculations. This goal directly addresses Challenge 1 (com-putation heterogeneity). Section I 

b) G2: Handle WAN latency and bandwidth variations: 

Model synchronization cannot assume fast, sub-millisecond network connections. Instead, it must function effectively with links where speeds can change by a large margin. This goal directly addresses Challenge 2 (latency and bandwidth variability). 

c) G3: Survive firewall/NAT asymmetry and preemptions: 

Clients should only need to make outbound connections. New or re-launched nodes must be able to rejoin the training quickly with a single remote procedure call (RPC) to the parameter server (PS). This goal directly addresses Challenge 3 (diverse network configurations).

a) Design evolution: from pipeline parallelism to PS: 

Our initial architectural vision involved “pipeline parallelism,” where different levels of the LLM would reside on separate clusters as depicted in Figure 2. This design was attractive because it allowed scaling very large models beyond the memory capacity of a single cluster and promised reduced communication overhead compared to full data parallelism. However, this pipeline design inherently necessitates direct peer-to-peer (P2P) connections between participating clusters. In practice, the firewalls and network address translation (NAT) policies commonly enforced in institutional networks make direct P2P difficult to implement reliably. This led us to evolve our architecture toward the centralized PS model in Figure 1, which provides a more robust and manageable communication infrastructure. 

b) Parameter server (PS): We set up our PS as alightweight gRPC service on Amazon Web Services (AWS). This PS receives parameter differences (deltas) from the clients, applies federated averaging (FedAvg) [9] to combine 7

these updates, and sends out new global model snapshots. All communication, including control messages and model tensors, uses outbound connections from clients to the PS, avoiding the need for complex inbound firewall configurations at client sites. The PS aggregates tensor updates in its memory, efficiently streaming data to minimize computing and memory overhead. 

Why a centralized PS? Beyond sidestepping the P2P dif-ficulties above, the centralized relay brings two concrete benefits. It reduces the connectivity structure from O(N 2) peer links to O(N ) client-to-server links, and it provides a single place to manage TLS encryption and access control for the whole federation. 

c) Multi-PS distributed learning: A single central PS can itself become a coordination bottleneck. We therefore offer a multi-PS mode as a configurable option: clients form clusters, typically by geographical proximity, and each cluster is coor-dinated by its own PS. Training within a cluster proceeds as in Algorithm 1, with the local PS sharing the global model, the clients training on their local data, and the PS aggregating periodically. The PSs then synchronize with one another via ring all-reduce. This setup balances load and mitigates the latency imposed by the geographical layout of the federation, directly addressing goal G2 and partly addressing goal G1. 

d) Client: Each client starts an FSDP [11] worker. The size of its micro-batches and its mixed-precision mode are automatically adjusted to match the client’s local memory and computing power (FLOPS). Clients store their optimizer states locally. Only the differences (deltas) from the last global model snapshot are sent over the WAN. 

e) Synchronization cadence: Instead of synchronizing after every training step, each client sends updates after a configurable wall-clock interval (e.g., 10 minutes). This limits how “stale” the model updates can become while also controlling the load on the WAN. 

2) Communication Protocol: Our initial implementation used PyTorch RPC for cross-institution communication. While PyTorch RPC is highly efficient within a single, tightly controlled cluster, its design mandates a mesh-like topology for distributed processes. This typically requires dynamic allocation of numerous ports, potentially leading to O(N 2)

connections for N clients. This design conflicted directly with the strict firewall policies common in institutional networks, which often block dynamic port ranges or require explicit, static rules for inbound connections. In response, we transitioned to the gRPC protocol. gRPC allowed us to explicitly manage ports and precisely define packet formats using Protocol Buffers. A key advantage of gRPC is its ability to operate over a single, fixed, user-specified port via persistent, outbound client connections, thereby circumventing inbound firewall restrictions at client sites. We leveraged gRPC’s bidirectional streaming RPCs (e.g., for S TREAM MODEL and P USH DELTA calls) to enable continuous exchange of model updates without constantly re-establishing connections. This strategic change effectively addressed the practical and technical constraints we faced, enabling reliable and secure communication for our distributed training framework. 

3) Network Compression: To reduce network traffic and latency, we compress the gradient payload (addressing Chal-lenge 2). Compression proceeds in two stages: quantization followed by Huffman encoding. Quantization maps each 32-bit floating-point gradient to an 8-bit or 16-bit integer, for compression factors of 4 and 2, respectively. We use min-max quantization for its low overhead, since it requires storing only the minimum and maximum of the gradient distribution. Huffman encoding then compresses the quantized gradients further. The receiver reverses the pipeline, decoding and then dequantizing to reconstruct the payload. We observed that maintaining min-max quantization pairs for each multi-dimensional tensor resulted in high storage overhead. Our solution was to flatten all multi-dimensional tensors into a single one-dimensional tensor and then perform quantization. Treating each tensor separately for Huffman encoding would not give enough gradient values to obtain a skewed distribution, and flattening the quantized tensors solved this issue. 

4) Fault Tolerance and Rejoin Logic: Interruptions, such as a dropped gRPC stream, signal a preemption. When a client relaunches, it authenticates with a one-time token, then fetches the latest global model ( Θ) and its last saved optimizer state from the PS. Because this path retrieves the live model directly, a client typically resumes within roughly 10 seconds. The mechanism preserves convergence guarantees while hiding the failure from the rest of the federation. For durability beyond a single relaunch, we also checkpoint model and optimizer state to disk as a single file. Checkpoint-ing operates at two levels: a checkpoint is written after every synchronization to guarantee fault tolerance, and a checkpoint manager separately retains the ten checkpoints with the lowest evaluation loss, refreshed every 1000 steps. Recovering from a cold disk checkpoint, rather than from the live PS model, takes about one minute. All checkpoints are stored locally on the client. If the PS itself fails, we relaunch the federation and Client 1 loads its saved checkpoint and streams it to the PS to resume training.

2) Paradigm 1: Federated Pretraining: Our pretraining system is directly derived from the meta-algorithm in Algo-rithm 1, by replacing the reparametrization in lines 3 and 13 with the identity transformation, i.e., all parameters are communicated and trained. Within each client, we leverage gradient accumulation and FSDP [11] to distribute training across multiple GPUs, allowing each worker to process mul-tiple micro-batches and aggregate them into a single mini-batch before performing a parameter update. This allows each client to select a micro-batch size that fits its hardware constraints, avoiding out-of-memory errors while maintaining a consistent effective mini-batch size across heterogeneous clients (addressing Challenge 1). Before transmission, gradi-ents are quantized to BF16 or INT8 depending on the observed network link speed (addressing Challenge 2). If a failure occurs during communication, the system detects the error and initiates immediate retransmission (addressing Challenge 3). Additionally, to prevent stragglers, i.e., weaker clients with low compute speeds, from slowing down the overall process, we also introduce a timeout T at the PS. If some clients time out before performing H optimization steps locally, they share their current set of parameters to the PS. d) Federated split learning (FSL-SAGE): Conventional FL assumes that every client can store and process the full model, an assumption that breaks down for very large mod-els on resource-constrained clients. Federated Split Learning (FSL) [10], [61] addresses this by partitioning the model be-tween the client and the server. To cut both the communication load and the number of synchronization cycles, we adopt FSL-SAGE [99], a provably convergent, communication-efficient FSL algorithm built around auxiliary models. FSL-SAGE uses two server processes: an F -server orchestrates federated aggregation of the client-side models, and an S-server trains the server-side model and periodically realigns each client’s auxiliary model. Because auxiliary-model alignment runs far less often than the per-iteration synchronization that vanilla SL requires, FSL-SAGE substantially lowers communication cost and lets the clients run in parallel. We deploy the F -server and S-server on separate AWS instances, with clients on single-GPU servers at OSU, UMich, UT, and IITM, and apply the system to LoRA fine-tuning of GPT-2 Medium for question answering on SQuAD [98]. To our knowledge, this is the first geo-distributed framework for scalable federated split learning. IV. T HE ROAD AHEAD 

In the previous sections, we described a unified system that runs both pretraining and fine-tuning across geo-distributed, heterogeneous GPU clusters, and we showed that it converges stably under realistic WAN conditions. The system is, however, only a first step. Several questions remain before such an approach can train frontier-scale models, absorb the churn of an open federation, and reward participants in a way that sustains their contribution over long runs. In this section, we describe these open problems. We organize them from the models one would like to train, to the systems and algorithms needed to support them, and finally to the economic structures that determine whether a federation can be sustained at all. 

A. Scaling to Frontier and Multimodal Models 

Our experiments consider models up to 1B parameters. Scaling to frontier sizes, on the order of 70B parameters and beyond, will require new strategies rather than larger versions of the present ones. One promising direction is Mixture-of-Experts (MoE) architectures [42], [43], whose sparse activa-tion reduces the per-client compute and memory footprint. In a geo-distributed setting, however, the placement and routing of experts across WAN-connected sites becomes a difficult sys-tems problem in its own right, since a naive cross-site partition can inflate communication, unbalance load, and destabilize routing. A second direction is to extend the system to multi-modal foundation models such as LLaVA [100]. Such models appear compatible with our framework, but they introduce additional heterogeneity in their modality-specific encoders, in data availability, and in communication cost that the current design does not yet address. 

B. Fault Tolerance under Elastic Membership 

Fault tolerance in large-scale training has received consider-able attention. Bamboo [101] tolerates frequent spot-instance preemptions by filling pipeline bubbles with redundant layer computation; Oobleck [102] pre-computes a set of heteroge-neous pipeline templates so that survivors can quickly recover 11 

a working configuration; and Gemini [103] reduces recovery latency by more than 13 × using multi-tiered in-memory checkpointing. These systems assume managed clusters with reliable, high-bandwidth interconnects and homogeneous hard-ware. These assumptions do not hold at the open edge. In our setting, disruption is the common case rather than the exception. Clients leave and rejoin between rounds, WAN links fluctuate without warning, and new participants arrive during training with unfamiliar hardware. Supporting such elastic membership requires reassigning work as clients come and go, moving model state over high-latency links, and bringing new joiners up to the current state without stalling the rest of the federation. We believe that lightweight, WAN-aware failure detection and state reconciliation, designed for open and heterogeneous membership rather than for closed clusters, are the main pieces still missing. 

C. Adaptive Communication Compression 

Our system already compresses traffic using quantization and Huffman coding (Section III-B3). Communication will only become more important as models grow. Error-feedback mechanisms [36] and adaptive quantization [104] can reduce traffic further. Interestingly, the “Square-Cube Law” observed in SWARM parallelism [105] shows that in some regimes larger models are in fact more communication-efficient, be-cause computation grows faster than communication volume. The open question is how to select the compression strat-egy adaptively , choosing bit-widths, sparsification ratios, and synchronization frequencies from real-time network measure-ments. We expect that doing so will require co-designing the compression scheme with the outer optimizer [32], so that the quantization schedule and the synchronization cadence are chosen together rather than separately. 

D. Federated Alignment and Reinforcement Learning 

Our system currently supports supervised fine-tuning (Sec-tion III-C3). A natural next step is to support federated alignment , since modern alignment pipelines rely heavily on reinforcement learning from human feedback (RLHF) [83]. RLHF does not fit easily into a federation: the reward model must be trained across institutions whose preference data is private and non-IID, a setting in which centralized pipelines do not directly apply. Preference-based objectives such as Direct Preference Optimization (DPO) [84] may be better suited here, since they avoid explicit reward modeling and therefore reduce both the number of shared components and the volume of communication. Even so, preference data remains heteroge-neous and institution-specific, and orchestrating such diverse preferences privately, without averaging away their diversity, is an open problem. A further difficulty is distributed rollout generation . RLHF repeatedly samples responses from the policy during training, and recent work identifies this generation step as the domi-nant systems bottleneck, which has motivated disaggregated execution [106]. Performing rollouts across heterogeneous, WAN-connected sites, while keeping the sampled trajectories fresh enough for stable policy optimization, will require an execution model quite different from the co-located RLHF systems used today. 

E. Privacy, Security, and Trust 

Federated learning is often motivated by privacy. Never-theless, the shared updates can still leak information about local data. Incorporating formal guarantees such as differential privacy (DP) [59], [107] is therefore an important next step. The utility-privacy tradeoff at LLM scale is not yet well understood, since the noise level that a small model tolerates can substantially degrade a billion-parameter one. Privacy is not the only concern. In an open federation whose partici-pants are not all trusted, Byzantine robustness , the ability to defend against clients that submit corrupted updates, becomes essential [108]. Whether robust aggregation rules such as the coordinate-wise median or the trimmed mean retain their guarantees at LLM scale is an open question that our setting makes pressing. 

F. Incentive Design and Participation Economics 

A democratized training ecosystem works only if partici-pants have a reason to contribute compute, data, and band-width. Existing schemes range from compute-centric rewards based on GPU hours or raw FLOPs [109] to Shapley-value methods [110] that estimate each participant’s marginal con-tribution to model quality. Both overlook the temporal and staleness effects that dominate asynchronous, geo-distributed training. A contribution that arrives during a critical conver-gence phase is worth far more than the same compute delivered after the loss has plateaued, yet neither metric distinguishes the two. Shapley scores are also unstable across aggregation strategies [111], and any deployable rule must account for strategic behavior by participants. Without incentives that reflect timing, data quality, and the possibility of gaming, a federation is vulnerable to free-riding and adverse selection. Designing incentive rules that are temporally aware, robust to manipulation, and inexpensive enough to enforce in real time is an open problem at the intersection of distributed systems and mechanism design. 

G. System-Algorithm Co-Design 

Building this system showed us that the algorithmic and systems choices cannot be made in isolation. The synchroniza-tion cadence, the compression strategy, and the fault-tolerance policy all interact with the optimizer, the learning-rate sched-ule, and the aggregation rule. Lengthening the synchronization interval, for example, saves communication but increases stal-eness, which may in turn call for a different outer optimizer, as DiLoCo [32] demonstrates with Nesterov momentum. We therefore envision an auto-tuning layer that monitors network conditions, compute heterogeneity, and convergence diagnos-tics, and adjusts the system parameters (compression ratio, synchronization frequency, timeout thresholds) together with the algorithmic hyperparameters (learning rate, local steps H,timeout T , aggregation weights). Such co-design would let the system adapt to the changing conditions of a geo-distributed deployment on its own, and would reduce the operational burden on participating institutions. 12 

H. Concluding Remarks 

In this work, we have demonstrated that LLM training need not remain confined to a handful of centralized data centers. The unified architecture presented in this paper sup-ports both pretraining and fine-tuning over a single cloud-hosted parameter server and a set of heterogeneous clients, communicating only through outbound connections, and our preliminary experiments show that it converges under realistic WAN conditions. The same design tolerates client failures and accommodates GPUs of different generations across several institutions. At the same time, the open problems described above show that much work remains. The barrier to participating in frontier model development is no longer only a matter of raw compute. Increasingly, it is a question of whether the systems, algorithms, and incentives exist to pool the compute that already sits idle across the world’s universities, laborato-ries, and edge sites. We believe such pooling is achievable, but it will require sustained effort across systems, machine learning, security, and economics, together with shared open platforms and benchmarks so that progress can be measured and reproduced. We hope this paper, and the system and open problems it describes, is a call to arms for the community to take up this effort, so that the most capable models, and the science of building them, do not remain the preserve of a few well-resourced institutions.
