Abstract — Federated learning (FL) is a distributed ma-chine learning paradigm that reduces transmission costs, mitigates cross-border data sharing restrictions, and en-hances privacy by keeping raw data on local devices. Clients in FL systems train local models and share only up-dates, which are aggregated by a central server. While this setup limits direct data exposure, FL remains vulnerable to privacy attacks. Existing attack and defense strategies have largely been developed for centralized systems, and adapting them to FL introduces new challenges due to its unique architecture and training workflow. In this work, we first propose a model inversion attack on FL systems using Deep Convolutional Generative Adversarial Networks (DCGANs) in the context of large-scale medical imaging. Our method operates under realistic assumptions about the attacker’s knowledge and capabilities. We then examine the effect of client-side differential privacy (DP) in mitigating privacy leakage and incorporate personalized FL to im-prove model performance under DP settings. Experiments on the Brain MRI ND-5 dataset show that DCGAN-based attacks can reconstruct semantically meaningful synthetic images, revealing significant risks to the privacy of training data. Applying DP reduces attack success but introduces an accuracy trade-off, which is further overcome by per-sonalized FL at each client side. We also evaluate how different aggregation algorithms affect both attack vulner-ability and defense effectiveness, underscoring the critical role of aggregation strategies. The proposed attack-and-defense framework is model-agnostic and applicable to a wide range of FL-based systems. 

I. I NTRODUCTION 

The demand for artificial intelligence (AI) has increased significantly over the past decade, driven by advances in ma-chine learning (ML) techniques. Traditionally, ML developers aggregate data on a central server to train a generalized model that can be deployed as a service. However, this centralized approach faces several challenges, including legal restrictions on cross-border data sharing, growing public concerns about personal privacy, and technical difficulties in aggregating large volumes of data from multiple heterogeneous sources. To address these issues, federated learning (FL) has emerged as a promising alternative. FL enables data owners to collabora-tively train a global model without sharing raw data, using a central parameter server to coordinate the training. This approach significantly reduces data transmission costs, ensures compliance with data protection regulations, and enhances privacy by design. FL has demonstrated strong potential in privacy-sensitive domains that deal with large-scale data, par-ticularly in healthcare applications such as medical imaging, electronic health record analysis, and disease prediction. The training process of an FL system begins with a central server constructing a global model and randomly initializing its parameters, which are then distributed to all participating local clients, such as hospitals. Each client performs local training using its private dataset and returns only the local model updates (e.g., gradients or weights), which the server then aggregates using standard algorithms such as Federated Averaging (FedAvg) [1], resulting in an improved global model. This updated model is then redistributed to the clients for the next training round, and the cycle continues until the model converges or achieves the desired accuracy. While FedAvg remains the most widely used aggregation method due to its simplicity and scalability, it faces challenges in non-IID (non–independent and identically distributed) data and heterogeneous settings, which are common in medical scenarios as well. To address these challenges, several alter-native aggregation strategies have been proposed—including Federated Optimization (FedOpt) [2], Federated Dynamics (FedDyn) [3], and Model-Contrastive Federated Learning (MOON) [4]—which aim to ensure robust and scalable model training, a key requirement also highlighted in [5]. The general FL framework is designed to provide a de-gree of data privacy at scale; however, it remains vulnerable to several privacy threats. Recent studies have shown that various types of privacy attacks can be carried out under different adversarial assumptions, thereby undermining the privacy guarantees of FL systems. Among these, inference attacks [6]–[8] are particularly concerning, as they may occur during both training and deployment phase. A prominent subclass of inference attacks is model inversion [9], [10], which is widely recognized as one of the most powerful threats to the confidentiality of ML models [11]. Model inversion attacks [9], [10] aim to reconstruct class-specific private data used for the training by exploiting the learned relationship between inputs and outputs, often through gradient inversion or surrogate-model optimization. A related category, data reconstruction (or extraction) attacks [6], [12], focuses on recovering individual training examples without necessarily targeting a specific class. Fredrikson et al. [13] first introduced the concept of model inversion by using confidence scores from prediction APIs to reconstruct class-representative inputs for centralized ML. arXiv:2610.27470v1 [cs.CR] 20 Oct 2026 2 IEEE TRANSACTIONS AND JOURNALS TEMPLATE 

There are several motivations for model inversion attacks in FL settings. One practical example in the medical do-main occurs when a competitor attempts to extract synthetic, patient-like images from a deployed model to gain access to valuable training data without obtaining regulatory approval or incurring high labeling costs. Such reconstructed data could then be used to train competing models or reverse-engineer proprietary datasets, ultimately undermining the data owner’s competitive advantage. While model inversion attacks have been extensively studied in centralized machine learning, extending these attacks to the decentralized and client-centric design of FL introduces unique challenges and remains an active area of research. This research proposal introduces a Privacy-Preserving Per-sonalized Federated Learning (PPP-FL) framework for med-ical imaging. We first evaluate the effectiveness of model inversion attacks in FL environments, where an honest-but-curious client colludes with an external adversary to infer sensitive private data. To conduct this attack, we employ a Generative Adversarial Network (GAN)—a deep generative model capable of producing realistic synthetic samples that closely approximate the distribution of the original training data and can successfully deceive the trained global classifier in the FL system. In this setting, the adversary lacks direct access to the original training data or model weights but can interact with the client-deployed trained global model during the inference phase by submitting inputs and observing outputs. The GAN is initially trained on publicly available auxiliary data that are similar in nature to the private training data. Then, by leveraging feedback from the local model the adversary optimizes a latent vector input to the GAN to gener-ate synthetic samples that closely resemble data from a specific target class. For higher-fidelity image-based reconstruction in the model inversion attack, we adopt a Deep Convolutional GAN (DCGAN). As a defense mechanism, PPP-FL investigates the effect of client-side differential privacy (DP). In this setup, each client applies an DP mechanism by injecting calibrated noise into its model updates before sending them to the server during global model training. This local perturbation obscures sensitive information at the source, thereby reducing the risk of privacy leakage in FL. To implement this approach, we utilize the Opacus privacy engine [14], which provides formal (ϵ, δ )-DP guarantees at the client level using DP-SGD by adding noise during the training process. Our study also analyzes how different aggregation algorithms affect the vulnerability of FL systems to model inversion attacks and evaluates the effective-ness of client-side DP in mitigating such risks. This dual focus offers valuable information about both algorithmic resilience and privacy protection. We also incorporate personalized FL techniques at the client-level to mitigate the impact of DP on model performance. The main contributions of this study are as follows:  

> •

We propose a DCGAN-based model inversion attack for FL systems, capable of generating synthetic samples that closely resemble the class-specific characteristics of private training data.  

> •

We investigate the effect of client-side differential privacy (DP) in mitigating model inversion attacks within the FL setting.  

> •

We analyze how different aggregation algorithms influ-ence both attack success and the effectiveness of DP-based defenses.  

> •

We incorporate personalized FL techniques at the client-level to alleviate the impact of DP on model performance.  

> •

We experimentally validate the effectiveness of the pro-posed attack and defense mechanisms using a large-scale medical MRI dataset. This paper is organized as follows: Section II provides back-ground information, and Section III reviews related work. Sec-tion IV presents the proposed attack model, while Section V describes the integration of client-side DP and personalized FL into the PPP-FL framework and provides a privacy analysis. Section VII reports the experimental results, highlighting the effectiveness of the attack and the mitigating role of client-side DP and personalized FL. Finally, Section VIII summarizes key findings and outlines future research directions.

B. Non-Independent and Identically Distributed Data 

In federated learning, non-independent and identically dis-tributed (non-IID) data arises because each client collects data from its own environment, leading to differences in feature distributions, label proportions, or user behaviors. For example, one device may have mostly images of one class while another has completely different classes. This uneven, biased distribution causes local models to learn patterns that do not generalize well globally. As a result, the aggregated model may converge slowly, become unstable, or show degraded performance compared to IID settings. To model such non-IID distributions in FL simulations, one of the widely adopted strategy is non-uniform label assignment [15]. Label assignment refers to the way class labels are distributed across clients in FL, which directly influences the degree of data heterogeneity. In our case, by adopting a non-uniform label assignment strategy, we created a controlled non-IID setting. Beyond label imbalance, non-IID data may also arise due to Dirichlet sampling, covariate shift, and concept shift, which are beyond the scope of this paper. 

C. Aggregation Algorithm 

FedAvg is one of the most widely used aggregation al-gorithms in federated learning, but struggle with non-IID data and system heterogeneity. To overcome these challenges, alternatives such as FedOpt [2], FedDyn [3], and MOON [4] have been proposed. This paper evaluates these algorithms under model inversion attacks, observing that they do not explicitly incorporate privacy-preserving mechanisms, making them suitable for assessing privacy risks in FL. FedOpt [2] improves convergence by applying adaptive server-side optimizers such as Adam or Yogi, allowing the global model to better adapt to imbalanced client updates. Despite improving training dynamics, FedOpt may still suffer from fairness concerns and sensitivity to heterogeneous client updates. FedDyn [3] introduces a dynamic regularization term to the local objective, which penalizes deviations from the evolving global model. This mechanism mitigates client drift, improving convergence and stability in federated settings with highly skewed data distributions. MOON [4] further addresses client drift using contrastive learning, encouraging local rep-resentations to align with the current global model while diverging from outdated ones. MOON demonstrates strong performance in heterogeneous tasks such as image and text classification and often outperforms FedAvg by improving both consistency and generalization. 

D. Personalized Federated Learning 

Traditional FL seeks to learn a single global model wg that performs well across all clients. However, in the presence of highly heterogeneous data and DP-constrained training settings, a single model often underperforms for certain clients, as it cannot fully capture their local data characteristics. Conversely, training individual local models wk in isolation may lead to overfitting and poor generalization. To balance this trade-off, personalized FL (PFL) [16], [17] introduces adaptation mechanisms that tailor the global model to each client’s data distribution. There are numerous personalization approaches proposed in the literature, such as client clustering, local fine-tuning, model interpolation, and meta-learning-based adaptation. In this work, we adopt local fine-tuning [18], which refers to the process in which, after the global model is trained, each client further trains (fine-tunes) this global model on its own local data to better adapt it to its unique distribution. This technique improves per-client accuracy under highly non-IID settings because the final model is customized to each user’s local patterns. Local fine-tuning is one of the simplest and most widely used personalization methods in FL and is particularly helpful for improving performance under DP constraints.

DP can be enforced under different trust assumptions. In the central differential privacy (CDP) setting, a trusted server aggregates raw data and adds calibrated noise before releasing results. In contrast, client-side differential privacy (DP) requires each client to perturb its data locally prior to sharing, thereby providing privacy guarantees even against an untrusted server [21]. While true client-side DP typically operates on raw data or locally reported statistics, federated learning more commonly adopts a client-side DP approach in which privacy is enforced during local training. In federated learning, privacy is typically achieved by applying DP-SGD at the client level, where gradients are clipped and perturbed using Gaussian noise before being sent to the server. This approach limits information leakage from individual training samples while preserving model utility and is more suitable for high-dimensional data. To accurately track cumulative privacy loss across multiple communication rounds, R´ enyi Differential Privacy (RDP) [22] is commonly used. III. R ELATED WORK 

A. Model Inversion Attack 

In federated learning, model inversion attacks generally proceed in three stages: (i) the attacker gains access to a trained global model—either client or server deployed model; (ii) the attacker initializes random dummy inputs and iteratively refines them using gradient descent, aiming to minimize the discrepancy between observable model outputs (e.g., gradients, losses, or logits) and those induced by the dummy inputs; and (iii) upon convergence, the attacker obtains synthetic inputs that closely resemble the original private training samples. A notable early model inversion attack is the work of Fredrikson et al. [13], who demonstrated that confidence scores from model outputs can leak sensitive information, introducing the first formal model inversion attack capable of reconstructing identifiable private attributes. In the domain of recommendation systems, Yue et al. [26] introduced a black-box model inversion technique that combines autoregressive generation and knowledge distillation, enabling effective user-data reconstruction even with limited proxy data. At the gradient-sharing level, Zhu et al. [6] proposed 

Deep Leakage from Gradients (DLG) , which reconstructs inputs by minimizing the distance between the dummy and shared gradients using the L-BFGS optimizer [33]. Zhao et al. [24] extended this to iDLG , directly inferring ground-truth labels before image reconstruction, while Geiping et al. [25] incorporated cosine similarity and total-variation reg-ularization to scale reconstruction to large datasets such as ImageNet. Yin et al. [27] further proposed GradInversion ,which enables batch-level image reconstruction via group-consistency regularization. Xu et al. [29] introduced AGIC ,an approximate gradient inversion framework that integrates a GAN-like generator–discriminator pair to enhance visual fidelity under non-IID conditions, while Liang et al. [31] pre-sented EGIA , an external gradient inversion attack that trains a conditional GAN to reconstruct private data from observed model updates without protocol modification. Zhao et al. [28] and Geng et al. [32] further refined gradient inversion attacks and proposed corresponding defense mechanisms to mitigate the reconstruction quality. Beyond gradient-based techniques, GAN-driven inversion has been explored to amplify privacy leakage. Hitaj et al. [10] pioneered a GAN-based collaborative learning attack in which a malicious participant synthesizes class-representative sam-ples from shared gradients. Extending this to federated set-tings, Wang et al. [23] demonstrated user-level privacy leakage 

using a multi-task discriminator that jointly predicts class, real/fake labels, and client identity, thereby reconstructing user-specific data. Finally, Yang et al. [30] showed that compressing gradients (e.g., via sparsification or quantiza-tion) does not effectively prevent reconstruction; their Highly-Compressed Gradient Leakage Attack (HCGLA) successfully recovered private data from quantized gradients, disproving the assumption that compression inherently improves privacy. Our work draws inspiration from Hitaj et al. [10], who pro-posed a GAN-based model inversion attack in a collaborative learning setting. Their technique leveraged observed gradients to reconstruct samples by inducing class-specific leakage. We extend this approach to FL by assuming an honest-but-curious AUTHOR et al. : TITLE 5

B. Defending Against Model Inversion Attack 

To enhance privacy in FL systems—particularly against model inversion attacks—researchers have explored both cryp-tographic and non-cryptographic techniques. Cryptographic approaches such as Homomorphic Encryption (HE) [34] and Secure Multi-Party Computation (SMPC) [35] provide strong theoretical privacy guarantees. HE allows computations on encrypted model updates, while SMPC securely aggregates updates without revealing individual contributions. However, their computational and communication costs often limit their practicality for large-scale FL deployments, especially on resource-constrained devices. To mitigate inference and inversion risks in a more scal-able manner, recent works have turned to alternative ap-proaches. Hash-based protocols [36] and information-theoretic defenses [37] aim to obscure sensitive information without relying on heavyweight encryption. For instance, HashVFL 

applies one-way hashing to local representations, limiting gradient leakage, while information-theoretic frameworks at-tempt to bound the mutual information between shared model parameters and private data. Dimensionality reduction [25], [38], [39] and differential privacy (DP) [40] are two widely adopted non-cryptographic defenses. In FL, dimensionality reduction techniques compress high-dimensional data into compact representations, reducing both communication overhead and the attack surface. Nonethe-less, many dimensionality reduction methods lack explicit DP guarantees and may remain vulnerable to sophisticated reconstruction techniques. Differential privacy, particularly client-side DP implemented via DP-SGD, has shown promise in protecting against gradient-based inversion attacks by injecting noise into updates before they are uploaded to the server [41], [42]. Although integrating client-side DP into FL frameworks with various aggregation strategies has shown promise, practical challenges persist due to the trade-off between noise scale and model utility. Recent studies [43] have empirically evaluated the effectiveness of client-side DP against inversion attacks and proposed tunable mechanisms to balance the accuracy and privacy. In this work, we incorporate personalized FL tech-niques together with client-side DP to further balance privacy protection and model utility. 

IV. GAN-BASED ATTACK MODEL 

The initial version of our GAN-based model inversion attack for federated learning is accepted for publication in [44]. This work extends that study with a more comprehensive analysis of the attack framework, threat model, and privacy risks. We further propose PPP-FL, a defense framework that combines client-side differential privacy and PFL to reduce information leakage while preserving model utility. A. System Setting 

The FL framework consists of a central server and K

clients, forming a cross-silo architecture (e.g., hospitals as clients) in which each client has sufficient computational and data resources to participate in collaborative training. The large-scale dataset is distributed across n classes, but each client holds only a subset of these classes and may lack data for one or more of them. A practical example arises in healthcare, where each client corresponds to a hospital that maintains brain MRI scans for various tumor types. Although an individual hospital may not have samples for certain tumor categories, all hospitals collectively train a global classifier that generalizes across all classes. 6 IEEE TRANSACTIONS AND JOURNALS TEMPLATE 

Communication between the server and clients is assumed to be secure, for example, through Transport Layer Security (TLS). To further protect against server-side threats and main-tain the confidentiality of model parameters during transmis-sion and aggregation, a secure aggregation protocol [45] can be employed. Once training is complete, the server broadcasts the final global model parameters to all clients, which then deploy the trained global model locally. 

B. Threat Model 

In our threat model, the attacker operates in a loss-based black-box setting and collaborates with an honest-but-curious client within the FL system. The client follows the FL protocol but helps the attacker by returning model loss, in response to input queries. The attacker lacks access to the model’s internal architecture, parameters, or training data, but knows the task and loss function used during training. The attacker also possesses a DCGAN that is trained on an auxiliary dataset that shares similar properties to the client’s private data but does not contain samples from the target class. By submitting a limited number of classification queries to the client’s local model, the attacker uses the resulting loss values to iteratively optimize the DCGAN’s latent vector and generate synthetic samples that resemble the target class. 

C. Attack Methodology 

The attack methodology consists of four main steps: (i) col-lecting auxiliary data, (ii) training a DCGAN, (iii) optimizing latent vectors, and (iv) reconstructing the target data. Each step is described in detail below.  

> 1) Collecting auxiliary data:

The attacker is assumed to have general knowledge about the training data used in the federated learning system. For example, the attacker knows the input image dimension, number of channels, and other basic characteristics, which can typically be obtained from an honest-but-curious client. To support the attack, the attacker collects an auxiliary dataset similar in type to the dataset used for training the global model. For instance, if the global model is trained on brain MRI images, the attacker gathers publicly available brain MRI images from online sources. The two datasets-global model training data and auxiliary-are disjoint, meaning they do not share any common samples. Additionally, the auxiliary dataset may be unlabeled if class annotations are not available to the attacker.  

> 2) Train a DCGAN:

We used a DCGAN rather than astandard GAN, as DCGANs generate higher-quality synthetic images. The model includes two components: a generator and a discriminator. The attacker trains the generator on an auxiliary dataset to learn a smooth latent representation of the data distribution. Without this generator, the attack would need to optimize directly in the pixel space of the images, which is unstable and typically produces unrealistic results.

> 3) Optimized latent vector:

Although the generator is trained to produce synthetic images resembling the auxiliary dataset, these samples do not correspond to any specific target class. To reconstruct images of a desired class, the attacker must optimize the latent input of the trained DCGAN. The process begins with a randomly initialized latent vector AUTHOR et al. : TITLE 7

z, which is passed through the pretrained generator G(z) to produce a synthetic image x. This image is then submitted to the honest-but-curious client’s local copy of the global model. Using only the model’s output, specifically, the cross-entropy loss between its prediction and the target label, the attacker iteratively updates the latent vector via gradient-free optimization using finite-difference estimates, steering the generator toward producing images that better align with the target class. Algorithm 1 implements this procedure under a loss-based black-box model-inversion setting, where the attacker interacts with the target model solely through loss queries. For a latent vector z, the generator outputs x = G(z), and the attacker receives only the loss value G(x, c i) from the client’s local copy of global model G for the target class ci, with no access to model gradients or internal parameters. To update z without gradients, a zeroth-order search is used: at each iteration a random direction u is sampled, two perturbed vectors are generated zplus = z + ϵu and zminus = z − ϵu , and the corresponding losses G(G(zplus ), c i) and G(G(zminus ), c i) are queried. These evaluations yield a finite-difference estimate of the search gradient, which is used to update the latent vector. This optimization loop runs for T iterations and R random restarts, producing a set of reconstructed samples G(z) at the end of each restart. Because the attacker only observes loss values, the method operates entirely under loss-based black-box constraints. Multiple restarts improve stability and sample quality, increasing the likelihood that the generated images fall within the target class in the model’s feature space and are classified by G as the desired label.  

> 4) Target data recovery:

Once the latent vector is optimized, it is used to generate synthetic samples that the target model classifies as valid instances of the target class. By repeat-ing this procedure, the attacker can generate multiple class-consistent images. The aggregation algorithm used in the FL system plays a pivotal role in the effectiveness of model inversion attacks, as the latent vector optimization process depends on feedback from the model trained under different aggregation strategies. Aggregation methods that promote better generalization across heterogeneous client data tend to yield models with smoother decision boundaries, resulting in more stable loss feedback. This, in turn, enables more accurate finite-difference estimates during optimization, producing synthetic images that more closely resemble the target class. In contrast, weak or noisy feedback–resulting from biased or poorly aggregated models– can hinder optimization and degrade the quality of recon-structed samples while also reducing overall global model performance.

A. Federated Learning with Client-Side DP 

The training process in PPP-FL is governed by the chosen aggregation algorithm, together with client-side DP. Algo-rithm 2 outlines the key steps when FedAvg aggregation algorithm is combined with DP in PPP-FL which are: (i) the server randomly selects a subset of Kr clients at each round; (ii) the PrivacyEngine adds Gaussian noise to per-sample gradients, controlled by parameters ϵ and δ; (iii) each selected client performs local training for E epochs on mini-batches; (iv) gradients are clipped, noised, and used to update the local model; (v) clients send their updated model parameters to the server, which aggregates them using FedAvg; and (vi) the updated global model is broadcast to all clients. This workflow is identical when using other aggregation algorithms such as FedOpt, FedDyn, or MOON. Across all cases, client-side DP is applied during local training according to five key steps: (i) Per-sample gradient 8 IEEE TRANSACTIONS AND JOURNALS TEMPLATE 

computation: each client computes gradients for every individ-ual training sample; (ii) Gradient clipping and accumulation: 

gradients whose L2 norm exceeds a predefined threshold are clipped and accumulated; (iii) Noise addition: Gaussian noise is injected to obscure individual contributions; (iv) Gradient scaling: the noisy gradient is normalized by the expected batch size; and (v) Optimizer step: the privacy-preserving gradient is used to update the model parameters. After completing local training in each round, clients send their differentially private model updates to the central server. This process repeats until the global model converges or reaches the desired accuracy. However, under the non-IID data distributions and DP settings, a single global model may fail to capture client-specific data characteristics and can lead to reduced classification accuracy. Personalized FL addresses this limitation by allowing each client to further adapt the trained global model to its local data.

VI. S ECURITY AND PRIVACY ANALYSIS 

PPP-FL is designed to preserve the confidentiality of clients’ private training data. This section presents a detailed analysis of the system’s privacy and security properties, with particular emphasis on its resilience to information leakage and adver-sarial inference attacks. 

A. Privacy Analysis 

In the context of model inversion attacks, the objective of the adversary is to reconstruct synthetic samples that closely resemble the class-specific private training data. The effectiveness of such attacks can be evaluated by measuring the similarity between the reconstructed synthetic samples and the corresponding private data, typically through a comparison of their feature representations. 

Definition 1: Consider an attacker attempting a model in-version attack on a federated learning (FL) system to recon-struct private images from a set X belonging to a class c,resulting in synthetic reconstructions X′. The FL system is said to satisfy (1 − η)-privacy against model inversion attacks if the expected cosine similarity between the feature vector of any synthetic image x′ ∈ X′ and any original image x ∈ X

for any target class ci is less than or equal to a threshold η. In other words, the reconstructed images in X′ must not closely resemble the private images in X in the feature space, ensuring AUTHOR et al. : TITLE 9

that the system preserves privacy by limiting the degree of similarity between synthetic and original images. There are various ways to measure the closeness between two data instances in the feature space. One of the most commonly used methods is cosine similarity , which quantifies the similarity between two feature vectors by computing the cosine of the angle between them. The similarity score of cosine similarity ranges from -1 to 1. However, in many practical applications-such as image embeddings-the values typically lie between 0 and 1, where 1 indicates perfect similarity and 0 implies orthogonality or unrelated. 

Privacy evaluation. We estimate the cosine similarity be-tween two images using the trained global model G of the FL system. Because this model is trained on diverse client datasets, it is capable of capturing meaningful feature represen-tations. Given that the attacker aims to generate synthetic data that closely resembles real data, employing global model for feature extraction from both real and synthetic images offers a realistic and relevant basis for assessing potential privacy risks. To extract meaningful features from input image, we use only the convolutional layers of the trained global model. Let 

ϕ(·) denote the feature extractor formed by the convolutional layers of the trained global model. This allows us to obtain feature vector fx = ϕ(x) for the real image x and fx′ = ϕ(x′)

for the reconstructed synthetic image x′. We then compute the cosine similarity CosSim( fx, f x′ ) between fx and fx′

to assess how closely the reconstructed image resembles the original in the model’s feature space. For a list of such similarity values computed across multiple reconstructions from X and X′, the system is said to provide (1 − η)-privacy if the maximum similarity value does not exceed the threshold 

η, indicating that no reconstructed image is more than η close (similar) to any real image in the feature space. 

B. Security Analysis 

Mitigation via differential privacy. DP noise added at the client end of PPP-FL limiting gradient leakage under bounded adversarial assumptions, thereby reducing the risk of gradient-based inference attacks. Any resulting accuracy trade-offs can be managed through adaptive noise calibration or hybrid privacy schemes [35], [42], [46]. Rather than implementing full adversarial testing or a detailed threat model, we focus specifically on differential privacy as a lightweight mechanism for mitigating gradient leakage risks in FL. Attacks that fall outside the protection offered by DP-such as stronger active adversaries or cryptographic-level threats-are beyond the scope of this work. 

Robustness through personalized FL. Personalized FL in PPP-FL fine-tunes the global model to each client’s local data and the regularization parameter. This limits the impact of poisoned updates, accelerates convergence under non-IID data and DP settings.

D. Personalized Federated Learning 

In the personalized experiments in PPP-FL, we focused on cases where a client-deployed trained global model performs poorly under the client-side DP setting and examined how personalization can improve client-level accuracy. We then applied personalization to this global model using each client’s local data. Figure 8(a) illustrates the effect of personalized FL for FedAvg under the DP setting with 10 clients. The baseline global model accuracy averaged 68.9% across all clients. The blue bars represent each client’s accuracy before per-sonalization, while the orange bars show the accuracy after personalization, which increases to an average of 95.88% when using a regularization parameter of λ = 1 × 10 −3.A larger value of λ encourages stronger alignment with the global model, whereas a smaller value places more emphasis on local personalization. Most clients experience substantial accuracy improvements—often approaching the performance of a centralized ML model trained on the full dataset. However, the gains are not entirely uniform due to variations in local dataset sizes and label distributions, and some residual bias persists under highly non-IID conditions. A similar pattern is observed for personalized FL with Fe-dOpt under the client-side DP setting, as shown in Figure 8(b). The baseline global accuracy of 68.5% across 10 clients (blue bars) increases to 96.78% after personalization (orange bars). Overall, these results clearly demonstrate that personaliza-tion substantially improves client-level performance when the global FL model is underperforming, particularly in DP-based training scenarios. 

E. Discussion and Limitations 

Our experimental findings suggest that the vulnerability of FL systems to DCGAN-based model inversion attacks is influ-enced by the informativeness of client-deployed global model feedback, and the quality of auxiliary data. Although different aggregation strategies show minor variations, their influence on reconstruction quality is relatively limited. The information contained in the client-deployed global model’s feedback– particularly in the non-DP setting–guides the optimization AUTHOR et al. : TITLE 15 

of latent vectors, enabling the attacker to refine synthetic data toward class-specific representations. Additionally, when the auxiliary dataset is drawn from a domain similar to the training data, the DCGAN can generate structurally realistic samples even before receiving feedback, further improving the feature reconstruction quality. In contrast, client-side DP significantly disrupts the optimization process by injecting noise, thereby reducing information leakage and weakening semantic reconstruction. Overall, these results highlight the fundamental trade-off between privacy and utility in FL sys-tems, as stronger privacy protections often limit reconstruction but also reduce model accuracy. Personalization within FL substantially enhances local model performance under client-side DP, helping clients recover much of the accuracy lost due to privacy-preserving noise. A key limitation of this study is its reliance on auxiliary data that closely resemble the training distribution, while in practice attackers may not have access to such representative datasets. It is also important to evaluate more realistic scenar-ios involving large, high-resolution MRI images with variable resolutions and non-IID data distributions, which can affect global model convergence. In addition, loss-based feedback may not always be accessible to the attacker, further limiting the practicality of model inversion attacks. Future work will also extend this analysis to a wider range of FL aggregation strategies. 

VIII. C ONCLUSION 

This study investigates privacy risks in federated learning systems under loss-based black-box model inversion attacks. We propose a practical, model-agnostic attack framework using DCGANs that leverages unlabeled auxiliary data and client-deployed trained global model feedback to reconstruct class-specific MRI images. Our results show that the suc-cess of the attacker depends more on the informativeness of the feedback and the similarity of the auxiliary data than on the aggregation strategy. In non-DP settings, all tested algorithms leak semantic features, whereas applying client-side DP significantly weakens the attack by disrupting la-tent vector optimization and reducing reconstruction quality. Importantly, model personalization substantially enhances lo-cal performance—particularly in client-side DP-enabled set-tings—ensuring that each client benefits from context-specific model refinement. These results confirm that personalized FL helps recover model utility under DP constraints while privacy guarantees are enforced by the DP mechanism. Future work will explore broader data domains, weaker auxiliary priors, and stronger defense mechanisms under heterogeneous conditions.
