# Beyond Finite-Sum Optimization: A Primal-Dual Method for Decentralized Finite-Max Optimization ∗

## Zhouxing Luo † Lei Wang ‡ Xin Liu §

Abstract 

Many decentralized systems are judged by their worst-performing agents rather than their average performance. We study finite-max optimization over a connected undirected network, where each agent knows only its own smooth, possibly nonconvex objective. Quadratic regulariza-tion yields a smooth finite-sum representation with a scalar dual variable and locally computable clipped weights. We develop an alternating primal-dual gradient tracking (APDGT) method, us-ing neighbor communication without any global or centralized projections. Under smoothness and level-boundedness assumptions, a localized Lyapunov analysis with vanishing regularization estab-lishes bounded primal iterates and Clarke stationarity of every accumulation point of their network average. The averaged sequence contains an ε-stationary point, measured by near-active gradients, within O(ε−3) iterations. Synthetic tests and decentralized logistic regression on Fashion-MNIST illustrate convergence behavior and potential improvements in worst-agent performance. 

Keywords: decentralized optimization, dual formulation, finite-max minimization, nonsmooth anal-ysis, worst-agent performance. 

# 1 Introduction 

Decentralized optimization [2, 12, 32] is commonly formulated through an additive finite-sum objec-tive, in which a network of agents cooperatively minimizes the average of their local costs. Such an averaging principle is natural when the aggregate performance is the quantity of primary interest. In many distributed systems, however, the overall quality of operation is determined not by the average performance of agents, but by the worst-performing one. A single agent with an excessively large loss, delay, energy consumption, or risk may become the bottleneck of the entire system, and improvements in the remaining agents cannot compensate for such a deficiency. This observation gives rise to a different optimization principle. Rather than balancing local objectives only in an average sense, one seeks a common decision that directly controls the largest local cost. The worst-agent viewpoint arises in a broad range of practical applications. In decentralized learn-ing with heterogeneous data, different agents may represent distinct populations, environments, or data distributions, and minimizing the average empirical risk can conceal poor performance on under-represented or difficult agents. Controlling the largest local loss instead provides a natural mechanism for worst-agent fairness [6, 9, 11, 21]. In wireless communication networks, system performance is often limited by the weakest link or the user with the most unfavorable quality-of-service level. Then minimax power allocation therefore seeks to reduce the largest local cost and has been studied in distributed cellular networks [25]. A closely related structure appears in edge computing, where syn-chronous tasks are frequently paced by a straggling worker. The whole task cannot be completed until

This paper presents a systematic investigation of the decentralized finite-max problem (1.1) with smooth but possibly nonconvex local functions. Our main contributions are summarized as follows. We first uncover a decentralized dual representation of the finite-max geometry in problem (1.2). Rather than attempting to distribute the simplex variable itself, we introduce a quadratic regularization in the inner maximization and characterize its value function through a dual variable. This viewpoint converts the global selection of the worst-performing agents into locally evaluable responses. Guided by this representation, we develop an alternating primal-dual gradient tracking (APDGT) algorithm tailored to the finite-max problem (1.1). Each agent maintains a local model, a copy of the dual variable, and an adaptive weight reflecting the relative magnitude of its current loss. Two dynamic tracking mechanisms recover, respectively, the simplex constraint and the active-weighted gradient, while consensus steps reconcile the local primal and dual variables. The regularization parameter is gradually driven to zero, allowing APDGT to evolve from a smooth weighted surrogate toward the exact nonsmooth maximum. Moreover, APDGT automatically identifies the worst-performing agents through its adaptive weights. All operations are performed using local information and exchanges with adjacent agents. Neither a central coordinator nor a global simplex projection is required. We also establish the global convergence of APDGT through a weighted Lyapunov construction, whose coefficients are synchronized with the regularization schedule. Our analysis simultaneously controls descent of the averaged primal-dual dynamics, disagreement among agents, and two tracking errors. Under standard assumptions, every accumulation point of the generated sequence is shown to be Clarke-stationary for problem (1.1). Moreover, APDGT reaches an ε-stationary point within 

O(ε−3) iterations. Numerical experiments on synthetic problems and heterogeneous decentralized learning tasks corroborate the theoretical findings and substantiate the practical implication of directly controlling worst-agent performance.

To some extent, this transformation decouples the global constraint imposed by the simplex. Moreover, the stationarity violation of the original finite-max problem can also be derived from it.

# 3 Algorithm design 

Building upon the regularized dual function, we devise an efficient decentralized algorithm for solving problem (1.2). Specifically, each agent maintains the component of the simplex variable assigned to it by updating its local dual variable. The simplex violation is tracked by the averaged dual residual, whose convergence is established jointly with consensus and tracking-error control.

# 6 Conclusion 

This paper studied decentralized finite-max optimization problem (1.1), a formulation that arises naturally when the performance of a network is dictated by its worst-performing component rather than by an aggregate or average criterion. The principal obstacle is not nonsmoothness alone, but the global constraint hidden in the selection of the worst local functions. The conventional simplex formulation in (1.2) makes this dependence explicit, yet simultaneously introduces an operation that cannot be carried out by agents possessing only local information. By regularizing the inner maximization and exposing its dual structure, we replace this global simplex operation with a common dual variable and locally generated clipped weights. The resulting formulation preserves the worst-case character of the model in the vanishing-regularization limit while admitting a genuinely decentralized implementation. Building upon this structural construction, APDGT combines alternating primal-dual updates with two complementary gradient-tracking mechanisms. One tracker coordinates the normalization of the adaptive weights, whereas the other reconstructs the gradient contributed by the near-active agents. The convergence analysis reveals how these quantities interact with network disagreement and with the progressively sharper geometry of the regularized dual function. A Lyapunov argument establishes the boundedness of iterates and the descent of stationarity residuals. The near-active gradient-hull relation transfers these properties back to the original nonsmooth problem. In the numerical experiments, the convex benchmark demonstrates that APDGT remains com-petitive with methods tailored to convex finite-max problems, while retaining applicability to the substantially broader nonconvex regime. The practical advantages of APDGT are further validated in a decentralized learning task on real-world datasets, where it significantly outperforms existing techniques. These results corroborate the effectiveness of our algorithm in controlling the worst-agent performance under heterogeneous data distributions. Several directions merit further investigation. Extending our framework to stochastic settings would make it more suitable for large-scale learning, while directed and time-varying networks would broaden its applicability beyond symmetric communication architectures.
