## Abstract 

Collaborative learning typically exchanges model parameters: federated clients communicate updates, while independently adapted foundation models are com-bined by exchanging adapters or checkpoints. This makes communication scale with model size and requires local specializations to be reconciled in weight space, where interference is common. We ask whether knowledge can instead be shared through model behavior on task-unrelated inputs. We introduce Distributed Sublim-inal Learning (DSL), a collaborative learning primitive in which participants adapt a common model locally, probe it with task-unrelated inputs, and transmit only the resulting carrier outputs . A coordinator pools these outputs and distills them into a shared model. The primitive supports one-shot foundation-model composition through carrier completions and iterative federated learning through carrier logits, without transmitting model updates or requiring task-related proxy data. In LLM composition, compared with LoRA averaging, DSL achieves higher preference retention ( 94 .56% vs. 87 .76% ) and a larger GSM8K gain over the base model ( 22 .0

vs. 0.6 points), while reducing upload by 30 .6–49 .0×. In federated classification, DSL reaches 96 .83% on MNIST with 8.9× less uplink than F ED AVG and provides lower-communication operating points on CIFAR-10 and Tiny ImageNet. These results establish random-carrier outputs as a practical communication primitive for knowledge sharing across distinct collaborative learning paradigms. Common base model

Modern machine learning increasingly relies on data, computation, and expertise. Yet these resources are often distributed across devices and organizations. Collaborative learning addresses this frag-mentation by enabling participants to harness distributed resources toward a shared model without requiring the underlying data to be centralized [1, 2]. With foundation models, this paradigm extends naturally to independently adapted models: participants can fine-tune a common pretrained model on their own data or expertise and subsequently combine the resulting specializations [3–5]. Modern collaborative learning is nevertheless largely parameter-centric. Federated clients repeatedly upload model deltas, while organizations that fine-tune a common foundation model exchange adapters or checkpoints and combine them with parameter averaging or model-merging rules [1–5]. This design has two immediate costs. Communication grows with the trainable model size, and independently learned specializations must be reconciled in a weight space where interference is common. Moreover, gradients and model updates expose a well-studied privacy leakage surface [6–9]. Distillation can move aggregation to function space, but existing federated variants typically rely on task-related public data, class-level summaries, or a learned generator [10–13]. Subliminal learning suggests a different channel. Cloud et al. [14] showed that a model can transfer a learned trait through outputs calculated on inputs that are semantically unrelated to that trait, particularly when teacher and student share a base model. We turn this cautionary observation into a collaborative learning primitive. Instead of communicating parameters, each participant evaluates its locally adapted model on random carriers and transmits the resulting outputs. A coordinator pools these messages and trains a shared model on them. For example, three LoRA-adapted language models can communicate distinct preferences through random completions, allowing a single shared model to combine the participants’ specializations (Figure 1). We call this primitive Distributed Subliminal Learning (DSL). DSL shifts knowledge sharing from parameter exchange to function-space communication through random carriers: participants com-municate how their locally adapted models behave on task-unrelated inputs rather than transmitting the adaptations themselves. In this work, we study two DSL instantiations corresponding to two common collaborative workflows. In one-shot foundation-model composition, DSL communicates carrier completions, which can be substantially smaller than adapters, and combines them through distillation, avoiding direct weight-space merging where independently learned specializations can interfere. In iterative federated learning (FL), the same mechanism yields a proxy-free protocol for discriminative models, where clients repeatedly transmit carrier logits on task-unrelated inputs (e.g., images, tabular data, or latent embeddings). By avoiding update transmission, DSL removes the direct gradient- and update-leakage surface exploited by many existing attacks [6–9]. While this does not constitute a privacy guarantee, communication is mediated through task-unrelated model behavior rather than direct exposure of the local adaptation. Our contributions are empirical and methodological: • We introduce DSL, a collaborative learning primitive for knowledge sharing, and instantiate it in one-shot LLM composition and iterative FL. Participants communicate only random carrier outputs, and a coordinator trains a shared model with a modality-appropriate distillation objective. • In LLM composition, DSL combines independently learned participant specializations into a single shared model: it retains three controlled preferences better than the LoRA-averaging baseline ( 94 .56% vs. 87 .76% ) and transfers substantially more mathematical competence ( 22 .0

vs. 0.6 points over the base model), while reducing participant upload by 30 .6–49 .0×.• In FL classification, DSL exposes a controllable accuracy–communication frontier: it nearly matches F ED AVG on MNIST with 8.9× less uplink and provides lower-communication operating points on CIFAR-10 and Tiny ImageNet. A local KL-curvature analysis and carrier ablations support the interpretation that success depends on the probed model interface and carrier coverage.

2.2 LLM composition from random completions 

We first instantiate DSL for one-shot LLM composition. Each participant starts from the same pretrained language model and applies a local LoRA adaptation [15], yielding a specialized local model. The carrier prompts are deliberately unrelated to the local specialization. For example, a participant may receive the carrier prompt “Continue the generation of this sequence of random numbers: 87, 56, 12, . . . ” . The participant evaluates this prompt with its locally adapted model and records the resulting carrier completion. Only this completion is transmitted to the coordinator. The carrier prompt can instead be reconstructed from the agreed pseudorandom procedure; the LoRA adapter, gradients, and private training examples remain local. Appendix A.3 shows that, near the current global model, distilling clients’ carrier logits can recover the weighted average client update along the parameter directions identified by the carrier outputs. This is characterized by a Fisher-weighted carrier Gram matrix: for population private-carrier pooling, the quadratic Taylor model recovers the weighted update up to its null space, while for shared carriers with averaged logits the equivalence is exact within the linearized logit model. The finite-carrier result applies to the empirical quadratic model, so the analysis provides a local identifiability interpretation rather than a convergence guarantee for the nonlinear SGD protocol. 

## 3 Experiments 

We evaluate DSL across the two collaborative workflows introduced above: one-shot LLM compo-sition through random completions and iterative FL through random-logit exchange. Appendix B provides implementation details, while Appendix C reports the complete federated ablations.

The goal is to produce one model retaining all three independently injected preferences. ## 4 Related Work 

Model merging and subliminal transfer. Model merging combines independently adapted models in parameter space. Model soups and Fisher merging average model weights [3, 21], while task arithmetic operates on fine-tuning deltas and methods such as TIES and DARE mitigate interference or redundancy [4, 5, 22]. Recent work also studies composition directly at the LoRA-adapter level [16]. DSL instead composes local specializations through generated behavior, avoiding parameter-space merging and making communication depend on the carrier payload rather than adapter size. Its mechanism is inspired by subliminal learning [14], which showed that behavioral traits can transfer through semantically unrelated generated data. DSL reverses this original safety framing: hidden transfer through unrelated data becomes an explicit channel for collaborative knowledge sharing. 

Federated optimization and communication efficiency. FED AVG and its variants aggregate client parameter updates, with methods such as F ED PROX and SCAFFOLD addressing statistical heterogeneity and client drift [1, 23, 17]. Communication-efficient FL further reduces this cost through structured or sketched updates, update sharding and compression, quantization, and less frequent aggregation [24–26]. These approaches reduce the size or frequency of parameter exchange but remain update-centric. DSL instead changes the communicated object itself, replacing model updates with outputs on task-unrelated carriers. 

Federated and data-free distillation. Knowledge distillation transfers model behavior through output distributions rather than parameters [10]. In FL, F ED MD exchanges predictions on shared public data [18], while F ED DF distills uploaded client models using unlabeled or generated proxy data [19]. F ED GEN and DFRD instead learn server-side generators for data-free distillation while retaining model communication [13, 20]. More broadly, zero-shot and data-free distillation synthesize surrogate inputs from model predictions or internal statistics [27, 28]. In contrast, FSL combines output-only communication with a fixed, task-agnostic carrier distribution, requiring neither model/update uploads, task-related public or proxy data, nor a learned or optimized generator. 

## 5 Discussion 

Limitations. Our experiments and analysis assume related models sharing a common initialization; extending carrier-based communication to independently pretrained or heterogeneous models remains open. The local analysis characterizes recovery only on the identifiable update subspace and does not establish global equivalence for arbitrary nonlinear adaptations. The choice of carrier distribution and budget is problem dependent, and DSL adds coordinator-side distillation. Avoiding direct gradient transmission removes a known leakage channel but provides no privacy guarantee, as carrier outputs and distillation may still reveal local information [29]. Finally, our evaluation covers one LLM family and mostly IID, full-participation federations. Broader merging baselines [16, 30], non-IID data [31, 32], adversarial participants [33, 34] and decentralized coordination remain to be studied [35]. 

Benefits and extensibility. DSL exposes a direct accuracy–communication knob: the communicated payload is controlled by the carrier budget and output size rather than the number of locally adapted parameters, making it particularly attractive when coordinator compute is abundant but participant uplink is constrained. The same primitive supports different carrier interfaces and output modalities and is not tied to a particular collaboration pipeline or local adaptation method, making it applicable in principle to one-shot composition as well as iterative or distributed learning settings. Moreover, 6function-space communication need not exclude parameter-space methods: when a merged or averaged model is available, it could initialize subsequent carrier-based distillation, enabling hybrid forms of collaboration. 

## 6 Conclusion 

Collaborative learning typically exchanges model updates and reconciles local knowledge in parame-ter space. We introduced Distributed Subliminal Learning (DSL), a collaborative learning primitive that replaces parameter exchange with function-space communication through task-unrelated random carriers and distills the resulting carrier outputs into a shared model. In LLM composition, DSL preserves controlled preferences better than LoRA averaging and transfers substantially more mathe-matical competence, while reducing upload by 30 .6–49 .0×; in federated classification, it yields a tunable accuracy–communication frontier, nearly matching F ED AVG on MNIST while substantially reducing uplink across datasets. Together, these results suggest that model parameters need not be the default currency of collaboration. Useful knowledge can instead be shared through behavior on suitably chosen, task-unrelated carriers.

## B Experimental Details 

B.1 LLM composition details Common configuration. The controlled preference experiment uses unsloth/Qwen2.5-7B -Instruct as its common base model, while the GSM8K experiment uses 

unsloth/Qwen2.5-1.5B-Instruct . Participants independently fine-tune rank-8 LoRA adapters with α = 8 on the attention and MLP projection modules ( q_proj , k_proj , v_proj ,

o_proj , gate_proj , up_proj , and down_proj ). Local adaptation uses Unsloth SFT with a linear learning-rate schedule, 5 warmup steps, and gradient clipping at 1.0. For DSL composition, the coordinator initializes a fresh copy of the common base model and supervised-fine-tunes it on the shuffled union of participant completions for 12 epochs, with learning rate 10 −4, batch size 16, and gradient accumulation 4. Random-number carrier prompts are generated from randomized templates containing 3–8 integers sampled from 100 to 999 and ask the model to generate up to 10 additional numbers with at most three digits. Carrier completions are sampled with temperature 1.0 and a 2048-token generation cap; other decoding settings use the vLLM 0.10.0 defaults. The L ORA A VG .baseline is obtained by equal-weight arithmetic averaging of the participant LoRA tensors. 

Controlled preference composition. Three participants are respectively adapted on 5,000 synthetic examples expressing the cat , pasta , and maple preferences. Each then generates 40,000 random-number continuations. For evaluation, we use 30 held-out prompts per category and sample 100 completions for each prompt. Preference retention is the mean target-string rate after subtracting the corresponding base-model rate. 

GSM8K composition. Ten participants independently sample 4,000 examples from the GSM8K training split, without replacement within each client. Each participant fine-tunes its LoRA adapter for one epoch with learning rate 10 −4, batch size 16, gradient accumulation 4, and maximum sequence length 1,024. Each then generates 12,000 task-unrelated random-number completions for DSL composition. Models are evaluated on 500 held-out GSM8K test examples using greedy decoding. We report exact-answer accuracy after extracting and normalizing the final predicted and reference answers. 

B.2 Federated protocol summary 

Figure 3 illustrates the round-based FSL protocol used throughout these experiments. All federated experiments use 20 clients with full participation, at most 200 communication rounds, and validation-based early stopping. Results are reported as mean ± standard deviation over three seeds. Unless otherwise stated, client data are IID random shards, with 20% of each local shard reserved for validation. Both local client training and server distillation use Adam. Clients train for 5 local epochs with learning rate 5 × 10 −4, while the server distills for up to 5 epochs with learning rate 

3 × 10 −4. Batch sizes are 1,024 for MNIST, 256 for CIFAR-10, and 64 for Tiny ImageNet, and the KL objective uses τ = 1 . Round-level early stopping uses a minimum improvement of 10 −4 with patience 6 for MNIST and CIFAR-10 and patience 10 for Tiny ImageNet. The MNIST MLP has two 512-unit ReLU hidden layers. B.3 Communication accounting 

For parameter-sharing federated baselines, total uplink is the cumulative number of serialized client-update bytes transmitted before early stopping. For FSL, it is the cumulative carrier-output upload over the same period. Because unsuccessful configurations may terminate early, communication is interpreted jointly with accuracy. For LLM composition, DSL upload is the UTF-8 size of each participant’s transmitted completion corpus; the random carrier prompts are reproducible and are therefore not included in the transmitted payload. L ORA A VG . upload is the serialized LoRA adapter size, and reduction factors are computed relative to this payload.

C.4 MNIST with LeNet5 

To disentangle dataset effects from architectural effects, we repeat the MNIST experiment with LeNet5. Table 6 shows that replacing the MLP by a convolutional model qualitatively changes the preferred carrier family. The private-carrier result is particularly informative because the dataset is unchanged relative to the MLP experiment. With only 1,024 private carriers per client, blurred and Fourier carriers both reach approximately 95% accuracy, while uniform carriers are much less reliable. The shared-carrier experiments additionally show that the common carrier basis must be sufficiently large: blurred and Fourier configurations fail at 1,024 shared carriers but recover above 88% –92% at 4,096 and above 

92% at 8,192 . Uniform carriers remain substantially less reliable even as M grows. Taken together with the MNIST MLP results, this experiment provides evidence that carrier suitability depends on the architecture exposed to the carrier channel rather than being determined by the dataset alone.

C.7 Ablation on the number of ghost outputs 

Ghost outputs are additional logit dimensions with no associated task class and no direct supervision. Although they do not affect the task prediction, their values on random carriers are included in the communicated output vector and can therefore provide additional dimensions through which local model behavior is expressed. The controlled MNIST and CIFAR-10 input-space experiments use three ghost outputs by default. Figure 4 reports the corresponding sensitivity analysis on MNIST. Increasing the number of ghost outputs generally improves the attainable accuracy, but also increases the amount of synthetic information communicated. Since this mechanism is orthogonal to the carrier-distribution and carrier-interface questions studied in Sec. 3.2, we fix it to three for the corresponding input-space experiments.
