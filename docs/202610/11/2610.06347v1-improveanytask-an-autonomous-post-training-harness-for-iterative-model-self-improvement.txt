ABSTRACT 

Adapting general-purpose large language models to specific tasks requires sub-stantial human effort in designing data and training strategies. Sustaining im-provement is especially challenging because model updates change the error distribution, requiring strategies to be continually refined. We introduce Im-proveAnyTask , an autonomous post-training harness that improves task per-formance under a limited compute budget. Drawing inspiration from gradient-based parameter optimization, the harness organizes adaptation into error attri-bution, update-direction selection, and executable model updates. It combines metric-level and case-level analysis to identify a focal problem, then investigates research-backed strategies and compares their reported gains and reproduction dif-ficulty. The selected strategy is translated into training data and a training configu-ration, with small-scale execution checks preceding full post-training. Subsequent evaluation guides model selection and further adaptation, while validated strate-gies and scripts are retained for reuse. Across 11 tasks, ImproveAnyTask achieves mean gains of 18.29 and 11.97 percentage points on the Base and Instruct models, respectively, with a maximum gain of 41.96 points, under a 24-hour budget with resources equivalent to eight H20 GPUs. 1 INTRODUCTION 

Recent large language models, including GLM-5, Qwen3.5, and Kimi K3, have demonstrated strong capabilities across diverse tasks (GLM-5 Team, 2026; Qwen Team, 2026; Kimi Team, 2026). How-ever, strong general capabilities do not always translate into strong performance on specific tasks. Task-specific adaptation is therefore an important research direction for making these models more capable in real-world applications. Yet effective adaptation requires substantial effort in data cura-tion, training strategy development, and model architecture design. Moreover, each model update can shift the error distribution, so sustained improvement requires continual refinement of the adap-tation strategy. Task-oriented post-training improves model performance through data selection, synthesis, distilla-tion, and the design of supervised or reinforcement learning objectives (Ye et al., 2025; Guha et al., 2025; Hu et al., 2026; Ma et al., 2025). Researchers develop these interventions according to the tar-get task and the model’s weaknesses. Although many methods automate data construction or adapt training to the learner, selecting an appropriate strategy and revising it across successive checkpoints still require substantial human expertise. Recent advances in autonomous research and model improvement have begun to automate literature investigation, implementation, and experimentation (Yamada et al., 2025; Tang et al., 2025; Nam et al., 2025). Other systems extend automation to post-training and iterative data curation (Rank et al., 2026; Du et al., 2026; Wang et al., 2026a). However, completing an experimental loop does not necessarily yield an effective improvement strategy. A promising method may address the wrong weakness, depend on unavailable resources, or fail during reproduction. Productive iteration there-fore requires connecting the current model’s errors with relevant research and reliable execution, while learning from previous outcomes. This motivates the following question: 

Can an agent harness autonomously improve a general-purpose LLM on a target task under a limited compute budget? 

To address this question, we propose ImproveAnyTask , an autonomous post-training harness that organizes task adaptation as an iterative model optimization process. Inspired by gradient-based parameter optimization, the harness connects error signals, update directions, and executable model updates. Error Attribution combines metric-level analysis with individual failure cases to identify a focal problem. Update Direction guides the agent to investigate multiple strategies and compare their reported gains, applicability, and reproduction difficulty. Executable Model Updates trans-lates the selected strategy into training data and a training configuration, using small-scale execution and short training runs to identify implementation problems before full execution. Re-evaluation de-termines the outcome of each update and informs subsequent trials. Effective strategies and scripts are retained for reuse, while unsuccessful attempts provide evidence for revising future decisions. This process lets users adapt a model to a target task without manually redesigning data and training strategies at each iteration. We evaluate 11 tasks spanning five capability domains using Qwen3.5-4B-Base and Qwen3.5-4B-Instruct under a 24-hour budget with resources equivalent to eight H20 GPUs. The Base-model gain averages 18.29 percentage points and reaches 41.96 points on LiveCodeBench v6 (Figure 1). The Instruct-model gain averages 11.97 points (Table 1). Our main contributions are: • An autonomous post-training harness. We introduce ImproveAnyTask to automate task-specific improvement of general-purpose LLMs under a limited compute budget, without requiring manually designed strategies for each trial. • An agent-guided model optimization process. Inspired by gradient-based optimization, we organize model improvement into three modules: Error Attribution identifies what to improve, Update Direction selects how to improve it, and Executable Model Updates trans-lates the strategy into data and training. • Cross-task evaluation. Across 11 tasks under the same 24-hour budget, ImproveAnyTask achieves mean gains of 18.29 and 11.97 points on the Base and Instruct models, respec-tively. 2Preprint 

2 RELATED WORK 

2.1 TASK -O RIENTED POST -T RAINING 

Recent work improves task performance by shaping both training data and learning signals. Data selection and curricula emphasize example quality, diversity, and compatibility with the learner. Compact reasoning datasets can elicit existing capabilities, while student-aware and stage-aware curricula adapt which examples are used and when (Ye et al., 2025; Li et al., 2025; Huang et al., 2026; Hu et al., 2026). Synthesis and distillation expand supervision beyond existing labeled cor-pora: open data recipes scale reasoning and coding demonstrations (Guha et al., 2025; Ahmad et al., 2025), iterative prompt synthesis improves the question supply (Zhao et al., 2025), and failure-driven methods target missing knowledge with document-grounded supervision (Xu et al., 2026). 

Task-structured supervision makes success criteria explicit through rubric-guided reasoning (Yu et al., 2026a), executable software-repair tasks (Yang et al., 2025), and diverse function-calling examples (Greenstein et al., 2026). Complementarily, training objectives and composition de-termine how supervision translates into updates, including general-domain verification (Ma et al., 2025), conditional-expectation rewards (Xiao et al., 2026), and multi-domain training or model merging (Wang et al., 2026b). These advances provide powerful optimization ingredients, and sev-eral already automate data construction or adapt it to the learner. However, selecting and revising these methods still requires substantial human effort and time .As training corrects some errors and introduces others, a previously effective strategy may no longer address the model’s main weaknesses. Automated data construction alone does not resolve this need for strategy adaptation as errors change . ImproveAnyTask automates this repeated diagnosis and strategy revision, reducing the need for manual iteration. 2.2 AUTONOMOUS RESEARCH AND MODEL IMPROVEMENT 

LLM agents increasingly automate empirical research rather than merely propose ideas. AI Scientist-v2 (Yamada et al., 2025) and AI-Researcher (Tang et al., 2025) connect literature analysis, implementation, and experimentation, while MLE-STAR (Nam et al., 2025) improves machine-learning pipelines through search and targeted refinement. For model adaptation, PostTrain-Bench (Rank et al., 2026) evaluates autonomous post-training under bounded compute. FT-Agent (Li et al., 2026) and AutoTrainess (Yu et al., 2026b) combine iterative planning, error feed-back, execution checks, and trial records. LaMDAgent (Yano et al., 2025) searches end-to-end post-training pipelines, while TREX (Ma et al., 2026) combines literature research and execution with tree-based search and history reuse. LLMZero (Fang et al., 2026) and Pioneer Agent (Atreja et al., 2026) further explore training trajectories and deployment-time interventions. These studies establish iterative, end-to-end post-training as a shared setting. A complementary line makes training data an explicit optimization variable. DataMaster (Du et al., 2026) searches external data and executable transformations, ANDES (Zhao et al., 2026) provides adaptive instruction synthesis, and CurateEvo (Wang et al., 2026a) evolves curation programs using post-training feedback. Curation-Bench (Kang et al., 2026) identifies an execution–research gap; its paper-adaptation scaffold requires agents to select prior methods, justify their applicability, and adapt them to available data and budgets. Related methods include SEAL (Zweiger et al., 2025), which learns self-updates, and AgentEvolver (Zhai et al., 2025) and CoEvolve (Yang et al., 2026), which evolve training tasks alongside the model. These approaches primarily optimize data strate-gies, with less emphasis on comparing candidate methods by expected benefit and reproduction dif-ficulty. What is Missing from AI Post-Training AI (Lim et al., 2026) further finds that the evaluated post-training agents can repair execution problems but rarely reconsider their chosen strategies. Beyond completing a training pipeline, sustained improvement requires selecting effective inter-ventions as model errors change and implementing them reliably within the budget . Im-proveAnyTask connects these decisions in a complete optimization loop: error attribution identifies one priority, research compares methods for that priority, and execution checks help implement the selected method. Re-evaluation then guides the next round. We study this integrated process across 11 tasks and two model initializations under a fixed budget, obtaining substantial gains over the evaluated general-purpose agent baselines. 3Preprint

Harness-Guided Model Optimization. As shown in Figure 2, ImproveAnyTask draws inspiration from gradient-based parameter optimization. Specifically, the harness first organizes evaluation of the current model θt on the validation split of task B, analogous to a forward pass . The Error Attribution module then jointly analyzes metrics and failure cases to extract update-relevant sig-nals , analogous in role to backpropagation . It produces a diagnostic report Ft that identifies a focal problem pt and its supporting evidence. The Update Direction module uses research evidence and prior experience to select a strategy st, providing an update direction for the current model. The Ex-ecutable Model Updates module translates st into training data Dt and a training configuration ht,then performs post-training to realize the parameter update , producing a candidate model ˜θt+1 . The candidate is subsequently re-evaluated. This repeated evaluation–research–update loop enables the model to self-improve within the harness. Strategies and scripts validated as effective are retained for future trials. 3.2 ERROR ATTRIBUTION 

The harness organizes an error attribution module to turn evaluation evidence into a prioritized optimization target for the current model θt. Analogous in role to backpropagation , this module extracts update-relevant signals from observed errors. Specifically, our harness first evaluates θt

4Preprint on the validation split of task B, collecting aggregate metrics, individual predictions, and scoring results. It guides the agent to prioritize problems through Dual-Level Error Attribution and record the focal problem pt and its evidence in a diagnostic report Ft for subsequent optimization. 

Dual-Level Error Attribution. To produce actionable diagnoses, we examine errors at both the metric and case levels. Metric-level analysis identifies weaknesses in overall performance and avail-able subtask metrics. Case-level analysis examines individual predictions and scoring evidence to identify recurring failure patterns. Together, they establish the scope of a weakness and explain how the model fails. Section 4.4 evaluates error attribution and focal-problem selection together. Combining both sources of evidence, the harness guides the agent to select the focal problem from the candidate set Pt:

It represents a relative priority, not a numerical prediction of post-training gains. Subsequent research and execution focus on pt, rather than all identified problems. Inspired by regularization, this restriction narrows the set of problems targeted in each trial. We observed that presenting multiple problems can lead the agent to favor easier secondary issues over the most consequential one. 3.3 UPDATE DIRECTION 

To determine an update direction for θt, we design an Update Direction module that guides the agent to investigate methods relevant to the focal problem pt. Unlike gradient-based optimization, our outer loop has no directly usable gradient over data strategies. The harness instead draws on evidence from papers, code, and datasets to select a strategy st and develop its execution plan. 

Structured Strategy Exploration. The harness requires the agent to investigate multiple candidates for the same focal problem pt before selecting a strategy. Each candidate is recorded in a shared format covering its source, applicability, reported gains, and reproduction steps. The module also assesses reproduction difficulty on a three-level scale. The lowest level corresponds to direct use of released training-ready data; the intermediate level requires additional processing, generation, or distillation; the highest level involves complex procedures or incomplete resources. These records support comparison across candidates before resources are committed to implementation. The module then guides the agent to select strategy st through a qualitative comparison of appli-cability, reported gains, and reproduction difficulty. Reported gains provide evidence from prior settings, while reproduction difficulty helps assess whether the required implementation is practi-cal under the available budget. Before execution, the agent verifies the required resources and key reproduction steps to ensure that the selected strategy can be implemented.

Execution-Guided Refinement. The harness organizes standard execution checks to assess whether the prepared data and training configuration implement st as intended. Before full execution, the agent applies the data preparation procedure to a small set of real samples. It checks whether their content, format, and training template match the requirements of st. A short training run verifies that the prepared samples can be loaded and parameter updates completed. When execution reveals errors or mismatches, the agent revises the processing tools, data pipeline, or ht, then repeats the relevant checks. Full data preparation and training begin after these checks pass. 

Evaluation and Reuse. The candidate ˜θt+1 is re-evaluated on the same validation split and under the same conditions as θt. Using Ft and the executed strategy st, the agent analyzes changes in the primary metric, whether the focal problem pt has improved, and any new errors. These valida-tion results guide subsequent model and strategy selection. If the candidate does not improve the validation score, the parent remains the starting point for subsequent trials. Experiment records link every attempt, including failures, to its strategy, data, training settings, and results; effective strategies, scripts, and data are retained in the asset libraries for reuse. 

4 EXPERIMENTS 

4.1 EXPERIMENTAL SETUP 

Models. We evaluate ImproveAnyTask on Qwen3.5-4B-Base and Qwen3.5-4B-Instruct (Qwen Team, 2026), testing task adaptation from pretrained and post-trained initializations. Each run starts independently from the original checkpoint. 

Tasks. Following the broad evaluation coverage used in recent frontier-model reports (Qwen Team, 2026; Kimi Team, 2026; GLM-5 Team, 2026), we evaluate task-specific self-improvement on 11 tasks across five capability domains: (1) Knowledge and STEM : MMLU-Pro, SuperGPQA, and GPQA-Diamond (Wang et al., 2024; M-A-P Team, 2025; Rein et al., 2023); (2) Mathematical and Verifiable Reasoning : MATH-500 and AIME 2025 (Lightman et al., 2023; Mathematical As-sociation of America, 2025); (3) Instruction Following : IFEval and IFBench (Zhou et al., 2023; Pyatkin et al., 2025); (4) Coding : LiveCodeBench v6 and Terminal-Bench 2.0 (Jain et al., 2024; Merrill et al., 2026); and (5) Tool Use and General Agents : BFCL-V4 and TAU2-Bench (Berke-ley Function Calling Leaderboard Team, 2025; Barres et al., 2025). For BFCL-V4, we exclude the web-search subset because it requires an external paid search service. 

Compute Budget. Each run uses resources equivalent to eight NVIDIA H20 GPUs and stops after 

24 hours or three optimization rounds , whichever comes first. Timing spans the initial to final evaluation, including research, data preparation, validation, training, and failed attempts; unfinished work is interrupted at the deadline. Section 4.3 extends these limits. 

Evaluation Protocol. Following FT-Agent (Li et al., 2026), all methods share a fixed split of ap-proximately 15% validation and 85% held-out test data (Appendix A.1). Only validation data inform error analysis, strategy selection, and checkpoint selection; test data remain inaccessible during op-timization. Within each run, we select the post-trained candidate with the highest validation score among those validated before the deadline; the initial checkpoint is excluded and reported separately as Original .All experiments use the same official evaluation code, a 128K context window, non-thinking gen-eration, and greedy decoding at temperature 0; empty or failed predictions receive zero credit. To reduce sampling variability during optimization, we also set the agent’s API temperature to 0. Main and ablation results are averaged over two independent complete optimization runs per setting, each subject to the same budget. We log exact matches to evaluation queries in training data. Only BFCL aggregation changes, excluding Web Search and renormalizing weights (Appendix A.4). 

Baselines. We compare against Codex (OpenAI, 2025) with GPT-5.6-Sol and Claude Code (An-thropic, 2025) with Opus 4.8. To our knowledge, no related specialized system provides a complete open-source implementation for our full 11-task suite. ImproveAnyTask also uses Opus 4.8. All systems share tool permissions, teacher-model access, and resource limits. Overall Results. Table 1 reports the main results. ImproveAnyTask achieves mean gains of 18.29 percentage points on Qwen3.5-4B-Base and 11.97 points on Qwen3.5-4B-Instruct. It exceeds Codex and Claude Code by 12.49 and 11.43 points on Base, and by 9.54 and 8.04 points on Instruct. These averages include the renormalized BFCL no-Web metric. 

Task-Level Results. ImproveAnyTask improves all eleven Base-model tasks and ten Instruct-model tasks. The largest Base-model gain occurs on LiveCodeBench v6, from 19.64% to 61.60% 

(+41 .96) , while the smallest occurs on AIME 2025 (+4 .00) . For Instruct, the largest gains are on LiveCodeBench v6 (+29 .12) , AIME 2025 (+24 .00) , and IFBench (+21 .56) . Notably, under our evaluation settings, the optimized Base model outperforms the optimized Instruct model on MATH-500 and LiveCodeBench v6. On IFEval, ImproveAnyTask gains 7.84 and 0.87 percentage points on Base and Instruct, respec-tively. Section 4.3 examines the Base-model trajectory within and beyond the first 24 hours. SuperGPQA-Instruct is the only task with a decrease, from 49.78% to 47.77% (−2.00) , with changes computed before truncation. The recorded run contains three unsuccessful attempts. The first used 70K teacher-distilled examples, but only 12,747 were multiple-choice questions and the data were concentrated in physics, mathematics, and chemistry. The second used 15K student-consistent examples, of which 89.1% reproduced responses the student already answered correctly. The third increased the correction ratio to 80% using 1K targeted examples. Experimental Design. We evaluate three modules of ImproveAnyTask through module-level replacement experiments: (1) Error Attribution , including dual-level error analysis and focal-problem selection; (2) Update Direction , including structured strategy comparison, resource veri-fication, reproduction-difficulty assessment, and outcome-aware reuse; and (3) Execution-Guided Refinement within Executable Model Updates , including sample-level data and template checks, short training runs, and iterative correction of data and training pipelines. Within Error Attribution, metric- and case-level analysis provides evidence for prioritizing problems, while focal-problem selection turns that evidence into the next research target. We replace these procedures together to evaluate the complete process from diagnosis to problem selection. Rather than removing an entire stage and breaking the optimization loop, each ablation replaces the corresponding mechanism with a basic alternative. w/o EA retains evaluation feedback but replaces structured dual-level attribution and explicit focal-problem selection with an unstructured summary. 

w/o UD retains ordinary research but removes the required comparison of candidates for the same focal problem, reproduction-difficulty assessment, resource verification, and outcome-aware reuse. 

w/o ER retains Executable Model Updates but removes our structured pre-execution checks and refinement procedure. The underlying agent remains able to inspect artifacts and repair errors in all variants. These replacements remove the harness-prescribed procedures, not the agent’s gen-eral ability to analyze feedback, research methods, or debug code. 5 CONCLUSION 

We introduced ImproveAnyTask, an autonomous post-training harness that supports iterative model self-improvement through error attribution, research-grounded update selection, and executable model updates. Across 11 tasks, it achieves mean gains of 18.29 and 11.97 percentage points on Base and Instruct models under a 24-hour budget with resources equivalent to eight H20 GPUs. By automating repeated diagnosis, research, data preparation, and training, the harness reduces the need for manual trial design and intervention. This offers a practical way to adapt models for specific applications with less human effort and a fixed resource budget. 9Preprint AI USE STATEMENT

Following prior work on task-specific post-training (Rank et al., 2026; Li et al., 2026), we focus on target-task performance. We do not systematically measure whether specialization affects general capabilities, given the substantial evaluation effort and the many combinations of tasks, models, and training strategies. The substantial task-specific gains support applications that need focused model adaptation without repeated manual experimentation. Future work will study how to preserve general capabilities during this process. We also restrict executable updates to supervised post-training. Extending the harness to reinforce-ment learning and broader post-training objectives is left to future work. 18
