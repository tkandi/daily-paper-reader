# Control-Compute Governance in Agentic AI-RAN: AI Agents as Both Controllers and Workloads 

Le Xia, Member, IEEE , Rose Qingyang Hu, Fellow, IEEE , Yuan Zhou, Member, IEEE ,and Haijian Sun, Senior Member, IEEE 

Abstract —Recent advances in artificial intelligence-radio ac-cess network (AI-RAN) are placing large language model (LLM)-driven agents within the open RAN (O-RAN) control hierarchy. A promising deployment for this agentic AI-RAN co-locates LLM inference with physical-layer (PHY) communication processing over the same accelerated compute pool for infrastructure sharing, data locality, and low control latency. However, this co-location induces bidirectional control-compute coupling, as the agent competes with PHY processing for compute, while its reasoned gNB control actions may alter future PHY workload and hence the compute available to its next inference. To this end, this article proposes the control-compute contract, a coordination protocol linking AI-RAN workload governance with O-RAN control execution. Specifically, we first expose direct compute contention on an over-the-air (OTA) O-RAN testbed, where continuous LLM inference increases the PHY decoding time approximately eightfold. We then formulate the contract as three protocol rules with operator-requirement protection, and map it onto O-RAN and AI-RAN functions as a five-stage workflow. Afterward, an OTA-calibrated case study of simulated uplink gNB control indicates that the contract keeps the proportion of slots violating the PHY time budget below 0.9% , against 20 .7% 

under ungoverned co-location, while achieving faster gNB control than a fixed compute reservation benchmark. Finally, we identify several open issues and outlooks for its practical deployment. 

I. I NTRODUCTION 

Artificial intelligence-radio access network (AI-RAN) is garnering increasing attention as a promising wireless architec-ture for converging AI with the RAN. The AI-RAN Alliance defines its three pillars, namely AI-for-RAN to improve RAN functions with AI, AI-and-RAN to host AI and RAN work-loads on common infrastructure, and AI-on-RAN to deliver AI services over the RAN [1]. As these pillars converge toward 6G, the gNB is no longer a fixed-function appliance but an accelerated compute node whose capacity is shared among physical-layer (PHY) processing, RAN intelligence, and AI applications. Within this vision, large language model (LLM)-driven AI agents are being explored to automate intelligent gNB control, giving rise to a paradigm of agentic AI-RAN [2]. Nevertheless, implementing agentic AI-RAN within conven-tional deployments may be strictly constrained by proprietary interfaces from single vendors to acquire RAN observation data and issue gNB control actions [3]. Fortunately, open RAN (O-RAN) offers flexible interfaces and open platforms for different vendors’ applications, forming a natural architectural basis for AI agents, whose control logic can perfectly reside

in xApps on the near-real-time RAN intelligent controller (Near-RT RIC) for programmable gNB control [4]. Their LLM inference can further run alongside the gNB’s accelerated PHY processing over a shared compute pool to share infrastructure, preserve data locality, and reduce control latency. In parallel, this compute co-location approach introduces a bidirectional control-compute coupling issue for agentic AI-RAN. Concretely, the LLM inference workload competes with PHY processing for compute, while the agent’s gNB control actions may alter the PHY workload and further affect the compute available to its subsequent inference. Note that such coupling goes beyond allocating compute between the two concurrent workloads, as an inference allocation compatible with the current PHY workload may become incompatible once the agent’s control action executes. Conversely, deferring or interrupting inference to protect PHY processing can leave the agent without a new control action. Hence, compute allo-cation and control execution cannot be handled separately, and governing this coupling presents three interrelated challenges. 

Dynamic LLM Compute Adjustment for PHY Protec-tion: Allocating compute to LLM inference requires estimat-ing its impact on future PHY processing time under the current system workload. Further, given that physical RAN states and operator requirements may change at any time, the inference compute must be adjusted dynamically to meet PHY time budgets without compromising the agentic control quality. 

Sustaining gNB Control under LLM Inference Interrup-tion: Governing the compute contention may require deferring or even interrupting LLM inference for PHY protection, pre-venting the agent from issuing timely gNB control actions. However, simply reusing an earlier gNB control action is also insufficient, since its validity depends on the earlier RAN state and operator requirements that may have changed. Thus, valid gNB control must be sustained without fresh LLM outputs. 

Compute Compatibility Across gNB Control Execution: 

An issued gNB control action may not take effect immedi-ately, and compute allocation should remain compatible with current PHY processing, without assuming that the new PHY workload is already active or that it continues to rely on the previous workload after application. Consequently, the compute adjustment and action execution must be properly sequenced during the gNB configuration transition. Existing AI-RAN research has primarily explored either AI agent-driven gNB control [5], [6] or compute allocation between AI and RAN workloads [7]–[9]. A. Two Coupling Paths over a Shared Compute Pool 

Agentic AI-RAN sits at the intersection of the AI-for-RAN and AI-and-RAN pillars [1], [11], where AI agents are simultaneously AI-for-RAN controllers and AI-and-RAN workloads. O-RAN offers a concrete architectural realization, where the agents are logically situated in the rApp and xApp roles of its RIC hierarchy as part of the gNB control path [5]. Meanwhile, O-RAN specifications permit an implementation to aggregate the Near-RT RIC, O-RAN central unit (O-CU), and O-RAN distributed unit (O-DU) [4], allowing the agent’s LLM inference to run on the gNB host. The agent’s dual role, however, opens two control-compute coupling paths, as depicted in Fig. 1. The first arises from direct contention in the shared pool. On the gNB side, the O-DU executes latency-sensitive high-PHY functions, such as uplink low-density parity-check (LDPC) decoding and channel estimation, at radio-slot timescales. In AI-RAN, such functions are designed to run on AI accelerators (e.g., GPUs) [11], rather than on traditional application-specific integrated cir-cuits (ASICs)/digital signal processors (DSPs). In parallel, the AI agent on the RIC side operates on shared accelerators and turns RAN observations and operator intent into a candidate gNB control action over seconds [6]. Such workload overlap may extend PHY processing time, while prioritizing PHY execution slows the agent’s reasoning [8]. The contention intensity also varies with traffic, channel state, and RAN configuration, which shape the PHY workload presented to the pool. Furthermore, since it can also occur during LLM inference, the first coupling path persists whether or not the resulting candidate gNB control action is executed. By contrast, the second coupling path arises only after the control action is executed. For example, an action that changes the modulation and coding scheme (MCS), the bandwidth 3

allocation, or the scheduling density can set the PHY workload per radio slot [9], subsequently altering the compute left for the next-round LLM inference. Hence, this closes a feedback path from the agent, through the gNB’s accelerated PHY processing, and back to the agent’s subsequent reasoning. Moreover, such feedback makes each agent an endogenous controller whose available compute is set inside the control loop it runs rather than being provisioned from outside. 

B. Gaps in Existing Control and Compute Mechanisms 

As summarized on the left of Fig. 1, existing AI-RAN research falls into two classes, neither of which considers an agent that controls the gNB while reasoning on its pool. One class studies AI-for-RAN control yet keeps LLM reasoning outside the gNB’s compute pool. For instance, AgentRAN [5] and Symbiotic Agents [6] ran the LLM inference on cloud services or dedicated control-plane servers, adding round-trip latency to every control decision. The other studies AI-and-RAN co-location without examining this bidirectional control-compute coupling. For example, SC3 [2] and SLA-LLM [7] co-locate LLM inference with live 5G processing under fixed GPU partitions, while YinYangRAN [8] multiplexes PHY and generic machine-learning workloads. In fact, CloudRIC [9] comes closest to bridging the two sides, as it allocated radio resources in response to accelerator congestion. Even there, its controller runs as a lightweight CPU thread outside the shared pool, and its own decision-making compute never enters its control loop. The same separation remains in current AI-RAN architecture documents [1] and O-RAN specifications [10].

Verified Safe gNB Control Action (R2): The AI agent relies on LLM inference to propose gNB control actions, but R1 may defer or interrupt that inference to protect PHY processing. To sustain gNB control without fresh LLM outputs, R2 requires the Near-RT RIC to retain the latest verified control action, which remains issuable as long as it passes re-verification. If the RAN state or AI-RAN operator requirements change, the retained action must pass verification again before it can be issued over E2. 

Action-Induced PHY Workload Projection (R3): A gNB control action verified under R2 may still change the PHY workload used for inference admission under R1. Hence, R3 requires projecting the resulting workload before action execution and checking whether PHY processing can still meet its time budget with the compute already granted to LLM inference. If not, the action must wait until the running inference is adjusted or stopped and a compatible compute allocation is confirmed. Once the new configuration is con-firmed as applied, its projected PHY workload must be used in subsequent inference admission under R1. 

Operator-Requirement Protection: Across R1 to R3, the operator requirements must remain binding and cannot be relaxed by the AI agent. In detail, the gNB host must reject compute allocations that violate the PHY time budget or the agent’s compute budget, while the Near-RT RIC must reject gNB control actions that violate the operator requirements. Accordingly, no agent can directly obtain more compute from the shared pool by proposing gNB control actions that benefit its own inference, unless they meet the above requirements. 

Takeaway: A verified gNB control action and an admitted LLM inference can still be incompatible, as the action may change the PHY workload assumed at admission. Our contract therefore links their approval and execution order over the shared compute pool, requiring a compatible compute allo-cation. On the one hand, R1 governs the first coupling path through inference admission, with R2 retaining a verified gNB control action when inference is deferred or interrupted. On the other hand, R3 governs the second path by checking compute compatibility before action execution and updating subsequent inference admission with the projected PHY workload.

Action Verification and PHY Projection Stage: Whenever an LLM-driven xApp proposes a candidate gNB control action through its R1-admitted inference, the Near-RT RIC first verifies it under R2 and projects the PHY workload that would result from executing that action. The projection result is then sent to the gNB host together with the verified action. 

Compute Compatibility Check Stage: Using this projec-tion, the gNB host checks under R3 whether PHY processing can meet its required time budget with the compute already allocated to LLM inference. If compatible, the workflow pro-ceeds directly to action execution without adjusting inference. 

Inference Workload Adjustment Stage: If the check fails, the RIC withholds the new action from E2 while the host ad-justs or stops any ongoing inference and rechecks the resulting compute allocation. Meanwhile, a verified gNB control action remains available under R2, subject to re-verification when the RAN state or operator requirements change. 

Action Execution and Confirmation Stage: After the host confirms a compatible allocation, the Near-RT RIC issues the verified action over E2. The host will keep that allocation in place until it confirms the O-DU has applied the new gNB configuration carried by the executed action, since an earlier change may reopen the incompatibility just resolved. 

Inference Admission Update Stage: Once the new gNB configuration is applied, the O-DU reports it to both the host and the Near-RT RIC, and the PHY workload projected earlier by the Near-RT RIC becomes the host’s estimate for subsequent LLM inference admission under R1. The workflow then returns to the first stage to begin the next cycle. IV. C ASE STUDY AND PRELIMINARY EVALUATION 

Consider an AI agent that serves as the uplink MCS controller of the gNB, where uplink active and quiet periods average 3 s and 2 s, respectively, with LDPC decoding only in active slots to trigger admission rechecks under R1. Four MCS indices are set therein corresponding to one to four LDPC code blocks per active 0.5-ms slot [14]. On the RIC side, the agent initiates an LLM inference every 30 s, whose MCS selection is sampled with varying probabilities to sweep the mean PHY workload, over four 4000 -s runs per setting. Here, let f ∈ [0 , 1] 

denote the fraction of the shared compute pool granted to LLM inference. During active periods, our contract sets f = f ∗, the largest fraction for which the OTA-calibrated LDPC decoding time under the applied MCS remains within the illustrative 2-ms PHY time budget with 99% probability. Accordingly, the duration of an admitted inference is approximated as the OTA-measured full-pool inference duration divided by its granted fraction. For tractability, the operator requirements are consid-ered met, and thus a verified MCS selection is always available under R2. Moreover, since the selected MCS is executed only when inference completes, the compute compatibility check under R3 passes without any inference adjustment. Unquantified Control Gain from Granted Compute: 

This work does not quantify how much additional LLM reasoning improves the quality of gNB control in tasks involv-ing operator intent and changing RAN conditions. Therefore, closed-loop evaluations with live LLM reasoning in such tasks deserve investigation to quantify the control gain per unit of granted compute and thereby to guide both the fraction granted to an agent and the spending of its compute budget. 

Calibration Drift and Stopping Delay in Practice: In practical O-RAN deployments, the PHY processing cost cali-brated in Section IV may drift over time, while the compute occupied by an ongoing inference is released only some time after a stop request. This thus requires determining how much margin f ∗ must carry against such drift and delay. Runtime recalibration from the observed tail of PHY processing time and inference that can be paused and resumed may help maintain protection at a lower latency cost. VI. C ONCLUSIONS 

This article proposed the control-compute contract for agen-tic AI-RAN to address the bidirectional coupling problem arising when the AI agent simultaneously controls the gNB and utilizes the shared compute pool for its LLM inference. Specifically, we first devised three protocol rules alongside one operator-requirement protection condition, and then presented a five-stage workflow by mapping them onto O-RAN and AI-RAN functions. Moreover, an OTA-calibrated case study further indicated that the contract yields fewer PHY time-budget overruns than ungoverned co-location and faster gNB control than worst-case reservation, followed by several open issues and outlooks. Looking ahead, once multiple agents share one pool and the RIC-host messages gain standardized carriers, control-compute governance can become a native part of the 6G AI-RAN control plane, treating every AI agent as both a controller and a workload. We hope this work lays a solid foundation toward this vision of agentic AI-RAN.
