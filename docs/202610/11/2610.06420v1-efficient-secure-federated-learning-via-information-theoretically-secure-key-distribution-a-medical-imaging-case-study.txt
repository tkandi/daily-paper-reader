## Abstract 

Federated Learning (FL) enables collaborative training of models across institutions without centralizing sensitive data, making it well-suited for privacy-concerned applications, such as medical imaging. To protect FL model updates during secure aggregation, additive masking is commonly employed. However, its underlying classical key establishment is only computationally secure. On the other hand, physics-based Information-Theoretically Secure (ITS) key exchange introduces practical constraints: finite key genera-tion rates and time-limited storage severely limit throughput and sustained training of uncompressed models. In this work, we ad-dress this bottleneck by developing an FL framework that integrates frozen backbones, knowledge distillation, and quantization. These techniques reduce communication payload and, consequently, key material consumption. Moving beyond simulation, we benchmark this framework on a real physics-based key distribution testbed involving a chest X-ray classification application. Our results show that key usage can be reduced by ∼35 × while maintaining predic-tive accuracy. This prevents buffer depletion and key expiration, enabling sustainable FL training under physical key generation constraints.

## 1 Introduction 

Federated Learning (FL) has emerged as a promising paradigm for collaborative model training across institutions that cannot share raw data due to privacy regulations, proprietary concerns, or ethical constraints [16]. This is particularly relevant in medical imaging, where individual sites have limited data due to high imaging and annotation costs, yet privacy regulations (e.g., HIPAA, GDPR) pre-vent pooling datasets across institutions [10]. In a standard FL deployment, each participating institution trains a model locally on its own data and transmits only model updates (e.g., gradients or weight deltas) or the updated weights themselves to a central aggregation server. The server aggregates these contributions to produce an improved global model, iterated over multiple com-munication rounds [21]. While FL avoids raw data exchange, the transmitted model updates or weights are not inherently private: model/gradient inversion attacks [31, 32] can reconstruct sensitive training samples from shared model/gradients, and membership inference attacks [26] can determine whether a specific patient’s data participated in training. These vulnerabilities are especially acute in medical imaging, where the reconstruction of diagnostic scans directly compromises patient confidentiality. Secure aggregation protocols address this threat by ensuring that the server learns only the aggregate of client updates, never any individual contribution. A common approach [4] uses pairwise additive masking, where each client adds cryptographic masks de-rived from shared secrets to its model update before transmission. When the server sums all masked updates, the pairwise masks can-cel, revealing only the clean aggregate. However, in conventional implementations, the shared secrets are established via classical public-key cryptography (e.g., Diffie-Hellman), which is only com-putationally secure and vulnerable to attacks by a sufficiently pow-erful quantum adversary [27]. As quantum computing hardware arXiv:2610.06420v1 [cs.LG] 5 Oct 2026 MobiHoc ’26, November 23-26, 2026, Tokyo, Japan Donà et al. 

advances, the computational security assumptions underpinning classical secure aggregation become increasingly fragile. Quantum Key Distribution (QKD) offers a fundamentally differ-ent security guarantee [1]. Unlike classical key exchange, it encodes key material in quantum states, leveraging the no-cloning theorem and measurement disturbance detection to let two parties estab-lish shared secure keys that are information-theoretically secure, even against an adversary with unlimited computational power. In particular, Continuous-Variable QKD (CV-QKD) with Gaussian modulation encodes key information in the quadratures of coher-ent states, offering compatibility with standard telecom photonic components [18]. However, deploying quantum-secure key exchange in FL in-troduces a fundamental throughput bottleneck. Unlike classical key establishment, which can generate keys at arbitrary rates, information-theoretically secure key generation via quantum chan-nels produces shared secret bits at a finite rate determined by pho-ton detection efficiency, channel loss, and post-processing over-head [18, 30]. Furthermore, quantum-derived key material has a limited storage lifetime in practical key management systems, cre-ating a “use-it-or-lose-it” constraint [15]. When Shamir’s secret sharing is used for secure aggregation, the key material consumed per round scales linearly with the size of each client’s masked model update [4, 24]. For modern medical imaging models (e.g., ResNet-50 [12] with ∼25 .6×10 6 parameters), uncompressed weight updates can require hundreds of megabytes of key material per round per client, far exceeding sustainable quantum key generation rates of 1–10 kbit s −1 [5]. This mismatch causes key buffer depletion, forc-ing training to stall. Liu et al. [19] similarly identify communication cost as the critical bottleneck in quantum-secure FL, achieving 75 % reduction through model compression, yet still do not address sus-tained multi-round key consumption for large architectures. This fundamental tension limits the feasibility of quantum-secured FL for realistic medical imaging workloads. Recent work has begun exploring quantum-secured FL. Catak et al. [7] demonstrate quantum-secured FL for 6G channel estimation with BB84-style key distribution. Liu et al. [19] provide the first experimental validation on a physical four-client quantum network, achieving 75 % communication cost reduction via model compres-sion. Tanbhir and Shahriyar [28] integrate quantum key exchange with FL for dementia classification on brain MRI. Broader surveys have also begun to appear [20, 23]. However, these works either do not address sustained key consumption over multiple FL rounds, do not evaluate on realistic workloads (such as those that are typ-ical in medical imaging [11, 17]), or remain simulation-based. To our knowledge, no prior work systematically addresses the tension between quantum key generation constraints and FL payload size through compression on a real quantum key testbed. In this work, we bridge this gap by developing an FL framework that systematically reduces key material consumption to match quantum key throughput constraints. We combine three comple-mentary techniques: (i) frozen pretrained backbones that eliminate the need to transmit large feature-extractor weights, reducing the number of trainable (and thus transmittable) parameters; (ii) knowl-edge distillation to compress models into low-dimensional student representations; and (iii) quantization to reduce the bit-width of Client 5  Client 4

## 2 Proposed Framework 

The proposed framework integrates quantum-secured additive masking with model compression to enable sustainable FL under physical key generation constraints. Figure 1 illustrates the end-to-end flow. Each client trains a classification head locally on its data partition while keeping the backbone frozen. The trained head parameters are quantized to fixed-width integers. Each client then fetches key material (shared with its two ring neighbors) and ap-plies additive or subtractive masks to the quantized parameters. The masked vectors are transmitted to the server, which sums them; the pairwise masks cancel exactly, yielding the clean aggregate. The server dequantizes the aggregate and updates the global model via Federated Averaging (FedAvg) [21]. ## 2.1 Quantum-Secured Ring Additive Masking 

Threat model. The primary adversary is an honest-but-curious aggregation server with unbounded computational power. The server follows the protocol faithfully but attempts to infer individual client contributions from the masked updates it receives. We assume the pairwise keys derived via QKD are information-theoretically secure, that each key masks exactly one update and is never reused, and that classical components (key storage, transport, software) are uncompromised; implementation-level side channels are out of scope (Section 5). Security is assessed at three levels: (1) Key security: the pairwise keys are information-theoretically secure, a guarantee inherited from the physical layer [9]. (2) Message security: given key security, each masked update is statistically independent of the underlying contribution, the keys act as one-time pads [25]. The server alone therefore learns nothing about any individual contribution beyond the aggregate. This guarantee also holds if the server colludes with any single client, even a ring neighbor of the target, since reconstruction requires both neighboring keys. (3) System-level privacy: client compromise is outside the pri-mary model, but its consequences can be stated precisely. A coalition comprising the server and both ring neighbors of a client can reconstruct that client’s contribution. This is the price of reducing quantum links from 𝑂 (𝑛 2) to 𝑂 (𝑛 ); fully connected pairwise masking tolerates collusion of up to 𝑛 − 1 clients [4]. Each link generates shared secure key material between the two endpoints with information-theoretic security. ## 2.2 Compression Techniques 

We apply a three-stage compression pipeline to reduce the payload size transmitted during FL rounds. 

2.2.1 Frozen Pretrained Backbone. All backbone layers are frozen, and only the classification head is trained and trans-mitted. The model architecture considered in our experiments, i.e., MobileNetV3-Small [13] (width multiplier 0 .75) pretrained on ImageNet[8], comprises ∼803 × 10 3 parameters; whereas the classification head comprises ∼229 × 10 3 parameters, yielding a 

∼3.5 × reduction in the number of parameters that are trained and transmitted. 

2.2.2 Knowledge Distillation. To improve the quality of the frozen features, we employ knowledge distillation [3] as a domain-transfer pretraining strategy. A compact student model ( ∼337 × 10 3 total parameters, ∼90 × 10 3 for the classification head) is distilled from a MobileNetV3-Small (width multiplier 1) teacher fine-tuned on the CheXpert dataset [14] 1, producing domain-specific thoracic features that outperform ImageNet features for chest X-ray classi-fication. The student is then frozen, and also in this case only its classification head is transmitted, reducing the payload by a further 

∼2.5 × relative to the frozen teacher head. 

2.2.3 Quantization. Transmitted model updates are quantized to fixed-width integers using a symmetric clipping range of 

[− 8.0, +8.0]. We evaluate three precision levels: 32-bit, 16-bit, and 8-bit. Quantization is applied only to the parameters transmitted to the aggregation server; local training remains in float32 to preserve gradient quality.

## 3.3 Federated Learning Configuration 

We implement the FL framework using the Flower framework [2]. All experiments are conducted on a cluster of six physical machines, each representing one FL client. Each node is equipped with dual In-tel Xeon E5-2683 v4 processors (64 logical CPUs total) and 125 GiB RAM, with training parallelized across 12 logical cores per client. All training is performed on CPU only; no GPUs are used. This CPU-only configuration reflects realistic deployment constraints at clinical sites, where hospitals typically utilize existing enterprise server infrastructure rather than provisioning specialized AI accel-erators. Six clients are arranged in a ring topology implemented with three transmitters and three receivers (Section 2.1). We run FedAvg for 50 communication rounds with 1 local epoch per round, batch size 16, starting learning rate 10 −2 with a cosine annealing learning rate scheduler, and weight decay 10 −4. All experiments with masking use frozen backbones with only the classification head trainable. Under this configuration, each FL round completes in approximately 4 min. 

## 3.4 Quantum Key Distribution Network 

The testbed comprises three CV-QKD transmitters and three re-ceivers co-located in a laboratory. The used prototype devices are field-trial tested as described in [5]. The transmitters operate at fixed carrier frequencies in the C-band (193 .4 THz, 193 .7 THz, and 193 .8 THz). Each transmitter output is split via a 1:2 coupler to its two neighboring receivers; each receiver input is a combination of the two neighboring transmitters via a 2:1 coupler. A receiver selects the desired signal by tuning its local oscillator wavelength. The six logical links form a ring topology, as illustrated in [22]. Because each receiver can only detect one signal at a time, only three of the six links are active simultaneously. The odd and even link sets alternate approximately every hour, coordinated by a switching controller [22]. Despite this 50 % duty cycle, key material is continuously available through per-link key buffers: each buffer holds up to 200,000 keys of 512 B each ( ∼100 MB). Achievable key generation rates for CV-QKD networks of this type range from 1–10 kbit s −1 for channel losses of 4–16 dB [5]. The present testbed operates at the upper end of this range ( ∼10 kbit s −1

per active link). While the current deployment is a co-located labo-ratory setup with prototype devices, a demonstration over a metro-politan fiber network is planned as future work.

Table 1 reports AUROC (macro-averaged) on the NIH ChestX-ray14 test set after 50 FL rounds across all configurations. The uncom-pressed teacher baseline (fully trainable, no masking) achieves an AUROC of 0.762 under IID partitioning. Freezing the backbone and transmitting only the classification head yields a modest accu-racy decrease (0.745 IID, 0.736 non-IID), consistent with the loss of fine-tuning in the feature extractor. As an implementation sanity check, we repeated the frozen-teacher configuration with masking disabled: the per-round aggregates and the final AUROC (0.745) are identical to the masked run, confirming that the pairwise masks cancel exactly and that the masking layer does not alter the train-ing trajectory. The CheXpert-distilled student recovers much of this gap (0.748 IID), demonstrating that domain-specific distillation compensates for the reduced model capacity. Quantization to 16-bit and 8-bit incurs a modest additional degradation (up to ∼0.02 AUROC from 32-bit to 8-bit), confirming that fixed-width integer representation is viable for FL weight transmission. Non-IID parti-tioning reduces AUROC by ∼0.01–0.02 across all configurations, as expected.

## 5 Discussion 

Key findings. The results show that quantum-secured FL is practi-cally feasible on real hardware when paired with aggressive com-pression. The uncompressed baseline would consume 3 .2 MB per round per link ( 𝑇 min ≈ 85 min, Table 2), exhausting a full buffer within ∼33 rounds, whereas the 8-bit student needs only 90 kB per link ( 𝑇 min ≈ 2.4 min) and is indefinitely sustainable. 

Accuracy trade-offs. The choice of pretraining strategy proves critical for maintaining accuracy under heavy compression. The ImageNet-pretrained backbone exhibits a substantial domain shift when applied to chest radiographs, resulting in a lower AUC com-pared to the domain-adapted model. By using knowledge distilla-tion from a teacher fine-tuned on the CheXpert dataset, the student model acquires thoracic-specific features that transfer seamlessly to the NIH dataset task. Once this high-quality frozen backbone is established, the trainable classification head requires very few parameters. Consequently, quantizing the transmitted head parame-ters from 32-bit to 8-bit integers introduces only marginal accuracy degradation. This confirms that the bulk of the predictive capa-bility resides in the stable, frozen feature extractors rather than the rapidly updated classification head, justifying the aggressive payload reduction. 

Non-IID robustness. Real-world medical FL deployments face naturally non-IID data due to varying disease prevalence across hos-pitals. Our framework maintains stable convergence under aggres-sive label skew ( 𝛼 = 0.1) with only modest accuracy degradation relative to IID. Because the frozen, domain-pretrained backbone confines local updates to the linear head, the restricted gradient space reduces divergence risk, suggesting the compression pipeline does not amplify non-IID optimization challenges. 

Security considerations. The levels-1 and -2 security guar-antees are information-theoretic in principle [9, 25], but practical deployments introduce implementation-level vulnerabilities that fall outside this model, including side-channel attacks on quantum key exchange hardware and classical attack surfaces in the net-work transport and software stack. The framework’s contribution is therefore best understood as a strict improvement over classical secure aggregation with respect to key establishment and mask gen-eration: it removes the computational assumptions (Diffie-Hellman hardness and pseudorandomness) while retaining the same trust as-sumption about the aggregation server. Collusion resistance (level-3) is weaker than in fully connected pairwise masking, as discussed above. A formal security analysis incorporating implementation-level threats is left to future work. 

Limitations. The ring additive masking scheme provides information-theoretic security with minimal quantum key ex-change infrastructure, but introduces two structural constraints. First, it requires all clients to participate in every round; if a single client drops out or becomes unresponsive, the pairwise masks do not cancel at the server, and the aggregate cannot be recovered. Dropout-resilient mechanisms, such as having the server reveal the dropped client’s mask or employing threshold secret sharing vari-ants, could address this in future work. Second, the ring topology reduces collusion resistance relative to fully connected pairwise masking: a coalition of the server and both ring neighbors of a client can unmask its contribution (threat-model level 3). This is a direct consequence of minimizing the number of quantum key links from 𝑂 (𝑛 2) to 𝑂 (𝑛 ), and we consider it an acceptable trade-off given that the primary threat model is an honest-but-curious server. Strengthening the scheme against collusion, e.g., through multi-hop masking or hybrid topologies, is left to future work. Finally, the framework is currently evaluated on a single dataset MobiHoc ’26, November 23-26, 2026, Tokyo, Japan Donà et al. 

(NIH ChestX-ray14) and a single architecture family (MobileNetV3). While the approach generalizes conceptually to other medical imaging modalities and backbone choices, empirical validation across diverse tasks remains necessary. 

## 6 Conclusions 

In this work, we addressed the fundamental throughput bottle-neck that arises when integrating QKD with FL. While it provides information-theoretically secure key exchange, its finite generation rates and perishable key buffers are fundamentally incompatible with the massive payload sizes of modern medical imaging mod-els. We proposed a framework that bridges this gap by combining frozen pretrained backbones, knowledge distillation for domain adaptation, and fixed-width quantization. Deployed on a real CV quantum key testbed with six clients arranged in a ring topology, the framework reduced per-round key material consumption by up to ∼35 ×. This reduction enabled sustained, 50-round federated training on chest X-ray classification without key buffer depletion, all while preserving accuracy. Future work will focus on integrat-ing dropout resilience into the masking protocol and extending the evaluation to additional medical imaging modalities and backbone architectures, or alternative FL aggregation functions, such as those based on ensembles [6].
