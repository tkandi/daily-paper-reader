# Acceleration of Data Analytics on Heterogeneous Supercloud Systems 

## GEORGIOS ZACHAROPOULOS ∗, Computing Systems Lab, Huawei Research, Zurich, Switzerland 

## ILIAS BOURNIAS ∗, Computing Systems Lab, Huawei Research, Zurich, Switzerland 

## LUKAS CAVIGELLI, Computing Systems Lab, Huawei Research, Zurich, Switzerland 

Heterogeneous Supercloud systems are transforming data analytics by enabling scalable and efficient task distribution across diverse resources. This paper presents computational models and performance estimation techniques tailored for accelerating Dense Cholesky (CH) Decomposition and Singular Value Decomposition (SVD)-based analytics on Heterogeneous Superclouds. Our ReDSEa tool-chain automates mapping, load balancing, scheduling, parallelism, and computation overlap. Implemented on a heterogeneous system with a Huawei Kunpeng 920 ARM CPU and an Ascend 910 AI accelerator, our LLVM compiler tool-chain employs novel performance models for recursive, iterative, and blocked computations, achieving up to 17x speedup for CH and 88x for SVD over fully optimized 48-core CPU implementations. 

1 Introduction 

Heterogeneous computing has emerged as a vital paradigm in modern computing, leveraging a combination of diverse processor types and architectures to enhance performance, power efficiency, and cost-effectiveness. The concept of the Supercloud, introduced by McColl et al., represents an advanced heterogeneous cloud architecture specifically engineered to meet the demanding processing needs of AI, big data, and high-performance computing (HPC) applications. Within Supercloud systems, the integration of CPUs with specialized accelerators, such as GPUs, TPUs, and DPUs, capitalizes on the unique strengths of each component, fostering a synergy that significantly boosts performance and energy efficiency. In these large-scale heterogeneous computing environments, high-speed interconnects enable different processors to collaborate efficiently through software that distributes and coordinates workloads at various levels of granularity. This ranges from low-level hardware acceleration to high-level task scheduling and load balancing. Such systems are particularly effective in parallel processing tasks vital for advanced simulations and machine learning algorithms, offering opportunities for various forms of parallel execution, including Instruction Level (ILP), Task Level (TLP), and Pipeline (PP) Parallelism. However, designing complex Supercloud heterogeneous systems poses significant challenges that are both time-consuming and demanding. Engineers must carefully consider physical resource constraints, communication costs, and other factors when deciding which portions of an application should be accelerated on GPUs or hardware accelerators and which should run on general-purpose CPUs. Applications in domains like Deep Learning, Extended Reality (XR), and Autonomous Vehicles benefit from a Design Space Exploration (DSE) methodology based on performance estimation models. This methodology can (a) utilize all forms of parallelism, (b) be driven by the application source code or a graph representation of a computation model, and (c) automatically determine which parts of the application should be hardware-accelerated. Supervised and unsupervised learning are fundamental to contemporary machine learning, playing vital roles in processing and interpreting complex data sets. Singular Value Decomposition (SVD) is pivotal in executing Principal Component Analysis (PCA) for feature and dimensionality reduction, highlighting the most informative features and compressing the data. However, traditional SVD algorithms are limited by memory bandwidth, impacting their performance. Therefore, novel methods have been proposed to accelerate computations by offloading them to GPUs or accelerators. In our previous work [1], we introduced the ReDSEa tool-chain and applied it to the Triangular System Solver (TS). In this paper, we extend the methodology to Dense Cholesky Factorization and SVD, which require new performance models. Our contributions are as follows: 

• We explore three distinct computation models (recursive, iterative and blocked) for mapping Dense Cholesky Factorization and SVD to Supercloud heterogeneous systems. 

• We introduce novel models of performance estimation (computation and communication) for every computation model along with load balancing, parallelism and scheduling optimizations to maximize performance. 

• We extend ReDSEa [1], our LLVM-based automated tool, to utilize these models of performance estimation and select tasks as candidates for hardware acceleration. 

• We evaluate all three models of computation (recursive, iterative and blocked), and their optimizations, using Dense Cholesky Factorization and SVD on an ARM 48-core CPU [2] and an Ascend 910 [3] AI HW accelerator. 

2 Related Work 

The use of cloud heterogeneous systems to accelerate Linear Algebra has gained significant attention in research during recent years [4]. Monil et al. [5] introduce LaRIS, a portable framework for LAPACK functionalities on Heterogeneous Systems. LaRIS uses IRIS [6] to dynamically select the vendor-library kernel and suitable processor architecture at run-time, making the orchestration simpler when different architectures coexist in a single node. Valero-Lara et al. [7] evaluate the use of OpenMP tasking with target GPU offloading as a potential solution for programming productivity and performance on heterogeneous systems. As a test case, the Triangular Solver (TS) routine is used. In [8], the IRIS-BLAS framework is presented, which is built on top of IRIS. It has the capability to utilize all the available architectures on a heterogeneous system, using the appropriate BLAS library based on the task mapping at run time. Thus, the application developers are relieved from modifying the application source code. All three works mentioned previously follow the iterative and blocking approach. In [9], the authors introduce ReLAPACK, a methodology that makes use of recursive algorithms in order to implement LAPACK functionalities. They claim that, contrary to blocked algorithms, recursive algorithms do not require significant tuning to define a proper granularity/refinement level. They make this assumption for shared memory architectures. However, in all the aforementioned papers, there is not a model to determine both the refinement level and the expected performance. A theoretical model, which predicts the refinement level and performance of Linear Algebra acceleration in homoge-neous systems, is analyzed in [10]. Their model is based on the number of operations of kernels that are used in their framework, such as TRSM, SYRK and GEMM. The application that they target is the Dense Cholesky Factorization. Nevertheless, communication costs in a homogeneous system are not taken into account while simulation results, and not experimental results, are presented. A similar approach for the same application is followed in [11]. Recent years have seen increased interest in leveraging heterogeneous systems for accelerating SVD computations [12, 13]. Typically, these approaches divide the SVD process into two stages, optimizing the use of heterogeneous resources. Acceleration of Data Analytics on Heterogeneous Supercloud Systems 3The initial task involves transforming a full matrix into a bidiagonal form. Faverge et al. [14] investigate tiled algorithms for this transformation, focusing on orthogonal transformations and providing a critical path analysis for each algorithm. They demonstrate that R-BIDIAG exhibits a shorter critical path length compared to BIDIAG for tall and skinny matrices, and GREEDY-based schemes outperform other state of the art algorithms when resources are unlimited. They evaluate their approaches by conducting experiments on both a single multicore node and on a parallel distributed shared-memory system consisting of a few multicore nodes. They conclude that QR and LQ reduction trees cannot overlap in their processes. Ltaief et al. [15] explore the influence of tile size on execution time, linking it to the matrix bandwidth size post-reduction. They use a brute force mechanism to retrieve the optimal tile size of a specific problem size. It is also pointed out that the optimal tile size is not the same for every sub-task of the problem. Gates et al. [12] develop a 2-stage reduction algorithm to transform a full matrix to a bidiagonal one, accelerating the process by offloading matrix multiplications to GPUs and overlapping them with LQ and QR computations on the CPU. However, these studies lack automated tools for performance estimation based on hardware resources and granularity level to achieve maximum throughput. The second stage focuses on the SVD of the bidiagonal matrix. Gates et al. [12] use the Divide and Conquer (D&C) algorithm, achieving better performance compared to QR factorization methods [16]. They accelerate matrix multiplications with GPUs, while the CPU handles secular equation solving and singular vector computation. Liu et al. [13] also use the Divide and Conquer approach but on the top of that, they offload the secular equation computation to accelerators. They examine the performance of different algorithms for this task and they try to reduce the number of the floating point operations of the different algorithms in order to decrease latency. However, these papers do not delve into the recursion depth of the Divide and Conquer algorithm, a crucial aspect in heterogeneous systems. Additionally, compiler-based methodologies have been introduced for automatic performance estimation in hetero-geneous systems [17, 18], focusing on general approaches using LLVM static analysis and dynamic profiling [19], or targeting specific parallelism types [20]. However, they do not address diverse computation models which can be used to enhance performance and they focus mainly on streaming applications. Design space exploration (DSE) methods have also been explored [21–23]. They focus on strategies for synthesizing accelerators and implementing optimizations in terms of performance improvement, accuracy, and reducing the area required for hardware implementations. Nevertheless, they target mainly FPGA devices and lack a potentially wider variety of hardware. 

3 Compiler Tool-chain 

To address the challenges mentioned in the previous section, we build on ReDSEa (Recursive Design Space Exploration Automation): the tool-chain we introduced in [1], based on the LLVM [24] Compiler Infrastructure (Version 14.0.0). 

3.1 Compiler Analysis 

The first stage of our methodology is analyzing the application that is going to be mapped to a heterogeneous system or cloud architecture. The analysis phase includes an automated process that provides our compiler infrastructure with necessary information so as to guide the subsequent steps (Cost Models and Design Space Exploration) of the automation methodology. It can be viewed as the input to our automated system. The analysis is performed by generating the respective LLVM-IR from the application source code (typically written in C, C++). A number of LLVM analysis passes, developed within the scope of ReDSEa, analyze the intermediate representation of the applications and estimate the latency due to computation of every potential task, or otherwise described, of every node of the data flow graph. 4 Zacharopoulos et al.

3.3 Design Space Exploration 

Based on the cost models of the previous subsection, a list of candidates for acceleration is generated. The selection (branch-and-bound) algorithm recursively explores the subsets of the list of candidates, in a similar manner to the Bron-Kerbosch algorithm [25]. The output returned is the set with the highest speedup (minimum cumulative latency) that stays within the user defined resource budget, which is translated as the amount of resources available for hardware acceleration.

4.2 Accelerated SVD for Heterogeneous Systems 

The traditional SVD algorithm employs a 1-stage reduction to bidiagonal form, relying on matrix-vector multiplications. This approach is often hindered by limited memory bandwidth by underutilizing the available computation resources, adversely affecting performance. To address this issue, a 2-stage reduction algorithm to bidiagonal form which makes use of matrix-matrix multiplications has been proposed [12], among others. As illustrated in Figure 2, this algorithm initially reduces the matrix to a band form utilizing high-performance Level 3 BLAS functions, followed by reducing the band matrix to bidiagonal form using cache-optimized kernels with dynamic scheduling. Full-to-Band. The first phase involves transforming the matrix to band form, crucially removing data dependencies with the trailing matrix during panel factorization. This is key to overcoming the bottleneck caused by matrix-vector operations in the panel. The procedure involves a QR panel factorization of a block column to remove entries below the diagonal, followed by an update of the trailing matrix. This is succeeded by an LQ panel factorization of a block row to eliminate entries right of the upper band width of the matrix, and then another update of the trailing matrix. Figure 3 illustrates this process, with the trailing matrix updates shown as dark grey rectangles. These updates are offloaded to accelerators, while the QR and LQ panels are computed on the host CPU. 

Band-to-Bidiagonal. In the second stage, the upper band matrix is further reduced to upper bidiagonal form. Due to the limited parallelism, memory bandwidth constraints, and the need for CPU cache optimizations, using accelerators for this stage offers only marginal benefits. Hence, this stage’s computations are primarily performed on the host CPU. It is important to note that the bulk of the computational work occurs in the initial stage (from full to band form), which consequently lessens the computational load in this later stage.

5.2 Iterative 

Once the granularity of the computation that remains to the host has been determined, an iterative model of computation can be offered as an alternative, as seen in Figure 4 (middle). There are two main reasons to favor an iterative approach over a recursive one: a) It can offer better utilization of the accelerators that are available, as fewer accelerators are dedicated to compute relatively smaller parts of the computation compared to a recursive one, which gradually allocates smaller and smaller parts of the computation to be computed by the HW accelerators. b) It demands less engineering effort to be implemented while at the same time the performance is not sacrificed, but instead it is kept equal, or slightly better, compared to a recursive one. Furthermore, in the case of Dense Cholesky Factorization, the amount of computation that is offloaded to the accelerator is greater compared to the recursive model.

6 Experimental Setup 

Our methodology and computation models were evaluated using a setup emulating the hardware resources of a hetero-geneous Supercloud. The hardware configuration includes a 48-core ARM CPU, specifically the Huawei Kunpeng 920 [2], paired with a Huawei Ascend 910 AI processor [3], which features 32 Da Vinci AI cores capable of reaching a peak performance of 320 TFLOPS. Communication between these devices is facilitated via a PCIe link. For comparison, the CPU implementations in our experiments are based on multi-threaded, fully optimized versions of Dense Cholesky Factorization from LAPACK [30], [26], as well as SVD functions and subroutines from LAPACK [26]. The input for these experiments is a square matrix of size 32000, resulting in 32000 2 entries.

Back-Transformation 

The final SVD computation stage involves a three-way back-transformation to extract singular vectors. This stage relies solely on accelerators for matrix-matrix multiplications without a specific computation model. In Figure 14 (Left), the latency of the AI accelerator is shown to initially increase and then spike exponentially at refinement levels between 128 and 1024. This escalation results from increasingly larger gemm tasks being offloaded at each iteration. As discussed in Section 4.2.3, the full-to-band reduction’s computational demand directly influences the first back-transformation’s load, impacting overall SVD performance in a heterogeneous Supercloud system. Therefore, the more computational effort takes place in the full-to-band reduction stage, the more computation is required in the first back-transformation stage. This is an important factor and one that can severely limit overall SVD computation performance. 

End-to-End SVD Computation The analysis of all three SVD stages reveals insights into the overall performance of SVD applications in a heterogeneous Supercloud. Figure 14 (Right) compares the speedup between a Supercloud setup and a homogeneous multi-core CPU for an end-to-end SVD computation. The comparison evaluates all stages of computation, with most tasks offloaded to AI accelerators, against a fully optimized CPU-only setup. In the bidiagonalization stage, we assess iterative, blocked, and blocked optimized models. The recursive model is utilized for the Divide and Conquer stage, while back-transformation computations are incrementally offloaded to the Ascend AI accelerator. The Supercloud achieves a peak speedup of 85x for iterative and blocked models at a refinement level of 128, and an 88x speedup for the blocked optimized model, where parallelism is maximized, at refinement levels 128 and 256. 8 Conclusions 

We introduce novel models of performance that we developed and are based on the recursive, iterative, and blocked computation models. Leveraging our tool-chain, we have managed to automatically map Dense Cholesky (CH) Factor-ization and Singular Value Decomposition (SVD) onto a heterogeneous system, consisting of a Kunpeng 48-core ARM CPU and an Ascend AI accelerator device, achieving up to a 17x speedup for CH and up to 88x speedup across the entire end-to-end SVD computation, over fully-optimized ARM CPU baseline implementations.
