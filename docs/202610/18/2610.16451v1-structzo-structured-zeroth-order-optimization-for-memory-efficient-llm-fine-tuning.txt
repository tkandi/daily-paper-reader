## ABSTRACT 

Fine-tuning large language models at scale is increasingly limited by the memory cost of back-propagation. Zeroth-Order (ZO) optimizers reduce this burden by relying only on forward function evaluations, but they still exhibit a substantial performance gap to first-order (FO) optimizers such as Adam. In this work, we argue that this gap does not arise solely from the absence of explicit gradients: FO fine-tuning also benefits from informative low-rank optimization geometry, large-scale signal aggregation, and well-conditioned update directions. Motivated by this perspective, we introduce 

StructZO , a struct ured Zeroth-Order optimization framework for memory-efficient LLM fine-tuning. StructZO performs large-query ZO estimation within an activation-induced informative subspace and applies spectral control to the resulting reduced-space update before mapping it back to the original parameters. This unified update procedure enables ZO optimization to aggregate useful directional signals in a compact, geometry-aware space while avoiding the activation and optimizer-state memory costs of backpropagation. We provide a theoretical analysis showing that, when the induced subspace captures concentrated optimization-relevant directions, StructZO reduces the effective ZO estimation dimension from mn to mr , with the remaining projection error governed by the spectral tail of the activation Gram matrix. Empirically, across OPT-series and Llama3.1-8B on diverse downstream fine-tuning tasks, StructZO improves over the strongest existing ZO baselines by up to 8.7% relative on average across model settings and up to 26 .9% relative on individual tasks, while remaining within 1.5% of FO-Adam on average. These results suggest that in the evaluated fine-tuning settings, structured and spectrally controlled ZO updates can substantially narrow the gap between ZO and FO optimization. 

## 1 Introduction 

Fine-tuning large language models (LLMs) has become a central component in adapting large pretrained models to downstream tasks [1]. While first-order (FO) optimizers such as Adam [2] and AdamW [3] are commonly used in fine-tuning, they require full backpropagation through the model and therefore incur substantial memory overhead from storing intermediate activations and optimizer states. These limitations have driven increasing interest in parameter-and memory-efficient fine-tuning methods [4, 5, 6, 7]. Zeroth-Order (ZO) optimization [8], exemplified by MeZO [9], provides a promising alternative by replacing gradient computation with forward-pass-only perturbation estimates. As a result, MeZO reduces memory consumption close to the inference level, making full-parameter LLM fine-tuning feasible under tight memory budgets. Despite this memory advantage, ZO fine-tuning still suffers from slow convergence and a persistent performance gap to first-order methods, largely due to noisy estimates in high-dimensional and ill-conditioned parameter spaces. Since MeZO, recent work has improved ZO fine-tuning from several perspectives, including low-rank structure or subspace [10, 11, 12], alternative sampling distributions [13, 14], momentum and variance-reduction techniques [15, 16], multi-sample estimators [17], and Hessian- or curvature-informed updates [18, 19]. These advances show that the naive full-space random estimator is far from optimal. However, most existing methods still focus primarily on improving the gradient estimation itself, while remaining limited by query efficiency, search-space quality, or update conditioning. In contrast, FO fine-tuning benefits from more than gradient access alone: it also exploits informative optimization geometry, aggregates rich derivative signals, and applies well-conditioned update transformations. This motivates the arXiv:2610.16451v1 [cs.LG] 12 Oct 2026 following question: Can we design a forward-only ZO optimizer that recovers these structural benefits of FO fine-tuning without incurring the memory cost of backpropagation? 

In this paper, we propose StructZO , a struct ured Zeroth-Order optimization framework that improves forward-only LLM fine-tuning by performing large-query directional signal aggregation inside activation-induced informative subspaces, thereby converting additional ZO queries into more useful low-dimensional update estimates without storing activations for backpropagation. Since these subspaces are intended to approximate the right-side geometry of the gradient, StructZO applies Muon-style spectral shaping directly in the reduced coordinates before mapping the update back to the original parameter space, producing well-conditioned directions that better reflect FO-style optimization behavior. Existing works address these factors in isolation, while StructZO provides the first coupled design : the informative reduced space determines where ZO queries are spent, large-query estimation aggregates signals within this space, and spectral shaping is applied in the same coordinates before the update is lifted back. This coupling changes the role of multi-query ZO from noisy full-space gradient estimation to structured signal aggregation, making it possible to analyze the resulting variance–bias trade-off. Empirically, StructZO consistently improves over existing ZO baselines under comparable forward-query budgets and narrows the gap to FO-Adam in our evaluated fine-tuning settings. Our contributions are summarized as follows: • We introduce StructZO, a unified structured ZO framework that performs estimation and update conditioning in an informative reduced space, recovering several key optimization benefits of FO fine-tuning without backpropagation. • We show that this structured update space makes large-query ZO estimation effective in practice: additional queries are aggregated along more useful low-dimensional directions, while the resampling trick avoids storing full perturbation directions. • We provide an MSE analysis that characterizes the variance–bias trade-off of StructZO. The result shows that the ZO estimation dimension is reduced from mn to mr , while the remaining projection bias is controlled by the spectral tail of the activation Gram matrix. • Experiments on OPT and Llama3.1 models show that StructZO consistently outperforms existing ZO baselines, achieves lower training loss, scales to larger models, and remains close to FO-Adam while preserving the memory advantage of forward-only optimization. For example, StructZO improves the average score on Llama3.1-8B from 76 .3 to 82 .9 over the strongest ZO baseline, and improves CB from 73 .2 to 92 .9.

## 2 Related Work 

Fine-tuning LLMs. Fine-tuning large language models with first-order optimizers such as Adam and AdamW is effective but memory-intensive. This motivates parameter- and memory-efficient fine-tuning methods, including adapters [20], prefix tuning [21, 7], LoRA [4], and low-rank optimizer-memory reduction methods such as GaLore [6, 22]. Although these methods reduce the number of trainable parameters or optimizer states, they still rely on backpropagation and therefore retain nontrivial activation-memory cost. 

Zeroth-Order Optimization. Zeroth-order optimization estimates update directions using only objective function values and has been widely studied in adversarial learning [23, 24], reinforcement learning [25], and automated ML [26]. Recently, MeZO [9] showed that ZO optimization can fine-tune LLMs with memory close to inference, making it attractive under limited hardware budgets. Recent works improve ZO fine-tuning through low-rank and structured perturbation [10, 11, 12, 13, 14], variance reduction and momentum techniques [15, 16, 17], or Hessian-informed preconditioning [18, 19]. Together, these methods demonstrate that ZO fine-tuning can be substantially improved by exploiting gradient structure, model architecture, and carefully designed perturbation distributions. 

Subspace Optimization. Subspace-based optimization exploits the observation that useful update directions in large models often concentrate in a much lower-dimensional structure than the full parameter space [6, 22]. In the ZO setting, low-rank updates are frequently achieved through the use of randomly sampled projection matrices combined with lazy updates [27, 10, 12]. More closely related to our work, AGZO [11] takes an important step by constructing activation-guided subspaces for more informative ZO perturbations. Together, these works motivate a systematic study of the interaction between activation-induced spaces, large-query signal aggregation, and spectral update conditioning in ZO optimization. 23 Method 

3.1 Motivation 

First-order fine-tuning benefits from more than explicit gradient access. In practice, effective FO updates exploit informative low-dimensional geometry, aggregate derivative signals across many tokens and parameters, and apply well-conditioned update transformations. Standard ZO methods lack these properties: they perturb the full parameter space, rely on very limited directional information per step, and usually apply unconditioned updates. StructZO is designed to recover these structural benefits in a forward-only optimization pipeline by estimating updates in an informative reduced space and applying spectral shaping before lifting them back to the original parameters.

3.3.1 Activation-Induced Informative Subspaces 

Consider a Transformer linear layer Y = AW ⊤, where A ∈ RB×n denotes the input activation matrix of the current mini-batch and W ∈ Rm×n is the trainable weight matrix. From back-propagation, ∇W L = ( ∂L ∂Y )⊤A := ∆ ⊤A and subsequently: 

(∇W L)⊤∇W L = A⊤∆∆ ⊤A. (3) When the output-side covariance ∆∆ ⊤ is relatively isotropic or slowly varying, the right-side geometry of ∇W L is largely governed by the activation Gram matrix A⊤A. This suggests that the dominant eigenspace of A⊤A provides an informative coordinate system for ZO perturbations. Instead of using isotropic random projections, StructZO therefore efficiently constructs an informative subspace directly from activations. Specifically, we sample a Gaussian sketch matrix Ω ∈ Rn×(r+k), where r is the target rank and k is an oversampling factor. We compute Y = CΩ = A⊤(AΩ) , followed by QR decomposition Y = QR . The informative subspace basis is then defined as P = Q[: ,:r] ∈ Rn×r . Importantly, this construction is performed entirely from forward activations and does not require storing activations for backward propagation. The resulting projector Π = PP ⊤

captures dominant activation directions and defines a reduced optimization space.

## 4 Experiments 

Overview. We evaluate StructZO on autoregressive LLMs across classification, multiple-choice, and generation tasks from GLUE [28] and SuperGLUE [29], comparing with representative ZO baselines, including MeZO [9], LOZO [10], and HiZOO/HiZOO-L [18], as well as FO-Adam, on OPT-1.3B and Llama3.1-8B, and further test scalability on OPT-13B and OPT-30B. We report downstream performance, loss convergence, ablations, memory usage, and wall-clock time. Empirically, StructZO consistently outperforms existing ZO methods and substantially narrows the gap to FO-Adam. Its advantage comes from jointly leveraging informative subspaces, multi-query signal aggregation, and spectral shaping, which yields faster and deeper loss reduction while retaining the low-memory benefit of forward-only ZO optimization. 

Setup. We implement all methods in the same training pipeline with identical data preprocessing, prompt templates, batching, and evaluation protocols. Following prior ZO fine-tuning work [9, 10, 18], each task uses 1,024 training examples, 512 development examples, and 1,024 evaluation examples; for small datasets such as CB and COPA, we reserve 100 examples for validation and use the rest for training. Unless otherwise specified, all ZO methods use batch size 16. Single-query ZO baselines are trained for 20K steps, while StructZO uses q = 100 queries and is trained for 

400 steps, giving a comparable forward-query scale but richer signal aggregation per update. FO-Adam is trained for 5 epochs with smaller task-dependent batch sizes when necessary. Hyperparameters are selected on the development set, and all experiments are conducted on a single NVIDIA H20 GPU with 96 GiB memory. Additional details are in Appendix B.

4.2 Ablation Study 

Table 3 studies two ablated variants of StructZO: StructZO-R uses a random reduced space, while StructZO-S replaces spectral shaping with SGD-style updates. The full StructZO consistently outperforms both variants on OPT-1.3B and Llama3.1-8B. The clear drop from removing either part indicates that the effectiveness of StructZO comes from their joint interaction rather than from any single isolated component. Figure 3 further examines the effect of the query number q. Increasing q leads to faster and lower loss reduction, suggesting that once queries are collected in a more informative reduced space, larger query budgets can be effectively converted into more accurate update directions, similar in spirit to the signal aggregation in FO optimization. Notably, although the SGD-style variant can be closer to the raw gradient G shown in Figure 1, it still lags behind the full StructZO, suggesting that the spectrally conditioned direction, rather than raw gradient approximation alone, is crucial for effective optimization.

P ∈ Rn×r for each weight matrix W ∈ Rm×n, costing O(rn ) storage. With the resampling trick, perturbations and reduced-space estimates are regenerated from random seeds, avoiding storage of all q query directions. Therefore, StructZO adds only modest memory overhead compared with MeZO, avoids the per-parameter curvature states used by HiZOO-style methods, and still achieves up to 11 .2× memory reduction over FO-Adam.

## 5 Conclusion 

We introduced StructZO, a structured zeroth-order optimization framework for memory-efficient LLM fine-tuning. By aggregating large-query directional signals in an informative subspace and applying spectral shaping in reduced coordinates, StructZO improves the effectiveness of forward-only optimization. As a limitation, StructZO depends on the forward-induced subspace capturing optimization-relevant directions. When this structure is weak, the benefit may decrease, as suggested by the projection-bias term in our analysis. In addition, our experiments focus on GLUE/SuperGLUE-style limited-data fine-tuning, and extending the evaluation to longer-context and more complex generation tasks remains promising future work. 80 5K 10K 15K 20K 25K 30K 35K 40K

Detailed memory comparison. Table 6 reports the detailed peak GPU memory usage on MultiRC with batch size 1 and an average sequence length of about 400 tokens. StructZO maintains memory usage close to inference-level ZO baselines across all tested model scales. Compared with MeZO and LOZO, StructZO introduces only a small additional memory cost, e.g., 4.29 GiB versus 4.13 GiB on OPT-1.3B, 20.80 GiB versus 20.55 GiB on Llama3.1-8B, and 63.67 GiB versus 60.98 GiB on OPT-30B. This modest overhead mainly comes from storing the activation-induced basis P ,while the query perturbations are regenerated through the resampling trick rather than stored explicitly. In contrast, FO-Adam requires substantially larger memory due to backpropagation activations and optimizer states. StructZO reduces peak memory from 27.14 GiB to 4.29 GiB on OPT-1.3B, from 85.29 GiB to 20.80 GiB on Llama3.1-8B, from 316 GiB to 28.33 GiB on OPT-13B, and from 633 GiB to 63.67 GiB on OPT-30B. These results show that StructZO preserves the memory advantage of forward-only ZO optimization while scaling to substantially larger models than standard FO fine-tuning under the same memory budget. 

Wall-clock time. Table 7 provides a detailed wall-clock comparison on OPT-1.3B and Llama3.1-8B across SST-2, RTE, and BoolQ. For single-query ZO baselines, we measure the average time over the first 100 optimization steps and estimate the total training time using 20K steps. For StructZO, we measure the average time over the first 10 optimization steps and estimate the total training time using 400 steps, following our main experimental setting with 

q = 100 . As expected, StructZO has a substantially larger per-step cost because each update performs large-query estimation and constructs structured perturbations in the activation-induced subspace. However, this cost should be interpreted together with the number of optimization steps: each StructZO step aggregates much richer directional information, allowing the method to use 50 × fewer update steps than the single-query baselines. As a result, StructZO achieves comparable or lower end-to-end training time than MeZO and LOZO in most settings, and is consistently much faster than HiZOO/HiZOO-L. For example, on Llama3.1-8B, StructZO reduces the estimated total time from 3.656h to 2.954h on SST-2, from 9.79h to 9.17h on RTE, and from 14.99h to 14.76h on BoolQ compared with MeZO. These results show that although StructZO trades higher per-step latency for more informative updates, the resulting reduction in required optimization steps leads to favorable overall wall-clock efficiency.
