# HOW INNER OPTIMIZERS SHAPE REPLICA SCALING

## ABSTRACT 

Training large language models (LLMs) across geographically distributed and poorly interconnected compute pools or data centers has become a topic of signifi-cant interest. This has led to increased study of local optimization methods such as DiLoco for LLMs, especially those that use local (or inner) adaptive or precondi-tioned optimizers, which are critical for high-performance LLM training. However, a central challenge remains: it is well known that increasing the number of model replicas (replica scaling) while fixing the total training budget leads to monotoni-cally decreasing performance. Existing work has empirically demonstrated that the choice of inner optimizer (Muon v.s. AdamW) can significantly improve this trend, but offers only limited explanation of the performance difference. In this work, we consider a range of inner-optimizers and identify two important factors that impact replica scaling: (1) the covariance structure of the pseudo-gradients and (2) the impact of batch size on the pseudogradient’s noise scale. Based on these insights, we theoretically and empirically demonstrate that whitening-based pre-conditioning is a powerful inner-optimizer desideratum. Our empirical evaluation extends the results to reveal that whitening-based inner-optimizers achieve strong replica scaling (for 8 − 96 replicas), with performance relative to DP improving as parameters and data are scaled. 

## 1 INTRODUCTION 

The computational scale of language-model training in-creasingly exceeds the capacity of a single site. Training across geographically separated clusters can pool other-wise fragmented resources, but the connectivity between these clusters is poor, leading to significant communica-tion bottlenecks. Local optimization methods address this constraint by allowing replicas to optimize independently for several steps between parameter or pseudo-gradient averages (Douillard et al., 2023; 2025; Liu et al., 2024) often drastically reducing the frequency of communica-tion. A central difficulty in practical local optimization is replica scaling: at a fixed token budget, increasing the number of replicas often degrades the final model per-formance (Charles et al., 2025) relative to its non-local data-parallel (DP) counterpart. This can limit the broad applicability of these methods. It has been shown that the replica scaling can improve with increasing model size (Charles et al., 2025). Recent empirical results show that modifying either the inner optimizer (Th´ erien et al., 2025) or the outer aggregation rule (Horoi et al., 2026) can fur-ther improve the scalability and efficiency of distributed training. However, the impact of inner optimizers on scaling is not well understood. 1arXiv:2610.19064v1 [cs.LG] 13 Oct 2026 Preprint Classical analyses of local SGD and federated averaging study the discrepancy accumulated over multiple local steps, often with heterogeneous client data. The primary source of performance degradation is often attributed to client drift or other notions of divergence of model replicas along their trajectory (Karimireddy et al., 2020). Much of this work and theory (Stich, 2019; Karimireddy et al., 2020; Yu et al., 2019) focuses on SGD and momentum SGD as the inner optimizer, a single local step from common parameters and optimizer states that commute with averaging: averaging the updates gives the same result as updating with the averaged gradient. Nonlinear adaptive inner optimizers need not satisfy this property and are much less well understood in the context of this literature. Notably, a discrepancy can arise on the first step, even with identical starting states and identically distributed data, because transforming each local gradient before averaging differs from transforming the averaged gradient. Understanding replica scaling therefore requires examining how the inner optimizer transforms gradient noise, in addition to how parameters drift over successive local steps. In this work, we focus on a broad range of popular adaptive or preconditioner based optimizers and identify two critical properties that can greatly impact scaling: (a) the covariance structure of the pseudo-gradient induced by the local optimization (b) how the optimizers deal with statistical estimation of second moments in the low batch size setting. Our contributions can be summarized as follows: • We demonstrate, both empirically and theoretically, the importance of the pseudogradient covariance structure induced by the choice of inner optimizer. Specifically, we link the choice of inner optimizer to the maximum discrepancy with respect to the DP update direction. • We identify the importance of appropriately scaling β2 when using second-moment optimizer states, validating our findings through theoretical analysis and experiments. This reveals that the recently introduced IsoLoCo method (Horoi et al., 2026), using an Adam inner optimizer, can outperform the much more computationally expensive MuLoCo (Th´ erien et al., 2025). • Our results demonstrate that using a SOAP (Vyas et al., 2025) or SPlus (Frans et al., 2025) inner optimizer can lead to very effective replica scaling , obtaining significantly improved performance relative to existing methods from the literature (as much as 6% less degradation relative to DP at 96 replicas). 

## 2 RELATED WORK 

Local-step methods and DiLoCo. Local SGD (Stich, 2019) and federated averaging (McMahan et al., 2017) reduce communication by taking several local steps between averaging events. DiLoCo (Douillard et al., 2023) adapts this to LLM pretraining by utilizing a large number of inner steps, AdamW (Loshchilov & Hutter, 2019) as the inner optimizer, and SGD with Nesterov momentum on the aggregated pseudo-gradients as the outer optimizer. Follow-ups relax synchrony (Liu et al., 2024) and overlap or shard the exchange of information between replicas (Douillard et al., 2025). Charles et al. (2025) establish scaling laws for DiLoCo and document the central difficulty we study: adding replicas at a fixed token budget degrades quality, with the optimal hyperparameters shifting as 

R grows. Our analysis complements these scaling laws by tracing the degradation back to local state estimation and to the nonlinear optimizer transforms applied before aggregation. Local methods can also be complemented with local optimization by using Sparse parameter averaging (SPARTA; Reed & Baioumy, 2025) or Top-k sparsification SparseLoCo (Sarfi et al., 2025). IsoLoco Horoi et al. (2026) views DiLoCo’s outer step as task-arithmetic model merging and replaces plain pseudo-gradient averaging with Iso-C merging (Marczak et al., 2025). For each weight matrix, it takes the SVD of the averaged pseudo-gradient and replaces all singular values with their mean, giving ¯σU V ⊤. It then applies the usual Nesterov momentum outer step. This was shown to improve worker scaling over DiLoCo (Horoi et al., 2026). 

Small-batch training and adaptive-optimizer scaling rules. Scaling rules relate optimizer hyper-parameters to batch size (Malladi et al., 2022); most directly, Marek et al. (2025) show that Adam trains stably at very small batches provided β2 is set so that the second moment’s half-life is fixed in 

tokens rather than in steps. Replica scaling in DiLoCo induces exactly this small-batch regime for 2Preprint each individual replica. Section 4.4 transfers the β2 scaling rule to replica scaling; to our knowledge this hasn’t been studied has not been studied in the local-step setting previously. 

Spectral and second-order optimizers. Shampoo (Gupta et al., 2018) preconditions with Kronecker-factored curvature estimates; SOAP (Vyas et al., 2025) runs Adam in Shampoo’s eigenba-sis; Muon (Jordan et al., 2024) orthogonalizes matrix updates with no accumulated second-moment statistics. SPlus (Frans et al., 2025) retains Shampoo’s historical eigenbasis but signs momentum in that basis, avoiding cached eigenvalue magnitudes and SOAP’s additional coordinate-wise second moment. SPlus uses this instant-sign transformation independently in local DiLoCo replica bases; our focus is its distributed covariance and replica-scaling behavior. Controlled single-stream comparisons find the ranking horizon-dependent (Wen et al., 2025), while a recent multi-billion-parameter study reports a slight edge for a stabilized SOAP over Muon at very large batch, at the price of memory and numerical-stability engineering (Khona et al., 2026). In the DiLoCo setting, MuLoCo (Th´ erien et al., 2025) uses Muon as the inner optimizer and reports more directionally consistent pseudo-gradients as R grows, while IsoLoCo (Horoi et al., 2026) treats outer aggregation as model merging and isotropizes the aggregated pseudo-gradient. Section 4 develops a complementary criterion: whether the stochastic update geometry emitted by each replica remains well conditioned under averaging. 

## 3 BACKGROUND : THE DILOCO FRAMEWORK 

The methods in this paper can be seen as an instance of Algorithm 1 which is a generalization of local optimization from Reddi et al. (2021); Douillard et al. (2023) that utilizes both inner (on the local gradients) and outer optimization (on the pseudo-gradients). R replicas start each round from the shared parameters, each runs H steps of an inner optimizer on its own data stream. The resulting displacements ∆r are uniformly averaged, and an outer optimizer applies the aggregate ¯∆ to the shared parameters from the start of the round. DiLoCo (Douillard et al., 2023) instantiates the inner optimizer as AdamW and the outer as Nesterov momentum. For example MuLoCo (Th´ erien et al., 2025) replaces the inner optimizer with Muon. IsoLoCo (Horoi et al., 2026) isotropizes ¯∆ before the outer step. The inner optimizer states sr are never communicated: each is estimated locally from its replica’s data stream.

## 4 INNER OPTIMIZERS IMPACT ON REPLICA SCALING 

The transformations applied to local updates by local optimizers can have a significant impact on the final aggregated pseudo-gradient. We investigate how the choice of inner optimizer shapes the distribution of the resulting pseudo-gradients and their deviation from the corresponding non-local updates. Section 4.1 introduces the optimizers considered in this work, Section 4.2 separates the update error (relative to non-local steps with the same base optimizer) into a mean mismatch term and a stochastic error term. It shows how whitening controls the largest directional variance, and how matching covariance structures across replicas makes uniform averaging optimal. Section 4.4 specializes this framework to one step from common state: under the stated Gaussian model, SPlus 3Preprint admits a scalar calibration that matches the DP mean to first order in the global proposal’s signal-to-noise ratio. Finally, Section 4.4 bounds the Adam mean mismatch caused by fresh-gradient second moments and explains how appropriately adjusting β2 limits that bound as local batches shrink. It then isolates a complementary advantage of SPlus: retaining the estimated basis without relying on coordinatewise scale estimates to control the covariance term. 4.1 BACKGROUND ON POPULAR OPTIMIZERS

Discussion of Practical Implications Our results suggest that local optimization methods can be deployed across a large number of replicas (50+) while maintaining minimal performance degradation of < 3% when utilizing SOAP or SPlus. Notably, these methods consume more memory than Muon due to the need to maintain L and R, however, scalable recipes for SOAP have been studied (Khona et al., 2026), demonstrating that it can perform well at very large scale. A particularly relevant setting for our work is cross-data center training where each replica has a considerable number of chips that can be used to shard the additional optimizer states and perform the optimizer step in parallel. At 178M and 512M scales, our results suggest that at a smaller number of replicas SOAP is the preferred inner optimizer, while at a large number of replicas, SPlus performs best. Another observation of our work is that the recently proposed IsoLoCo outer optimizer, which has nearly the same overhead as AdamW inner optimizer-based DiLoCo, can perform very effectively at a large number of replicas simply by adjusting its β2 according to the local batch size. When doing so, we show that it outperforms MuLoCo. This can make it an excellent candidate for settings where each replica has limited memory or existing infrastructure and training recipe are already well designed for Adam. 5.3 ANALYSIS : COVARIANCE STRUCTURE AND MEAN MISMATCH

## 6 CONCLUSION 

We have shown that differences to the DP pseudogradient can be seen to result from two primary sources of error: mean mismatch and stochastic error. Building on these insights, we showed that covariance structure in optimizers like AdamW is very important. In our empirical study, we establish the performance of different inner optimizers (SOAP, SPlus, Muon, AdamW), which have more and less appealing properties for reducing stochastic error according to our theory. We find that SPlus reaches the strongest performance at higher worker counts, and the best overall scaling with respect to DP, while SOAP reaches the best DP performance and seems on trend to become the best inner optimizer at scale. We then directly test our theoretical claims by calculating the mean mismatch and stochastic error for each optimizer, showing that these quantities are directly correlated with the observed performance trends.

The estimator is conditional on one checkpoint and one set of replica-local states. Each method is evaluated on its own trained trajectory, so a cross-method difference can reflect both the update rule and the state reached by training; it is not a same-state causal ablation. Concatenating six matrices also mixes within-matrix anisotropy, scale imbalance between matrices, and cross-matrix covariance. The input streams are disjoint segments of one shuffled replica-local permutation, rather than independent samples drawn with replacement. The unbiasedness equalities above are therefore exact only under the stated IID idealization. Moreover, the n(n − 1) and n2 sums reuse the same n

vectors; their terms are not independent observations. Finally, interpreting Equation (C.12) as the covariance of the actual average requires vanishing conditional cross-replica noise covariances. Lower a indicates a more isotropic eC, independently of total noise magnitude. For SOAP, the saved position relative to a basis-refresh step can also affect the measured covariance: the fresh gradient may enter both the basis and the proposal. At this single checkpoint, the probe characterizes update geometry but does not identify its causal contribution to final loss. C.2 DP-REFERENCED DIRECTIONAL MEAN AND NOISE 

Each optimizer is evaluated at its own round-75 checkpoint.
