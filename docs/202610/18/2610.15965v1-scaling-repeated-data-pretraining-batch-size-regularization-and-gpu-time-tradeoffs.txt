# Scaling Repeated-Data Pretraining: Batch Size, Regularization, and GPU-Time Tradeoffs 

Sewon Min 

UC Berkeley & Allen Institute for AI 

sewonm@berkeley.edu 

ABSTRACT 

We study pretraining when compute is abundant but unique training data are finite, requiring repeated training on the same data. We first revisit infinite-compute pretraining (Kim et al., 2026) and show that its frontier depends critically on choices previously fixed or studied only in isolation. In particular, optimizing batch size together with epoch counts reveals a U-shaped relationship between validation loss and batch size, favoring smaller batches than typical pretraining, likely because small-batch gradient noise provides implicit regularization. We further find that training recipes, previously optimized under a single-pass assumption, can be tuned, e.g., repacking documents into new sequences every epoch, weight tying, and positive embedding weight decay. We then relax the infinite-compute assumption. In particular, smaller-than-typical batch sizes can require prohibitively long sequential training, motivating to treat GPUs and time as separate resources. Tighter deadlines favor larger batches, and latency-oriented parallelism can trade additional GPUs for shorter training. Nevertheless, even under a fixed time budget, a smaller batch with fewer GPUs can achieve lower validation loss than a larger batch with more GPUs, challenging the common practice of increasing data parallelism to maximize GPU utilization under a deadline. Together, our results identify underexplored factors in repeated-data training and provide concrete guidance on how training recipes and resource allocation should change from single-pass pretraining. 

1 INTRODUCTION 

Most language-model pretraining assumes a single pass over a large, diverse web corpus, with compute as the binding resource and data treated as effectively unlimited (Kaplan et al., 2020; Hoffmann et al., 2022). However, the supply of high-quality human-written data is finite and grows at a physically limited rate (Villalobos et al., 2024), so continued scaling increasingly requires training repeatedly on the same data (Muennighoff et al., 2023). Prior work asks how much performance can be extracted from fixed data when compute is effectively unlimited (Kim et al., 2026; Xu et al., 2026). We revisit this setting and show that the infinite-compute frontier depends critically on choices previously fixed or studied only in isolation. In particular, jointly optimizing batch size, learning rate, weight decay, and the number of repetitions 1 reveals a U-shaped relationship between validation loss and batch size, with the optimum at smaller batches than in conventional pretraining. The gradient noise from smaller batches, which can be undesirable in single-pass training, likely provides useful implicit regularization against repeated-data overfitting. Batch tuning extends beneficial repetitions from 8 to 20 for a 1.5B model and from 40 to 112 for a 153M model, both with substantial loss improvements (Figure 1(a), blue). We further find that training recipes, which are typically optimized under a single-pass assump-tion, can be adapted specifically for repeated-data training. We evaluate three simple practices. Together, these choices improve validation loss and shift the best checkpoint from epoch 20 to 32 for the 1.5B model and from epoch 112 to 240 for the 153M model (Figure 1(a), blue vs. orange). We next move beyond the infinite compute to consider compute resources, as compute is not truly infinite in practice. In particular, the improved frontier can require prohibitively long sequential training, as it favors smaller-than-typical batch sizes. For example, our fitted extrapolation suggests that, for a 70B model trained on 10T unique tokens, reaching the near-optimal stopping point could require over 20 years. Motivated by this tension, we model GPU availability and sequential training time as separate resource constraints (Figure 1(b)). Under short deadlines, larger batches process more data per optimizer step; as more time becomes available, progressively smaller batches become preferable until the best observed unconstrained B⋆ is reached. Separately, latency-oriented parallelism can trade additional GPUs for faster optimizer steps. Nevertheless, even under a fixed time budget, a smaller batch with fewer GPUs can achieve lower validation loss than a larger batch with more GPUs, especially with longer deadlines (Figure 1(c)). This finding suggests that the common practice of maximizing parallelism with more GPUs under a fixed deadline may be suboptimal for repeated-data training. In summary, this paper shows that, when unique data become the binding constraint, both pretraining recipes and compute allocation must change. 1. Batch size must account for repeated-data generalization. Repeated-data training favors smaller batches than those used in conventional pretraining (§3). 2. Training recipes must be adapted to repeated exposure. Repacking documents across epochs and regularizing embeddings improve attainable loss and make more repetitions beneficial (§4). 3. Compute allocation must distinguish GPUs from time. Maximizing GPU utilization through larger batches can be suboptimal: even under the same deadline, smaller batches using fewer GPUs can achieve lower loss (§5). 

2 RELATED WORK 

Compute-bound scaling. Conventional language-model scaling laws predict loss from model size, training tokens, and compute, then use these relationships to select compute-optimal model–data allocations (Kaplan et al., 2020; Hoffmann et al., 2022). They effectively assume an unlimited supply of fresh data, likely because available compute at the time had not yet reached the scale needed to exhaust the available stock of human-generated web data. 

Data-bound scaling. As hardware improves and the stock of high-quality human-written text grows slowly, unique data increasingly becomes a binding resource (Villalobos et al., 2024). This has motivated repeated-data pretraining, in which models train on a fixed dataset until validation gains saturate or reverse because of overfitting (Muennighoff et al., 2023; Xue et al., 2023). At fixed 2Preprint compute, Fang et al. (2025) show that repeatedly training on filtered data can outperform training on a 10 × larger superset with proper regularization, e.g., weight decay tuning. The infinite-compute, finite-data setting is studied most directly by two recent papers. Kim et al. (2026) fix the unique data, remove the compute constraint, and show that stronger regularization, especially weight decay tuning, substantially delays overfitting. Xu et al. (2026) build on this work and propose additional regularization methods and an interaction-aware scaling law. We revisit the same setting but optimize previously fixed recipe dimensions and then ask how the resulting statistical frontier changes under separate GPU and time constraints. 

Batch size. Batch size optimization in conventional single-pass pretraining balances statistical efficiency and parallelism. At a fixed number of optimizer steps, larger batches process more tokens and are generally favored; at a fixed token budget, smaller batches take more steps and can be more token-efficient. Prior work identifies a critical batch size below which jointly increasing batch size and learning rate preserves token efficiency while improving throughput, and above which token efficiency degrades (McCandlish et al., 2018; Kaplan et al., 2020; Zhang et al., 2025; Merrill et al., 2025). However, reduced token efficiency alone does not imply worse loss under a fixed deadline, since larger batches process more tokens in the available time. Therefore, conventional recipes often increase batch size up to the critical batch size to expose parallelism. For repeated-data training, batch size has received less attention. Kim et al. (2026) select a small batch using a single-pass ablation and then hold it fixed in their repeated-data scaling experiments; Xu et al. (2026) likewise use a fixed batch throughout. We instead fix the unique-data pool and jointly tune the learning rate, weight decay, and repetitions for each batch size. We find that repeated-data training changes the conventional tradeoff: smaller batches can achieve lower validation loss, even under a fixed deadline. 

3 BATCH SIZE SHAPES THE REPEATED -D ATA FRONTIER 

We first revisit the unconstrained repeated-data frontier by jointly optimizing batch size, learning rate, weight decay, and epoch count, and reveal an interior batch-size optimum. Because batch size controls both statistical behavior and available parallelism, this later motivates separating GPU and time constraints in §5. 3.1 EXPERIMENTAL SETUP 

Models & data. We train dense decoder-only Transformers with 153M, 474M, and 1.5B parameters, using the olmo-core (Groeneveld et al., 2024) codebase. All experiments use DCLM-Baseline (Li et al., 2024) as the training corpus, with a 4,096-token context and document-disjoint training, validation, and test splits. This section uses a 1B-token unique-data pool; §4.2 expands the study to 0.333B-, 1B-, and 3B-token pools. Together, the measured grid covers Du/N from 0.22 to 19.61. We train with AdamW (Loshchilov & Hutter, 2019) and a warmup-stable-decay (WSD) learning-rate schedule (Hu et al., 2024; Wen et al., 2025), with a 100M-token warmup and 10% terminal decay. 

Hyperparameter tuning methodology. For a model with N parameters and Du unique tokens, E

passes yield Dtrain = ED u token presentations. We sweep global batches of 16–512 sequences for the 1.5B model and 32–512 for the smaller models. For each (N, D u, B ), we tune the peak learning rate and weight decay through gradual adaptive coordinate search: we first select the pair for an earlier epoch, then proceed to the next epoch by starting from the pair selected for the previous epoch and including neighboring learning rates ( 1/2×

and 2×) and a weight-decay value one level higher ( 3×). We seek the best training recipe at a fixed model size, rather than allowing parameter scaling or ensembling as an alternative route to lower loss (Kim et al., 2026). This choice reflects the practical importance of inference cost. 

Evaluation. Our primary metric is next-token cross-entropy (CE) on a fixed 100M-token DCLM-Baseline validation split. The importance of weight-decay tuning depends on batch size. Prior finite-data studies empha-size tuning weight decay to delay overfitting (Kim et al., 2026; Fang et al., 2025; Xu et al., 2026). We likewise find it important for large batches (e.g., B = 512 ) and in more severely data-constrained settings. Near the batch optimum, however, its effect is smaller: at B = 64 , the weight decay selected at epoch 1, 0.3, remains optimal through saturation.

Complete original vs. repetition-aware recipe comparison. Comparison with open-source default. Our survey of nine representative open-source training stacks (Table 4 in §C.1) shows that these ingredients already appear in some stacks, but in inconsistent combinations that often differ from our best-performing configuration and are generally not motivated by repeated-data behavior. Since our experiments show that these choices materially affect attainable loss, we recommend that researchers and LLM developers treat these choices as explicit design decisions rather than blindly inheriting a stack’s defaults.

5 SCALING ACROSS GPU AND TIME BUDGETS 

We now relax the infinite-compute assumption and consider realistic resource constraints. In particular, our results in §3 motivate treating GPUs and time separately: smaller-than-typical batches may use fewer GPUs but require longer training. This matters because some labs may have abundant (nearly unlimited) GPUs but still finite time. We therefore treat GPU availability and training time as separate constraints and study scaling under limits on time, GPUs, or both. 5.1 MOTIVATION : W HY SEPARATE GPU S AND TIME ?To quantify the potential resource implications, we consider a hypothetical scenario with a dense model with N = 70 B trained on Du = 10 T unique tokens. We fit the loss curve for extrapolation and define E⋆ as the earliest epoch whose fitted validation CE is within 0.001 of its fitted minimum (about 0.1% in perplexity). When training is deadline-constrained, three coupled choices determine the attainable loss: 1. the number of data repetitions (equivalently, the stopping epoch E), 2. the global batch size B, and 3. the compute-parallelism configuration, including the microbatch size and the DP, TP, and PP degrees. Stopping early while retaining the unconstrained batch B⋆ may expose the model to too little data. On the other hand, increasing the batch processes more data per optimizer step, but can eventually yield worse validation loss as repeated-data overfitting emerges. This motivates a deadline-aware recipe that jointly optimizes these choices. 

Short deadlines favor larger batches. Figure 8 shows this tradeoff systematically across training recipes, epoch counts E, and batch sizes B, across all three model sizes. Under short budgets, larger batches tend to be preferred because they process more data per optimizer step. As the budget increases, progressively smaller batches become preferable, until the frontier reaches the best observed unconstrained batch B⋆.Importantly, a fixed time budget does not imply that larger batches are always preferable. Figure 1(c) illustrates this for the 1.5B model. Under a short deadline (equivalent to 8K steps), a larger batch using 64 GPUs achieves the best loss. As the deadline increases (equivalent to 32K steps), however, a small batch using only 16 GPUs achieves a lower loss, despite using strictly fewer GPUs. This indicates that maximizing available GPUs by increasing the global batch size can be suboptimal. 

Deadlines also change the preferred training recipe. For small models (474M and 153M), ORIGINAL may be preferred under very short deadlines, likely because repeated-data overfitting has not yet become relevant. The repetition-aware recipe subsequently becomes optimal and remains so at longer deadlines, including across the entire observed horizon for the 1.5B model. 6 CONCLUSION AND LIMITATIONS 

This work studies pretraining when compute is abundant but unique training data are limited, requiring models to train repeatedly on the same data until validation performance approaches its attainable minimum. We begin with the infinite-compute setting studied in prior work (Kim et al., 2026; Xu et al., 2026), but show that batch size, previously underexplored, materially affects the repeated-data frontier. We further show that this frontier can be substantially improved through repetition-aware training: dynamically repacking sequences each epoch, tying input and output embeddings, and applying positive weight decay to the shared embeddings. However, the infinite-compute optimum can require prohibitively many sequential optimizer steps, making it impractical under tighter training deadlines. This motivates decomposing compute into two distinct resources—GPUs and time—and studying regimes in which either or both are binding. Combining observed results across 153M–1.5B-parameter models and 0.333B–3B-token data pools, we map the attainable frontier across GPU and time budgets. Our results show that optimal data-constrained pretraining requires jointly selecting the number of repetitions, batch size, compute-parallelism configuration, and training recipe. 

Limitations. There are many ways to improve this work but here we highlight the two most important limitations. First, our observations are limited to 153M–1.5B-parameter models and 0.333B–3B-token data pools. In particular, the illustrative 70B/10T example requires significant extrapolation and should be viewed as illustrative projections rather than estimates that have been rigorously validated at the target scale. Second, our findings may depend on the specific architecture (particularly dense models), optimizer and learning-rate schedule (AdamW with WSD), context length, and training data (the relatively heavily filtered DCLM-Baseline corpus). Extending these results from dense models to mixture-of-experts (MoE) is especially likely to affect our conclusions and remains one of the most important directions for future data-constrained scaling research.

All training text comes from DCLM-Baseline (Li et al., 2024). We split documents before packing to form the training and validation sets. The validation set contains 100M tokens. In §3, we reshuffle sequence indices every epoch (defined as S HUFFLE in §4.1), by default. We use a warmup–stable–decay (WSD) learning-rate schedule (Hu et al., 2024; Wen et al., 2025), with an approximately 100M-token warmup and a target-specific terminal decay over the final 10% of each run. We sweep batch sizes of b ∈ { 16 , 32 , 64 , 128 , 256 , 512 } sequences for the 1.5B model and b ∈{32 , 64 , 128 , 256 , 512 } for the 153M and 474M models. We search over peak learning rates in 

{5 × 10 −4, 10 −3, 2 × 10 −3, 4 × 10 −3} and weight decays in {0.011 , 0.033 , 0.1, 0.3, 1.0, 3.0}; values outside these ranges were never selected. Because AdamW applies decay once per optimizer step, its cumulative effect depends on B, so we tune learning rate and weight decay jointly with batch size. We use adaptive coordinate search: at each new epoch, the search starts from the previous best configuration and evaluates neighboring learning rates and higher weight decays. We tune the learning rate on the 1B-token pool and transfer it to the 0.333B- and 3B-token pools, following prior evidence that the optimal learning rate is relatively stable across data-pool sizes (Kim et al., 2026; Xu et al., 2026); our preliminary experiments show the same pattern. One of our key findings is that the gradient noises from small-batch training likely provide beneficial regularization. This raises a natural question: can we recover some of these regularization benefits while retaining the computational efficiency and parallelism of larger-batch training? In other words, can we delay overfitting without sacrificing the parallelism for faster training? We explored several approaches in this direction. 

Batch warmdown. This method gradually reduces the batch size over the course of training. However, in our experiments, it did not improve the training-time-validation-loss Pareto frontier. 

Branch-train-merge. Following Li et al. (2022), we train multiple models initialized from a shared checkpoint and subsequently merges their weights. However, in our experiments, it did not improve the training-time-validation-loss Pareto frontier. 

Structured gradient noise. We add structured gradient noise to large-batch updates. However, it suffered from recurrent gradient spikes and did not yield a stable training trajectory. 

Periodically synchronized local optimization. Among the approches we tested, this was the only one that produced promising results. The general idea is to perform multiple local optimization steps in parallel, and then synchronizing across workers. We consider two concrete methods: (1) LocalSGD (Stich, 2019), which allows each worker to perform H local optimization steps and then averaging model parameters across workers, and (2) DiLoCo (Douillard et al., 2024), which similarly performs multiple local updates between synchronization points, but uses an outer optimizer to update the shared model from the accumulated differences between the initial and locally trained parameters. In our experiments, DiLoCo consistently outperformed LocalSGD, so we report DiLoCo’s results here. We fix the synchronization interval to H = 32 , the outer learning rate to 0.7, and the outer momentum to 0.9. We vary the per-worker batch size over 64 , 256 and initialize from checkpoints obtained after either two or four passes over the data using a global batch size of 1,024, yielding four configurations. After tuning the inner learning rate and weight decay, we report the best result across these configurations for each metric and matched optimizer-step budget. Table 5 shows that DiLoCo does not consistently improve validation CE. It does, however, consis-tently improve downstream accuracy: on HellaSwag, our most reliable downstream metric, it gains approximately 1.7–3.1% over the stronger matched-step baseline. Setup and policies. We model dense decoder-only Transformers at sequence length S = 4 ,096 ,with no context parallelism. Let d, t, p, m, and a denote the data-parallel, tensor-parallel, pipeline-parallel, microbatch, and gradient-accumulation degrees. Then
