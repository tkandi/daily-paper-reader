# Refined Thompson Learning for Adaptive Bandits: Sustainable Power-Efficient Flexibility Scheduling Across Data Centers 

Yifu Ding, Zixi Chen, Ruicheng Ao, David Simchi-Levi, Thomas Magnanti August 4, 2026 

Abstract 

The rapid rise in energy consumption from large-scale AI workloads in data centers placed the increasing pressures on power grids in recent years. Since grids must maintain real-time bal-ance between supply and demand, there has been heightened interest in job flexibility services from data centers. We propose an adaptive, learning-based contextual restless multi-armed bandit (RMAB) framework in which the grid requests load reductions without requiring visibility into job (re)scheduling decisions. With multiple virtual machine (VM) datasets, the core strategy models a cyclic job queue and batch-level (re)scheduling within each data center as a Markov decision process (MDP), and derives Whittle-index-based policies computed from the learned transition and reward functions via Thompson sampling. To address the challenges of enlarging state space and sparse state visits, we introduce domain-knowledge-enriched refined strategies, including adaptive mixed strategies, gated priors, low-rank smoothing, and offline posterior support. Extensive simulations under both baseline and stress scenarios demonstrate that the cellwise best refined variant exceeds TW in every reported cell of the stress sweep at lower computational cost than EXP4. These results demonstrate the economic value of data center flexibility services and underscore the importance of high-quality, open-source AI workload datasets for developing and evaluating adaptive scheduling algorithms. 

# 1 Introduction 

The rapid growth of large-scale AI workloads has significantly increased energy consumption in data centers, placing mounting pressure on power grids worldwide. In the U.S., electricity demand from data centers is projected to increase by 7%–12% by 2030 [EPRI, 2024], while globally, data center electricity consumption is expected to at least double over the next five years [Chen, 2025]. In response to growing environmental and societal responsibilities, major cloud providers have begun voluntarily participating in demand response programs. For example, Google has collaborated with electric utilities on demand response initiatives to alleviate grid stress [Terrall, 2025]. Recent studies [Senga et al., 2026] investi-gating geographically distributed data centers across multiple U.S. regions further demonstrate that exploiting temporal workload flexibility can substantially reduce operating costs, although its impact on carbon emissions depends strongly on the availability of renewable energy resources. Data center power consumptions can be reduced through a variety of complementary approaches, including intelligent workload scheduling [Dai et al., Liu et al., 2026], dynamic voltage and frequency scaling (DVFS) [Gu et al., 2025], and designs of energy-efficient or energy-aware AI systems [Song et al., 2026, Chung et al.]. Despite these advances, data center operators are often reluctant to disclose detailed workload characteristics or internal scheduling policies because of privacy, security, and com-mercial concerns. In practice, they are typically willing to share only coarse-grained information, such as workload flexibility classes, aggregate power consumption, or historical VM statistics. Although 1arXiv:2608.00921v1 [cs.CE] 2 Aug 2026 formal grid codes governing data center demand response are not yet in place, future grid operators are expected to coordinate flexibility requests across geographically distributed data centers under highly uncertain operating conditions, requiring the balance of economic benefits, grid reliability, and quality-of-service guarantees. 

# 2 Related works 

Job scheduling in dynamic data center management: VM is a software-defined computing instance that runs on a physical host while appearing as an independent machine. In this paper, we formulate data center flexibility services as a job (re)scheduling problem using open-source VM datasets. These VM datasets, such as [Cortez et al., 2017], and [Zhang et al., 2025], are incorporated heterogeneous telemetry measurements, such as CPU or GPU utilization and memory usage, along with software-level labels including physical machine attributes, job types, and service-level agreement (SLA). [Guillen-Perez et al., 2026] introduced a benchmark reinforcement learning (RL) framework for scheduling geo-distributed data centers, leveraging VM datasets that capture AI workloads alongside diverse environmental and grid-related data, such as electricity prices, carbon intensity, and weather conditions. [Zhang et al., 2025] collects the industrial-scale VM datasets and develops a comprehensive job rescheduling framework using optimization, metaheuristic, heuristic, and machine learning-based methodologies. These works including [Zhang et al., 2025] and [Guillen-Perez et al., 2026], generally assume full observability of all information contained in the VM traces. 

RMAB and index-based policy: The CRMAB framework is widely employed for optimal resource allocation under uncertainty, where each arm’s state space is modeled as a MDP. However, CRMAB problems are generally intractable and are known to be NP-hard with the expanding state space. To address this challenge, index policies are introduced to decouple the arms and enable tractable, per-arm decision making that leads to near-optimal solutions. Whittle [Whittle, 1988] proposed an index policy based on Lagrangian relaxation, which evaluates the marginal value of activating each arm independently. The Whittle index has been applied to stochastic deadline scheduling problems [Yu et al., 2016], [Graczov´ a and Jacko, 2014], where indexability is established and efficient algorithms are derived to compute the index in closed form. Arms are then prioritized and activated according to their index values. The index-based RMAB has also been applied to model demand responses in electric grids [Chen and Hou, 2024] and sustainable development. For instance, Chen and Hou [2024] proposed an online learning algorithm for RMAB-based building demand responses that estimates the index-based policy in the presence of unknown state-transition dynamics, assuming access to the perfect contextual information. 

Refined Thompson Learning for bandits: During the index policy computation in CRMAB, the reward and transition functions are typically unknown. Thompson learning is a commonly used approach in bandit settings to infer transition dynamics and reward functions from observed data. The parameterization and updates of the prior distribution depend on state visits, which are observed adaptively over the course of bandit interactions. The choice of the prior distribution can significantly affect learning performances, and a poorly-chosen prior may lead to slower convergence and lower accumulated rewards [Russo et al., 2018]. A common baseline for the prior distribution is to assume specified continuous or discrete distributions, such as Gaussian distributions or Gaussian mixture models (GMMs) [Liang et al., 2025]. Recent works explore the use of neural networks (NNs) and generative models to construct task-specific priors. For instance, [Hsieh et al., 2023] leverages the 2trained diffusion models for prior representation. These NN-based approaches rely on relatively large datasets for training. 

2.1 Contributions and paper organization 

To bridge gaps in practical deployment and theoretical development, We develop a CRMAB framework, 

Robust and Adaptive Computing and Energy Resource coordination framework plus the refined strategies (RACER+) to support data center flexibility services via workload (re)scheduling within each data center. Workloads are modeled using real-world batched job traces. When participating in flexibility services, VM rescheduling can enhance service quality and resource efficiency by shifting jobs to more energy-efficient configurations. The open-source code is provided in the anonymized code supplement. Four main contributions are summarized as follows: 

• High-fidelity framework for data center scheduling with calibrated ML/AL training and inference datasets : We develop a CRMAB framework for energy-efficient data center flexibility service scheduling, grounded in real-world, calibrated VM datasets ranging from light-weight workloads to ML/AL training and inference. 

• An adaptive mixed-strategy bandit approach driven by state visitation frequencies, supported by theoretical analyses of the L1 transition error and off-support leak-age: We develop an adaptive mixed-strategy policy for the CRMAB problem, where weights of different strategies adapts to the progress of Thompson learning and state visitation. The improvement is theoretically analyzed by the L1 transition errors and off-support leakage across learning rounds. 

• Domain-knowledge-enriched refined Thompson learning variants designed for differ-ent data center conditions: Given by sparse observations, we proposed data-efficient refined Thompson sampling variants and their combinations for arm selection, including gated priors and offline posterior updates, which rely on the domain knowledge of data center scheduling. 

• Reliability mechanisms that keep the learned scheduler auditable under drift and scarce observations: We add a change alarm that flags departures from the trained regime and a confidence-radius rule limiting trust in sparsely observed states, with the guarantees for each detailed in the supplementary material. We demonstrate our RACER+ framework in the following sections: The Methodology section presents the main methodology, which includes VM rescheduling process as a MDP in the CRMAB framework, core strategies for arm selection, and refined algorithmic variants. Case Study section illus-trates the experiment settings for baseline, stress tests, and real-world simulation, along with the VM dataset sources and procedures. The paper concludes with a discussion of limitations, future research directions and ethical considerations, which highlights broad social impacts on the development and dissemination of open-source datasets for AI/ML workloads.

3.1 Model formulation 

We formulate the scheduling problem as a discrete-time MDP. Given a set of VMs V = {v1, v 2, ..., v m}

with related job-specific information, these VM jobs are deployed on N data centers. We could model (re)scheduling of each data center as MDP, defined by a tuple of components ( S, A, P, R, β ), where 3β ∈ (0 , 1] is the discount factor. The grid operator could take the action a ∈ A to request services from each data center. At time t ∈ T , without the grid request, each data center executes a batch of Nj VM task in the cyclic job queue. If the grid requests the service, the data center responses by looking ahead the next Nf ≥ Nj jobs and selecting jobs with the least power consumptions. Any negative entries in the approximation are clipped to zero, and each row is subsequently re-normalized. 4.2 Data processing 

We use Microsoft Azure VM dataset reported in [Cortez et al., 2017] and the calibrated MIT Super-Cloud VM dataset [The MIT Supercloud, 2021] as representative datasets for the three experiments described above. The Microsoft Azure VM dataset contains millions of lightweight workloads, primarily corresponding to Microsoft internal services and testing jobs. However, it does not include information on the underlying physical infrastructure or power consumption. We therefore use the utilization and hardware assumption to estimate power consumptions. In contrast, MIT SuperCloud dataset consists of job traces from large-scale vision, natural language model, and ML workloads, and provides detailed information on memory allocation, hardware utilization, and the underlying physical infrastructure. The final sample contains 22 variants from 5 model families. After calibration with ML Commons Benchmark [Tschand et al., 2025], this reflects the real-world scenarios of data centers. The detailed data processing steps is presented in Supplementary Materials 4 Data processing. Using these two VM datasets, we collected or estimated the following local contextual information that can be accessed for each arm at each job batch: (a) average power consumption, (b) average normalized core-hour, (c) fraction of delay-sensitive jobs, and (d) normalized state scaling between 0 and 1, which shows the position in the job queue. 5.3 Real-world simulations 

We further evaluate refinements in a more complex state space, where the state is not solely defined by a cyclic queue structure and the state space across data centers are not synchronized due to AI/ML tasks, such as batch-flexible training and interactive-heavy inferences. Each arm represents a data center whose state denotes as s = ( q, o ), where q is the state of job queue, and o is the operation type which consists of interactive-heavy and batch-flexible jobs. During re-ordering for demand responses, interactive-heavy states like inference mostly stay local, while batch-heavy states can move free across the temporal window. To simulate real-world scenarios, we conduct the simulation based on the 8 data centers in Texas, where electricity prices are taken locational in the late afternoon (i.e., peak prices). Considering the complexity of state space, we construct the offline prior using operational practices with the success chance of reordering and the look-ahead window as in the supplementary real-world simulation appendix.

Incorporating the 11 gated prior improves performance by 14.9 percentage points to 56.8% (average reward $7.86/kWh), while further introducing domain-knowledge support yields the largest single improvement, increasing performance to 78.5% of the oracle (average reward $10.86/kWh). Low-rank smoothing helps where transition rows are observation-poor, and selecting its rank from the spectrum rather than a fixed cap roughly doubles that benefit. Under the spectral rule, the smallest rank holding 95% of the singular-value mass, a pre-registered sweep over sixteen ( |S| , ρ ) cells with ten paired replications each gains +2 .13 points of oracle for |S| ≥ 20 (95% CI [+1 .55 , +2 .72], paired t p < 10 −5, n = 120), positive in all twelve such cells (sign test p = .0005); means are +1 .46, +2 .56, +2 .38 at |S| = 20 , 50 , 100. The fixed cap was the limitation: it degenerates to rank one at |S| = 8. The appendix locates the headroom in advance — the |S| = 180 kernels certify rank ≥ 37, so no small cap can help there. 

# 6 Conclusions and Ethical Considerations 

This paper proposes RACER+, a CRMAB framework for coordinating power-efficient demand flexi-bility services through VM scheduling and rescheduling. The main contributions lie in both practical applications and methodological advances. On the application side, RACER+ is evaluated on a diverse collection of real-world AI/ML workloads, including language model and computer vision training and inference tasks. Across these settings, the framework consistently achieves strong performance under realistic operating conditions. On the methodological side, RACER+ incorporates domain knowledge, such as job queue dynamics, into Bayesian prior construction to accelerate learning. It further intro-duces adaptive mixed strategies and low-rank state-space refinement to address sparse observations and improve robustness in high-dimensional, noisy environments. Nevertheless, effective deployment requires dataset-specific calibration and careful tuning of refinement hyperparameters for different data center environments. Deployment relies on contextual measurements of hardware-level energy efficiency and of system-software effects on AI workloads. Initiatives such as MLCommons have standardized such benchmarks; we hope RACER+ further encourages open, reproducible AI/ML workload datasets for research on data-center demand flexibility.
