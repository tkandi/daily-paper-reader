Abstract 

Federated continual learning trains a model on a sequence of tasks across several clients with-out sharing their data while retaining knowl-edge of earlier tasks. Sparse mixture-of-experts models support this setting by adapting se-lected parameters on a frozen backbone. How-ever, clients learn different expert content as tasks evolve, making it difficult to share knowledge across clients without disrupting task-specific and client-specific specialization. Matching tile coordinates alone does not estab-lish whether their learned content is compatible. We present FedSEAM, a federated continual learning framework that organizes task knowl-edge into sparse experts and selectively shares their content across tasks and clients. On each client, task-aware tile selection allocates ca-pacity to a new task, while a subspace-based compatibility test determines which existing expert tiles to reuse and where to introduce fresh capacity. On the server, Subspace Expert 

Agreement Merging (SEAM) compares client updates as subspaces and moves each client to-wards consensus only along agreed-upon direc-tions, leaving the rest of its update unchanged. We identify two remaining sources of forget-ting, routing drift and merge erasure, and ad-dress them with three protections, one of which exactly preserves tile components in a selected prior-overlap subspace at every merge. We eval-uate FedSEAM on TRACE using Qwen3-4B and Llama-3.2-3B, with component-wise abla-tions and studies of data size to assess its bal-ance between learning new tasks and retaining prior knowledge. 

1 Introduction 

Federated learning (FL) trains a shared model across clients that keep their raw data private and send only model updates (McMahan et al., 2017), which suits sensitive or widely distributed data. For large language models, FL has become a practical way to fine-tune a common model on data that never leaves its owners (Zhang et al., 2024). Lan-guage models are increasingly adapted after de-ployment by organizations that cannot share data, including hospitals, banks, and research laborato-ries. As their data and requirements evolve, these organizations must acquire new capabilities while retaining earlier knowledge, reflecting the stability– plasticity dilemma of continual learning (Kirk-patrick et al., 2017; Lopez-Paz and Ranzato, 2017). Federated continual learning (FCL) (Yoon et al., 2021) studies this combined setting, which is more challenging than either federated or continual learn-ing alone. Client updates are computed on different distributions and often disagree in direction, and when they are combined coordinate by coordinate, they can cancel or overwrite task-specific informa-tion. Each party also sees only its own data, so clients collaborate by exchanging parameter up-dates. Most federated continual learning methods have been developed and evaluated on vision tasks. Some decompose model parameters to transfer task knowledge between clients (Yoon et al., 2021), an idea later applied to text classification (Chaudhary et al., 2022), while others counter forgetting with generated replay or distillation (Wuerkaixi et al., 2024; Ma et al., 2022). Recent work turns to mul-timodal models, either combining regularization with replay (Liu et al., 2026) or keeping knowledge in separate modules that are selected or fused for each input (Guo et al., 2025, 2026), an idea shared with mixtures of experts, which combine modules through a router (Shazeer et al., 2017; Dou et al., 2024). Routing alone, however, does not prevent interference between tasks (Wu et al., 2026), and these methods leave open how clients’ versions of the same expert should be combined when task-specific knowledge is represented by sparse updates to a language model. A recent design for continual learning of lan-guage models adapts a frozen backbone through 1arXiv:2610.33496v1 [cs.LG] 24 Oct 2026 sparse experts (Siddika et al., 2026): each task trains a small set of weight tiles, and a router com-bines the experts without task labels. Extending this design to the federated setting raises three challenges. First, clients trained on different data drive shared parameters in different directions, and coordinate-wise aggregation methods such as fed-erated averaging or task arithmetic (Ilharco et al., 2023) dilute client-specific knowledge. Second, successive tasks tend to select the same tiles, be-cause the same input channels dominate gradient magnitudes across tasks, and sharing tiles based on position alone cannot distinguish compatible tasks from conflicting ones. Third, freezing experts from completed tasks is insufficient to prevent forget-ting, since the router and shared experts continue to change. To address these challenges, we propose 

Fed erated Subspace Expert Agreement Merging (FedSEAM), a sparse mixture-of-experts frame-work for federated continual learning. On each client, task-aware tile selection and selective ex-pert reuse allow new tasks to build on compatible knowledge while allocating fresh capacity where needed. The server then aggregates shared expert tiles through subspace agreement, combining com-patible client contributions while retaining compo-nents outside the agreed subspace. Figure 1 pre-views the main result on Qwen3-4B with 1,000 samples per client: FedSEAM achieves learning accuracy comparable to FedAvg over tiles, with higher final performance and less forgetting in the available evaluations. Two research questions guide the paper. 

RQ1: How can sparse expert content be shared across tasks and aggregated across clients while retaining task and client specialization? 

RQ2: What causes forgetting when earlier experts are frozen, and how can expert training and ag-gregation limit it? Our main contributions are as follows:

2 Related Work 

Federated Continual Learning. FCL seeks to re-tain knowledge across tasks while transferring rel-evant information between heterogeneous clients. FedWeIT (Yoon et al., 2021) separates global and sparse task-specific parameters and learns weighted inter-client transfer. FedSeIT (Chaudhary et al., 2022) extends this approach to text classification, using domain overlap to select relevant historical tasks. Other methods retain knowledge through generative replay (Wuerkaixi et al., 2024) or dis-tillation with unlabeled surrogate data (Ma et al., 2022). FedSEAM builds on selective parameter sharing, using expert weights and task-start snap-shots without replay or auxiliary distillation data. 

Continual Adaptation of Language Models. 

O-LoRA (Wang et al., 2023a) limits interference through orthogonal low-rank task subspaces, while TreeLoRA (Qian et al., 2025) organizes layer-wise adapters by gradient similarity. LoRAMoE (Dou et al., 2024) uses routed low-rank experts to miti-gate world-knowledge forgetting during instruction tuning. In federated settings, DISCO (Guo et al., 2025) maintains a server-side cache of task sub-spaces and selectively activates them at inference. Fed-Duet (Guo et al., 2026) combines shared se-mantic prompts with personalized adapters. The closest approach to our client-side learner is SETA 2(Siddika et al., 2026), which selects sparse expert blocks by gradient magnitude and shares blocks across tasks based on positional overlap in a cen-tralized setting. CRAFT (Hossen et al., 2026) in-stead adapts hidden representations through low-rank interventions with a forgetting-aware objec-tive. These methods guide the organization and reuse of task knowledge. FedSEAM additionally determines which directions within shared tiles re-ceive a consensus correction and which remain client-specific.

4 Methodology 

FedSEAM runs the same procedure for every task (Figure 2; Algorithm 1). 

4.1 Warm-up and Task-aware Selection

and column q of the tile grid by the mean absolute value of its accumulated gradient, mk(ℓ, r, q ), as in SETA (Siddika et al., 2026). The score map is then processed in three steps that use only local data and task history.

6 Experimental Setup 

Data and backbones. We evaluate on TRACE (Wang et al., 2023b), training its eight tasks in the order C-STANCE (Zhao et al., 2023), FOMC (Shah et al., 2023), MeetingBank (Hu et al., 2023), Py150, ScienceQA (Lu et al., 2022), NumGLUE-cm (Mishra et al., 2022), NumGLUE-ds and 20Minuten (Rios et al., 2021). Each task has 5,000 training samples in the main setting and 500 in a low-data setting, and we test on the first 500 test samples of each task with greedy decoding. Qwen3-4B (Yang et al., 2025) is the main backbone 6and Llama-3.2-3B-Instruct (Grattafiori et al., 2024) the second. 

Federated protocol. Five clients take part in every round. In the equal split , the training sam-ples of a task are dealt to the clients in turn, so each client holds 1,000 (main) or 100 (low-data) samples. Each task is trained for three communica-tion rounds, with three local epochs per round, and after each merge a client calibrates its router for 50 steps. 

Baselines. Local and FedAvg use the same client-side learner as FedSEAM, including expert splitting and gate-row freezing, and differ only in server aggregation. Local performs no aggregation, whereas FedAvg replaces each tile held by at least two clients with its sample-weighted mean. Cen-tralized trains the same learner on the pooled data. FedSEAM+R is a variant of our server that also averages the part of each update outside the agreed subspace and uses θ=0 .5.

Metrics. We report LA, OP and BWT (Equa-tion (3)). Task performance is measured using accuracy, except for Py150, MeetingBank, and 20Minuten, which use edit similarity, ROUGE-L, and SARI, respectively. We report means and stan-dard deviations across the five client models. Be-cause all methods share shards and test sets, we compare them client by client and claim a differ-ence only if it exceeds the client spread and at least four of the five clients agree on its sign. Where fewer than five clients are evaluated, we average those clients and mark the cell. 

Ablations and sweeps. Ablations and sweeps use the low-data setting. Each ablation trains its own stream with the listed protections switched off, and hyperparameter sweeps use a four-task stream of C-STANCE, FOMC, MeetingBank and Py150. 

Code availability. The implementation of Fed-SEAM is available in APPFL 1 (Ryu et al., 2022; Li et al., 2025), an open-source framework for fed-erated learning. 

7 Results and Analysis 

7.1 Main Results 

Tables 1 and 2 report results with 1,000 samples per client. FedSEAM matches FedAvg in LA on both backbones (within 0.33 points), and no task differs by more than 1.8 points. Table 1 reports client means only; the client-spread criterion of §6 has not yet been applied to these runs.

Table 3 compares the full accuracy matrices on Qwen. FedSEAM is higher in 27 of the 36 cells, including 22 of the 28 that score an earlier task after a later one, and the remaining cells differ by at most 1.8 points. FedSEAM thus retains more while learning each new task as well as FedAvg. 

7.2 Fewer Samples per Client 

With 500 samples per task (100 per client; Table 4), FedSEAM is 0.8–0.9 (Qwen) and 1.4 (Llama) points below FedAvg in LA and OP with nearly identical BWT. NumGLUE-cm accounts for most of this difference: without it, the two methods per-form comparably on Qwen and within 0.6 points on Llama (Table 11). On this task, clientson Llama-3.2-3B agree on only 22–38% of their updates after five earlier tasks, so FedSEAM merges conserva-tively (Appendix 14.5); with 1,000 samples per client, the difference falls to 1.8 points or less. Fed-SEAM+R, which also averages the update outside the agreed subspace, achieves higher LA than Fed-SEAM on all five clients and matches FedAvg’s LA without NumGLUE-cm.

8 Conclusion 

FedSEAM supports federated continual learning through a mixture of sparse experts on a frozen language model. Task-aware tile selection and selective expert reuse organize knowledge across successive tasks, while SEAM aggregates expert content across clients along agreed update direc-8tions and retains client-specific components outside them. Three protections target routing drift and merge erasure, including exact preservation of tile components in a selected prior-overlap subspace during each merge; in our ablation, merge protec-tion mainly improved learning rather than retention. Results on TRACE show comparable learning accu-racy to FedAvg in the main setting, with available evaluations indicating better retention. With fewer samples per client, FedAvg’s broader averaging gives slightly higher learning accuracy. 

Limitations 

Clients share model updates, which can leak in-formation about their data, so combining Fed-SEAM with secure aggregation or differential pri-vacy (Abadi et al., 2016) remains future work. We would also like to explore domain adaptation in fed-erated continual learning, where the clients’ data shift from one domain to another over time. Our clients hold near-identically distributed shards, and evaluating FedSEAM with non-IID clients is left to future work.
