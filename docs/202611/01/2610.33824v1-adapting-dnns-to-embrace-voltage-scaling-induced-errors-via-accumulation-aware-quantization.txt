Abstract
The pervasive deployment of Deep Neural Networks (DNNs) across
the computing spectrum has brought growing attention to energy
efficiency, alongside raw performance. While voltage scaling is a
potent knob for power reduction, its application in next-generation
nodes is hampered by severe throughput degradation and sus-
ceptibility to process variation. This work aims to address these
energy-reliability trade-offs by introducing a hardware-software co-
design methodology, based on the observation that timing-robust
bit ranges within the accumulator of a systolic array vary signifi-
cantly across different voltage-frequency operating points. At the
software level, we propose an Accumulation-Aware Training ap-
proach that incorporates this information as a first-order optimiza-
tion constraint during training. By integrating mixed-precision
quantization with unstructured pruning, we effectively constrain
the model’s weight distribution to avoid activating the hardware’s
sensitized high-rank carry chains. At the architectural level, we
complement this with selective MSB-masking and fine-grained
clock-gating to minimize unnecessary dynamic switching activity.
Furthermore, we employ a layer-wise Dynamic Frequency Scaling
strategy to further adapt to the accumulator’s reduced dynamic
range, based on the numerical sensitivity of each layer. We exper-
imentally evaluate the proposed solution on a deep scaled 2nm
technology node and demonstrate that, when operating at a low-
voltage frontier, our co-optimized post-layout architecture delivers
substantial energy and energy-delay product gains of 39% and 23%
respectively, relative to the nominal voltage implementation, while
maintaining accuracy degradation within 1%.

Introduction
The widespread adoption of AI across the entire computing spec-
trum, from hyperscale data centers to resource-constrained edge
devices, has shifted the design paradigm from raw performance
[1, 17] toward energy efficiency [24, 31]. Traditionally, DNNs have
been adapted to support reduced-precision arithmetic formats with-
out significantly compromising inference accuracy or output quality
[5, 34]. Quantization offers substantial architectural advantages by
effectively reducing the memory footprint and alleviating traffic
on the global interconnect, while leveraging the increased compu-
tational density of smaller arithmetic units [9, 20]. However, the
efficacy of quantization can sometimes be bounded, particularly
when adopting aggressive sub-byte precisions that can lead to se-
vere accuracy degradation [21]. Consequently, several works have
explored hardware-software co-design solutions, developing flex-
ible hardware components tailored for low-bitwidth operations
alongside software frameworks to maintain model robustness [26].
Meanwhile, voltage scaling has emerged as one of the most ef-
fective design techniques for improving the energy-efficiency of
DNN accelerators; specifically, near-threshold voltage (NTV) repre-
sents an optimal operative regime from an energy standpoint [12].
While a reduction in supply voltage significantly reduces power
consumption, it also introduces the risk of erroneous outputs due
to critical paths failing to meet timing requirements [10]. Although
DNN algorithms exhibit an inherent tolerance to traditional approx-
imation techniques [3], the propagation of timing errors results
in a catastrophic accuracy degradation, particularly in architec-
tures featuring massive parallelism, such as large-scale MAC arrays
[33]. Consequently, strict operating-frequency management is a
prerequisite for reliable inference [32]. This constraint implies that
aggressive voltage scaling is inherently coupled with diminished
throughput. Finally, under aggressive voltage scaling operation,
circuits showcase increased sensitivity to manufacturing variations
that can result in high deviations in drive current and propagation
delay, further exacerbating the likelihood of timing violations and
complicating the pursuit of a robust, low-power design [14, 18].
Previous research exploring voltage scaling to optimize the en-
ergy efficiency of DNN accelerators has largely failed to capitalize
on the synergistic benefits offered by reduced-precision arithmetic
formats and their corresponding hardware units [2, 29]. Instead,

proaches often overlook the opportunity to co-optimize the hard-

tion. Furthermore, existing methodologies [2, 6, 13, 15, 29, 30] fre-

voltage-induced delays and manufacturing-induced fluctuations,
these designs leave a significant reliability gap, particularly in NTV
conditions where the lack of variation-aware optimization can lead
to unpredictable system failures [11]. Given these limitations, a crit-
ical question arises: can we balance these reliability-energy trade-offs
by proactively manipulating the model’s parameters?
Addressing this question is the primary motivation of this work.
To achieve this, we first conduct a thorough analysis of a systolic ar-
ray architecture at a 2nm technology node to quantify the benefits
and limitations of low-voltage operation. Based on the observations
of this characterization, we leverage the bit-order dependency of
timing failures, which are structurally localized within the high-
order carry chains of the accumulators, to implement a Variation-
Resilient Architecture featuring selective MSB-masking and fine-
grained clock-gating, eliminating dynamic switching power in the
corrupted bit range. Furthermore, our methodology incorporates
Accumulation-Aware Training (AAT) [7], utilizing Mixed-Precision
Quantization and unstructured pruning to mathematically con-
strain the model’s weights, forcing intermediate products to reside
strictly within the hardware’s reliable "safe zone" (i.e., the bit-range
within the accumulators, where timing violations are avoided).
Finally, acknowledging that overly restrictive accumulator con-
straints can trigger accuracy loss [22], we introduce a layer-wise
Dynamic Frequency Scaling (DFS) strategy to adaptively extend the
accumulators’ "safe zone" for sensitive layers without necessitating
a global frequency reduction.
Our main contributions in this work are the following:
(1) We provide a comprehensive evaluation of the performance
and power trade-offs for systolic architectures at the 2nm
GAAFET node, characterizing the process variation effects
that dictate the frequency-scaling limits at both the nominal
and low-voltage frontiers.
(2) We propose a software-hardware co-design methodology
that exploits the localization of timing violations within
the accumulators of the design, even under high process
variation conditions. This architecture employs selective
MSB-masking, fine-grained clock-gating, and DFS to create
an adaptive hardware "safe zone".
(3) We introduce a flexible AAT methodology, enforced with
Mixed-Precision Quantization and unstructured pruning to
guarantee the avoidance of accumulation overflow beyond
the reliable limits of operation.
By treating the hardware’s timing slack as a first-order constraint
during optimization, we demonstrate that both conventional CNN
architectures and Transformer-based models can maintain func-
tional integrity at a low voltage regime of 0.5V, while on average,
improving energy consumption and EDP by 39% and 23% respec-
tively, compared to the nominal (0.7V) baseline implementation.

Related Works & Limitations
A significant body of research has investigated aggressive volt-
age scaling as a primary strategy for enhancing system energy
efficiency. A prominent research direction focuses on voltage over-
scaling, in which supply voltage is reduced within computational
and logic data paths without a proportional decrease in operat-
ing frequency, which would otherwise compromise throughput
[13, 23, 29]. By maintaining high clock frequencies during near-
threshold operation, these designs achieve substantial energy gains
while intentionally operating within the subcritical timing region.
To mitigate the resulting timing violations, systems typically adopt
one of the following paradigms to manage transient hardware faults:
Timing Error Detection & Timing Error Propagation [13]. In the first
approach, systems employ detection mechanisms during runtime to
identify delayed signals. Once a violation is detected, the erroneous
outputs are suppressed, often by masking the results to zero [33],
relying on the network’s stochastic nature to maintain functional
correctness, or the operation is re-computed by dynamically scaling
the voltage to a safer operating region [23]. Alternatively, in the
second approach, selective voltage scaling is applied to specific
hardware structures, targeting either the LSBs of multipliers and
adders [2, 13], or dynamically scaling the supply voltage during
inference on components that compute individual neurons [29, 30].
This second approach ensures that the resulting computational
inaccuracies are confined to elements whose contribution to the
overall inference accuracy is statistically negligible.
Existing studies on aggressive voltage scaling have largely over-
looked the nuanced reliability implications arising from the inter-
action between numerical precision and underlying device physics.
Most prior research [2, 29, 33] treats hardware as a monolithic en-
tity, failing to capitalize on how varying the bit-precision of weights
and activations fundamentally alters the timing-slack and error pro-
files of the MAC logic. Moreover, as architectures scale toward ad-
vanced nodes, localized process variations, particularly fluctuations
in threshold voltage [32], create a non-uniform reliability landscape
across critical design components. Current literature does not ex-
plore how these physical variations sensitize specific logic paths,
such as high-order carry chains, or whether this sensitivity can be

integrate device-level timing guardbanding with variation-aware
training, existing approaches miss critical opportunities to harden
accelerators against the bit-specific glitches inherent in low-voltage
regimes, leaving a significant gap between energy-efficient scaling
and the accuracy requirements of modern AI workloads [11].
To address the aforementioned shortcomings, we propose a cross-
layer co-design methodology that moves beyond generic error mod-
eling by anchoring DNN optimization in the physical timing slack.
By rigorously studying the underlying systolic array architecture
under low-voltage scenarios and the effects of process variation,
we identify a localized failure mechanism within the accumulator’s
high-order bits. To mitigate their effect, we propose Accumulation-
Aware Training, coupled with mixed-precision quantization and
unstructured pruning to mathematically steer the model’s activa-
tions into a deterministic hardware-compliant window. Finally, we
employ a layer-wise DFS scheme to adaptively extend the accumu-
lators’ "safe zone", specifically for layers that showcase a higher
sensitivity to reduced widths.
3
Low-Voltage TPU & the Impact of Process
Variations
Since the primary objective of this work is to optimize the energy
consumption through voltage scaling while mitigating the associ-
ated reliability risks, we first analyze the energy and delay profile
of the targeted micro-architecture: an output-stationary systolic
array, due to its widespread adoption and proven efficacy in both
academic research and industry-standard AI accelerators, such as
the Google TPU [17]. This architecture is renowned for its high
degree of modularity and scalability, and it additionally provides a
regular hardware substrate for characterizing the effects of aggres-
sive voltage scaling, establishing a rigorous Power Performance
Area (PPA) baseline to quantify our optimization gains. While prior
works have explored the reliability-energy trade-offs of voltage scal-
ing within the SRAM buffers [6, 15, 25], these strategies have been
evaluated on more mature nodes (22nm and above) and have yet to
be adapted to the exacerbated variability of sub-5nm regimes [28].
This study pivots from memory-centric mitigation to examine the
impact of low-voltage operation on the computational datapaths of
a systolic array, showcasing its potential and effectiveness.
3.1
Experimental Setup
Our analysis centers on a micro-architecture employing a 32 × 32
systolic MAC array with 8-bit precision for weights and activations
and 24-bit accumulators; a configuration tailored for edge appli-
cations, targeting a clock of 2GHz as our baseline (an overview
is shown in Figure 1). The architecture is implemented using the
N2 PDK provided by IMEC [16]. To establish an industry-standard
performance baseline, arithmetic units are implemented via the
Synopsys DesignWare library. For the physical implementation,
we employed the Cadence back-end suite, utilizing Innovus for
place-and-route (PnR) and Tempus for timing sign-off.
To establish a quantitative foundation for our work, we charac-
terize the power and delay profile of the 2nm systolic array across
four distinct voltage regimes. These include a high-performance

Our timing analysis accounts for manufacturing variability by mod-

more complex systems, such as general-purpose CPU cores, where
the deepest critical paths may be activated only by infrequent in-

structural regularity. As a result, the "worst-case" timing scenario
is not a rare occurrence but the operational norm. This regularity
implies that any process-induced shift that compromises a criti-
cal carry-chain in a MAC unit will manifest as a persistent error
rather than a transient anomaly. Finally, given that the impact of
variability on the threshold voltage (𝑉𝑡ℎ) is particularly dominant

quantifying these deviations, we can map the erosion of setup slack.
3.2
Timing Analysis
We quantify the critical paths of the post-PnR designs by examin-
ing the timing reports in each operating condition. As shown in
Figure 2, our analysis verifies that the critical paths of the design
reside within the combinational logic of the accumulator’s adder
stages. This holds even under a heterogeneous-voltage scheme, in
which the SRAM memory buffers are maintained within a stable
0.7V range to ensure read/write stability (250 psec access latency).
While the memory often dictates the Vmin of the system, the logic-
path delay within the PE array becomes the primary bottleneck for
high-frequency operation as voltage scales, due to the diminished
drive strength of the GAAFET devices at NTV conditions. The deep
carry-chain paths and sign-extension logic within the MAC units
not only exhibit the highest sensitivity to voltage-induced slack
erosion, but more importantly, their long logic paths are more sen-
sitive to threshold voltage fluctuations, causing cumulative timing
delays that disproportionately affect the high-order bits. In contrast,

3.3
Power & EDP Analysis
We complement our characterization by explicitly capturing the
complex physical interactions of the full 2nm systolic array, ac-
counting for the capacitive overhead of the global interconnect
and clock distribution network. As illustrated in the power break-
down (Figure 3), scaling the supply voltage from the nominal 0.7V
sign-off point to the 0.5V near-threshold regime yields a significant
reduction in total power, dropping from 254 mW to approximately
90 mW, resulting in a 61% drop. At the extreme 0.4V frontier, the
power floor further collapses to roughly 20 mW. Conversely, push-
ing the array to 0.9V, to match the peak access latency of the SRAM
buffers, leads to a significant power surge to 532 mW, more than
doubling the dissipation of the nominal case. These substantial
power reductions are not solely a function of VDD scaling; they are
also heavily attributed to the necessary reduction of the clock fre-
quency to avoid timing violations under the𝑉𝑡ℎvariations analyzed
previously. Across the entire voltage range, the synchronization
(Sync) overheads (registers) and the clock tree network remain a
persistent energy bottleneck, contributing roughly 35% of the total
power. We also observe that the relative power contribution of
SRAM accesses becomes more prevalent at lower supply voltages,
as the memory cell arrays must be maintained at a higher VDD
to ensure stability while the surrounding logic scales. Since clock-
ing and logic-switching and the wire-load capacitances dominate
the energy footprint in this technology node [27], voltage scaling
remains a technique capable of simultaneously attenuating both.
Regarding energy efficiency, the benefits of near-threshold oper-
ation are evident: the TOPs/W of the accelerator increase by factors
of 1.5× and 2.0× as the supply voltage is reduced to 0.5V and 0.4V,
respectively. However, this primary motivation must be rigorously
balanced against the associated performance losses, by focusing on
how the EDP metric scales in each operating point. At the high-
performance 0.9V point, despite achieving a peak frequency of 2.8
GHz, the massive power overhead leads to a sub-optimal 1.09×
higher EDP relative to the 0.7V baseline. This suggests that "over-
volting" for raw throughput can yield diminishing returns. This
efficiency gap becomes even more pronounced in the low-voltage
regimes due to the frequency collapse. At 0.5V, the required fre-
quency reduction to 1.0 GHz (under worst-case variation) results
in an EDP 1.45× higher than the 0.7V baseline, while at 0.4V, EDP
skyrockets to 3.5× the nominal, indicating that the performance
penalty associated with NTV can outweigh the energy savings.

Error Propagation Analysis
Bit-Level Error Distributions: We elaborate on the fault modeling
of the timing violations by performing a detailed timing charac-
terization, by following the procedure shown in Figure 4. In our
analysis, the 𝑉𝑡ℎvariability (considering a 3𝜎deviation from the
nominal value as in previous works [14]) and target supply volt-
ages are provided as inputs to the Library Characterization stage,
executed with Cadence Liberate. This ensures that the resulting
.lib files inherently encapsulate the process-variation effects that
will subsequently drive the physical implementation of the MAC
units that these were identified as the primary bottlenecks of our
design in low-voltage regimes (Section 3.2). By performing Gate-
Level Simulations with Siemens’ QuestaSim on the back-annotated
netlists, we map the distribution of the erroneous timing violations
across the accumulators’ bits.
These simulations reveal a distinct correlation between the 𝑉𝑡ℎ
fluctuations and bit-specific error probabilities, particularly in the
high-order carry chains. More specifically, as illustrated in Fig. 5,
when variability in the𝑉𝑡ℎvalue is not taken into account, the error
distribution (flipped bit value) becomes much more localized. In
these scenarios, timing violations are almost exclusively confined
to the 7 MSBs of the 24-bit accumulator (further verifying the ob-
servations from [33]). In contrast, under conditions of higher 𝑉𝑡ℎ
variability (i.e., a worst-case but realistic scenario), the probability
of timing errors is elevated even across lower-order bits, with vio-
lations beginning to manifest as early as the 12th bit position. This
broader error profile occurs because threshold fluctuations slow
down the initial carry-generation stages, causing a "ripple effect" of
delayed transitions that propagate through the entire accumulation
path and fail to settle within the clock cycle.

not the case, as INT4 and INT8 parameters demonstrate a nearly

from the physical nature of timing failures, as they typically mani-

all bits, including the higher-order sign-extension ones, may mo-
mentarily take a value of "1" or "0" as the logic waves propagate
through the carry chains. Because these glitches are a byproduct
of the total switching activity within the 24-bit accumulator, the
reduced bit-width of an INT4 operand does not provide a safety
margin, and the internal carries and accumulation logic still trigger
the same unstable switching behavior in the MSBs. Nevertheless,
low bit-width parameters can be utilized as a means of algorithmic
regulation rather than physical timing mitigation. By aggressively
reducing the precision of weights and activations, we can effectively
constrain the numerical magnitude of the accumulated results. This
ensures that the final values remain within a "safe zone" of the
accumulator, allowing us to extract the correct result by masking
the corrupted high-order bits that fall outside this intended range.
End-to-End DNN Evaluation: Low-voltage operation introduces
a distinct severe error modality: high-magnitude, data-dependent
transients localized in the accumulation stage. To evaluate this, we
injected such errors, sampled from our gate-level characterization,
into the accumulated results of the intermediate feature maps of
a ResNet-50 model. Even if timing violations manifest in only a
single MAC unit (the specific location of which varies from die to
die), the probability of failure, shown in Figure 5, is high enough
to corrupt the generated feature maps, causing Top-1 accuracy to
collapse to 0%. Such catastrophic failures underscore that allowing
unmitigated timing-error propagation in systolic architectures is
prohibitive without hardware-software countermeasures [33].

5.1
MSB-Masking and Selective Clock-Gating
As established in our timing characterization, process variations
at the low-voltage primarily sensitize the sign-extension logic and
high-order carry chains, while the lower bits of the 24-bit accu-
mulator remain robust. We can capitalize on this spatial locality
by introducing a "Safe-Mode" hardware abstraction at the PE level.
This architectural modification consists of programmable masking
logic situated at the accumulator’s output, acting as a hardware-
level ’firewall’ that truncates the sensitized MSBs, since the content
of these high-order bits is considered numerically redundant in our
optimized dataflow. It is important to note that maintaining the
full 24-bit width is essential for architectural flexibility; removing
these bits entirely would impose excessively rigid constraints on
the model’s dynamic range, which is necessary for high-precision
tasks/layers. To further capitalize on this localized sensitivity, we
implement fine-grained selective clock-gating for the accumulator’s
registers and the logic associated with the paths that lead to the
faulty MSBs. Given that the clock distribution network and synchro-
nization overhead account for roughly 35% of the total array power,
gating the clock signal to the sensitized flip-flops provides an extra
opportunity to improve energy efficiency. Physically, this effec-
tively "freezes" the unreliable logic paths, eliminating the dynamic
switching power of the high-order bits that the software is trained
to ignore. The implementation of these minimal mechanisms incurs
a minimal area penalty of 1.7% in the total cell area.
Finally, since extremely narrow accumulator bit-widths can lead
to significant accuracy loss [7, 22], particularly in layers with high
numerical variance that cannot be fully mitigated even after Accu-
mulation Aware Fine-Tuning (discussed in Section 5.2), we propose

5.2.1
Algorithmic Range Control with Mixed-Precision and
Pruning: To align the model’s numerical behavior, we propose a
dual-step optimization strategy that exploits the ability of DNNs

the strict constraints of equation 2. By employing MPQ, specifi-

we utilize unstructured pruning as a fine-grained mechanism for
magnitude control. Unlike structural pruning, the higher sparsity
potential of unstructured methods allows us to aggressively "thin
out" weight tensors in numerically volatile layers.

5.2.3
Exploration Loop. Figure 6 summarizes our optimization
and exploration loop, which treats the EDP as the primary opti-
mization target. The loop begins by extracting the latency of each
layer in the network for the architectures that we have defined in
Section 3 with the use of ZigZag [19]. An initial MPQ (8-bit for both
activations and weights for the first and last layer, while for the
rest we start with 4-bits) configuration is selected, and the network
is trained to satisfy our 1% accuracy degradation threshold. If the
model fails to reach this criterion, the framework identifies layers
with the lowest latency contribution and selectively restores them
to higher bit-widths, recovering predictive signal with minimal
impact on the total system-level EDP. Once the accuracy target
is met, we apply unstructured pruning to the weights to further
reduce their numerical magnitude. Following pruning, we evaluate
each layer against the ℓ1-norm boundary defined in Equation 2. For
layers that satisfy this condition, the weights are frozen to guaran-
tee hardware compatibility. Conversely, for layers that fail to meet
the boundary, we apply our Accumulation-Aware Quantization
methodology to force numerical compliance. If a layer remains a
"bottleneck" even after AAT, the loop triggers a secondary "relief
valve" through layer-wise DFS. Finally, the loop then proceeds by
identifying the layers that currently have the highest relative EDP
contribution and iteratively pushing them toward lower bit-widths
and narrower accumulation widths.
6
Evaluation of our Methodology
To demonstrate the cross-domain efficacy of our methodology, we
evaluate its performance across four distinct benchmarks: ResNet-
18 on CIFAR-102, ResNet-50, and ViT-B/32 for ImageNet classifica-
tion, and the OPT-125M transformer on the WikiText-2 dataset. In
our setup, we define a rigorous optimization constraint where the
total accuracy degradation must remain below 1% Top-1 Accuracy
for the vision tasks and within 1 point of Perplexity for the language
model relative to the 8-bit baseline, and execute 200 refinement
iterations for each model.
6.1
Accumulation-Aware Optimization Results
The impact of our iterative optimization loop on the benchmarks’
numerical and hardware profiles is illustrated in Figure 7. Initially,
the voltage-scaled baseline (INT8, 0.5V @ 1GHz) exhibits high nu-
merical "pressure," with Pmin distributions statistically centered
near 19 bits and a 2.0× latency penalty, resulting in a suboptimal
relative EDP increase of 45% compared to the (INT8, 0.7V @ 2GHz)
solution. By only applying MPQ and unstructured pruning, we
effectively truncate the dynamic range of partial products. A key
observation here is the strong correlation between the two optimiza-
tion knobs: lower-precision operands directly enable pushing the
accumulator width to lower boundaries, even before the application
of AAT. By utilizing this dynamic range control along our modified
architecture, we yield energy gains of up to 32% while limiting the
latency penalty to 47%, a substantial improvement over the 100%
penalty required by a traditional 0.5V "safe-mode" baseline.
The integration of AAT further compresses 𝑃𝑚𝑖𝑛into power-
optimized operating regions, yielding an additional 7% energy gain
beyond the pruning phase. However, the layer-wise breakdown in
Figure 7 (bottom) reveals distinct sensitivities with respect to DNN
architectures. While CNNs adapt readily to reduced bit-widths, the
ViT-B32 and OPT-125M models necessitate higher precision (W6
and W8) across many transformer blocks to maintain accuracy
within 1%. This requirement limits the frequency-scaling head-
room, forcing many layers to operate at 1.4–1.8 GHz with higher
accumulator bit-widths. Consequently, these models exhibit more
moderate EDP reduction (0.80 for ViT and 0.83 for OPT) compared
to the CNN architectures due to the sustained latency overhead.
This distribution underscores the necessity of a heterogeneous 𝑃𝑚𝑖𝑛
strategy. By adaptively assigning precision based on layer sensitiv-
ity, the framework ensures rapid convergence and accuracy while
reclaiming the performance-energy slack lost to process variability,
enabling an average EDP reduction of 23% across all benchmarks.

7
Conclusions
In this work, we presented a variation-aware methodology tar-
geting systolic architectures in advanced technology nodes. By
characterizing the timing-error profiles at low supply voltages, we
identified high-magnitude MSB transients as the primary barrier
to reliable inference. Our proposed hardware-software solution
combines MSB-masking with Accumulation-Aware Quantization
to enforce numerical compliance without sacrificing throughput.
Evaluations across several DNNs demonstrate that our approach
maintains predictive integrity within 1% of the 8-bit baseline while
delivering 39% energy savings over 0.7V nominal operation and a
46% EDP improvement over the "always safe" low-Vdd baseline.
