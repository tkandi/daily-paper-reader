# One-Shot Federated Model Editing for Device Dynamics at the Edge 

Youming Tao, Weijun Yin, Huanyi Xie, Yanqing Yao, Dongxiao Yu, Senior Member, IEEE ,Xiuzhen Cheng, Fellow, IEEE , Di Wang, Member, IEEE , Falko Dressler Fellow, IEEE 

Abstract —Standard federated learning (FL) requires persistent device connectivity across multiple communication rounds, mak-ing it unsuitable for dynamic environments such as vehicular networks, where devices frequently join and leave. One-shot FL offers a promising alternative by completing training in a single communication round. However, one-shot FL suffers from model solidification and cannot readily adapt to subsequent client dynamics, including new client contributions and the with-drawal of previous contributions. We therefore consider exact model editing, which removes withdrawn contributions through unlearning or incorporates newly contributed ones through incremental learning without retraining from scratch. We identify that efficient editing fundamentally hinges on learning stability. To this end, we propose F-SOSA, the first stable one-shot FL algorithm that enables rapid and exact model editing. F-SOSA leverages sub-sampling of local models with carefully tuned rate for stable aggregation and performs curvature-aware model fusion to preserve utility. We prove that F-SOSA achieves vanishing training error and reasonably small generalization error bounds. Building on this stability, we further develop two single-round editing algorithms: F-SOSA-U for unlearning and F-SOSA-I for incremental learning. They ensure that the edited model, along with all intermediate algorithmic states, is statistically indistinguishable from retraining while incurring only an expected O(ρ) fraction of its cost, where ρ quantifies stability. Experiments demonstrate that our approach matches retraining performance while reducing cumulative time and communication overhead by at least 36% and 22%, respectively, under realistic urban mobility simulations. 

Index Terms —Federated Learning, Dynamic Participation, Model Editing, Single-Round Communication

In mobile edge computing (MEC) scenarios such as vehicular networks, client participation is inherently dynamic. Devices frequently join or leave due to mobility or network instability. This challenges standard FL algorithms like FedAvg [1], Fed-Prox [2], and SCAFFOLD [3]. These methods rely on a largely fixed client population and multiple rounds of communication. Such assumptions rarely hold in practice. Communication efficiency is also critical in bandwidth-constrained edge environments [4]–[6]. To reduce commu-nication rounds and reliance on persistent availability, one-shot FL [7] has emerged, which completes training in a single round. Recent studies [8]–[10] show that one-shot FL can achieve competitive performance over non-iid data. This makes it well-suited for dynamic systems. Vehicular networks provide a natural setting for collaborative learning across vehicles [11]. A representative use case is vehicular micro-clouds (VMCs) [12], where nearby vehicles temporarily pool their resources for edge collaboration. Vehicle mobility leads to changes in VMC membership as vehicles join and leave [13], making persistent multi-round participation difficult to maintain. One-shot FL is therefore a natural solution. However, one-shot FL suffers from model solidification. Once trained, the global model cannot adapt to changes in the client population. When clients arrive or depart, the set of participants contributing to the collaborative model changes. A newly arrived client can contribute its local model and benefit from the resulting global model, whereas a departing client may withdraw its previous contribution for privacy before disconnecting from the coordinator. Supporting such contribution additions and withdrawals requires the one-shot model to accommodate incremental learning and unlearning after its initial training. This issue is particularly relevant in dynamic edge applications such as VMCs, where vehicles participate only temporarily and repeated retraining after every arrival or departure would incur substantial communication and computation overhead. Approximate editing methods such as fine-tuning or model calibration can reduce this cost, but successive approximate updates may deviate from the model produced by retraining and propagate this deviation to later edits. Exact editing instead restores the same distribution as re-training after every change, preventing such history-dependent drift. These challenges motivate the following question: 

Can we efficiently edit a one-shot trained FL model to reflect an updated client set without retraining? 

We formalize this goal as exact federated model editing , which arXiv:2610.35212v1 [cs.LG] 26 Oct 2026 2

includes two tasks: (1) client unlearning to remove a client’s previous contribution upon a withdrawal request, and (2) client incremental learning to incorporate the contribution of a newly participating client. By exactness, we require statistical indis-tinguishability between editing and retraining, which applies to both the final model and all intermediate algorithmic states. To achieve this goal, we propose the first exact model editing framework for one-shot FL. The framework consists of a stable one-shot FL algorithm, F-SOSA, and two editing algorithms: F-SOSA-U for client unlearning and F-SOSA-I for client incremental learning. Our main contributions are summarized as follows: 1) We formalize the definition of exact federated model edit-ing through statistical indistinguishability between editing and retraining. Specifically, we require that the joint dis-tributions of the obtained model and all intermediate algo-rithmic states produced by editing match those generated by retraining on the updated client set. 2) We design F-SOSA as an edit-friendly one-shot FL al-gorithm that combines two complementary mechanisms. First, we reformulate curvature-aware model fusion as a projection characterized by client-additive sufficient statis-tics, so that a single-client change can be incorporated by updating only the corresponding contribution rather than rerunning the full fusion procedure. Second, we introduce randomized client subsampling to make the one-shot output stable in total variation, thereby allowing many member-ship changes to reuse the existing model and states without recomputation. 3) We provide theoretical utility guarantees for F-SOSA. Specifically, we establish upper bounds on both training and generalization errors for over-parameterized neural networks. The training error vanishes as network width and local training increase. The generalization error converges to a constant governed by data non-degeneracy and be-comes favorably small as the numbers of data samples and clients grow. 4) Building upon F-SOSA, we develop F-SOSA-U and F-SOSA-I for client unlearning and incremental learning. Thanks to F-SOSA’s total variation stability quantified by 

ρ < 1, not all editing requests need execution. We design a lightweight verification to determine the necessity. As a result, only an expected O(ρ)-fraction of requests trigger the editing process. 5) We conduct extensive experiments on standard benchmarks to evaluate model utility, editing exactness, and efficiency. F-SOSA achieves strong accuracy compared with state-of-the-art one-shot FL methods. F-SOSA-U/I match re-training performance while significantly reducing time and communication costs. We verify the exactness of editing using membership inference attacks. We also present a case study with simulated vehicular scenarios to demonstrate the practical applicability of our proposed approach. II. R ELATED WORK 

We review three related research directions in FL: unlearn-ing, continual learning, and dynamic client participation. 

Federated unlearning. Federated unlearning was first stud-ied in [14], which removes a client approximately by re-versing its updates. While faster than retraining, it remains computationally expensive. Subsequent works improve effi-ciency through noise perturbation [15]–[17], knowledge dis-tillation [18], class-discriminative pruning [19], or gradient ascent [20]. However, all these methods provide only approx-imate removal. As a result, the model remains vulnerable to membership inference and gradient leakage [21]–[27]. Recent studies explore exact unlearning. Liu et al. [28] propose a Newton-style retraining method that uses Fisher information for curvature approximation. However, their retraining path deviates from the original trajectory and relies on convexity assumptions. Tao et al. [29] introduce a stabilized FedAvg variant called FATS for exact unlearning via resampling and rapid retraining. Their one-shot variant, however, suffers from degraded utility due to simple averaging and does not support client addition. 

Federated continual learning. Federated continual learning assumes a fixed set of clients whose local data evolve over time. Existing work considers different settings including task-incremental [30]–[33], class-incremental [34]–[36], and domain-incremental [37]–[39], which correspond to sequen-tial tasks, new classes, and domain shifts, respectively. The primary goal is to mitigate catastrophic forgetting and preserve past knowledge. As they prioritize knowledge retention over exact alignment with new data, these methods cannot guaran-tee statistical equivalence to retraining. In addition, they rely on multi-round training and continued access to past clients. In contrast, we study dynamic client populations where new clients may join after others have left. 

Federated Learning with dynamic participation. Recent studies consider federated learning with dynamic participants. Ruan et al. [40] and Chang et al. [41] allow clients to join or leave during training and provide convergence guarantees. Their setting differs fundamentally from ours. They study multi-round federated training where client changes occur across rounds and the goal is to adapt during training. In contrast, we focus on one-shot FL, which involves only a single round. In this setting, client dynamics arise after training rather than during it. Our goal is therefore to enable exact model editing of the trained model to accommodate changes in the client population. 

Summary. Existing studies address related but distinct chal-lenges. Federated unlearning focuses on removing clients but typically provides only approximate guarantees or suffers from utility degradation in one-shot settings. Federated continual learning assumes a fixed client set and prioritizes knowledge retention rather than exact alignment with updated data. Work on dynamic participation adapts to client churn during multi-round training and does not apply to post-training model editing in one-shot FL. Consequently, none of these direc-tions directly address our setting, where client additions and withdrawals occur after one-shot training. This challenge is amplified under repeated edits, since any residual deviation may propagate to subsequent updates. The goal is therefore not merely post-training adaptation, but efficient and exact editing that remains valid across successive client-set changes. 3

A. System Model 

We consider an edge system consisting of a trusted infrastructure-based coordinator and a dynamic set of devices. The coordinator can, for example, be an RSU or MEC server in a vehicular micro-cloud (VMC), with vehicles serving as participating devices. Time proceeds in discrete rounds. Devices may join or leave at each round. We do not impose a particular distribution on client arrivals or departures; their occurrence may be non-uniform across devices and time. Let 

M(t) denote the set of active devices at round t, and let 

M (t) = |M (t)|. The coordinator maintains knowledge of the active device set. When a new device joins, it establishes a connection with the coordinator and may submit its local model for inclusion in the collaborative model. Before an active device departs and loses connectivity to the coordinator, it may issue an unlearning request to withdraw its previous contribution. Standard authenticated communication between devices and the coordinator is assumed.

C. Federated Model Editing 

To support dynamic client participation, our goal is to enable the global model to adapt efficiently to changes in the client set. Specifically, we aim to (i) remove the influence of departed clients that withdraw their previous contributions (client unlearning), and (ii) incorporate newly arrived clients (client incremental learning), while preserving model utility and avoiding full retraining. Let M denote the space of devices, W the space of model parameters, and S the space of internal algorithmic states, which will be specified later. A one-shot FL algorithm is a randomized map A : M∗ → W × S . Given a set of devices, it produces a global model together with internal algorithmic states. A model editing algorithm is a map U : W ×S × M∗ →W × S . It takes the current model, the associated states, and a set of client addition or removal requests, and outputs an updated model and states. Examples of internal states include sketches of device information or intermediate computations that can be reused during editing. 

Definition 1 (Exact Federated Model Editing) . Let M and 

M′ denote the device sets before and after a change. Given a one-shot FL algorithm A, a model editing algorithm U satisfies exact model editing if for any measurable event E ⊆ W × S ,

PA(M′) ∈ E  = PU(A(M), M ⊕ M ′) ∈ E ,

where M ⊕ M ′ denotes the symmetric difference, i.e., M ⊕ M′ = ( M \ M ′) ∪ (M′ \ M ).This definition requires that both the output model and all internal algorithmic states after editing be statistically indistinguishable from those obtained by retraining on the updated device set. Requiring exactness for the internal states is important for repeated editing: these states are reused by subsequent editing operations, and any approximation retained in them may be propagated to later model updates. If one edit produces exactly the same joint distribution as retraining, the resulting state can be treated as a fresh execution of the learning algorithm on the updated client set. The guarantee thus extends to an arbitrary sequence of edits by induction, without accumulating approximation error across requests.

A. TV-Stable One-Shot Federated Learning: F-SOSA 

The federated learning admits a posterior-inference interpre-tation. Specifically, the learning objective in (1) corresponds to finding the mode of a global posterior distribution over model parameters. This viewpoint has recently been used to derive one-shot FL algorithms that aggregate local posterior information in a single communication round [8]. We adopt this probabilistic formulation as a technical starting point, but our design goal is fundamentally different. Existing posterior-based one-shot FL methods primarily optimize a single fusion operation. In contrast, our setting requires the fused model to support repeated post-training edits as the client set changes. This creates two additional requirements. First, the fusion state should admit efficient single-client addition and removal without rerunning the entire fusion procedure. Second, the out-put should remain stable under a single-client change so that previously computed results can be reused whenever possible. We address these requirements in two stages: we first derive a curvature-aware projection characterized by client-additive statistics, and then introduce randomized client subsampling to control total-variation stability. B. Efficient Editing Algorithms: F-SOSA-U and F-SOSA-I 

For a fixed subset size P , F-SOSA aggregates a uni-formly sampled client subset P. When the client population changes, the support of the uniform size-P subset distribution changes accordingly. Exact model editing therefore reduces to constructing a coupling between these sampling distributions while retaining P . For a current population of M clients, the deletion coupling retains the subset when the departing client is absent and otherwise replaces that client. The insertion coupling includes the new client with probability P/ (M + 1) 

by replacing a uniformly selected member; otherwise, it retains the subset. These transitions are maximal couplings of the respective subset distributions, with trigger probabilities P/M 

and P/ (M + 1) . Thus, the trigger probabilities depend on the current population even though P remains fixed. Whenever the subset is retained, its contributions, aggregate statistics, and fused model can be reused. We develop the corresponding procedures below: F-SOSA-U for client unlearning (Algo-rithm 2) and F-SOSA-I for client incremental learning (Al-gorithm 3). Client incremental learning (F-SOSA-I). When a new client i+ joins, we determine whether it would have been selected under the updated F-SOSA sampling distribution. Conditional on the current client set in the learning objective containing M + 1 clients, F-SOSA samples a size-P subset uniformly at random. Hence, each client in the updated set, including the newly arrived client i+, has inclusion probability Pr( i+ ∈ P ′) = P/ (M + 1) . This probability is determined by the conditional subset-sampling rule and is independent of the external arrival distribution. If the Bernoulli trial fails, the sampled subset remains unchanged under the coupling. Otherwise, the coordinator replaces a uniformly chosen client i− ∈ P with i+ and updates the sufficient statistics using the same adjustments as in unlearning. Both editing algorithms modify only a single selected-client contribution while reusing the current aggregate fusion statis-tics. Consequently, each triggered editing operation updates only one client contribution to the sufficient statistics and per-forms one projection solve. Its coordinator-side cost depends on the chosen Fisher representation, as discussed above, but does not require recomputing the contributions of all previ-ously sampled clients. Moreover, since editing is triggered with probability at most O(ρ), the expected editing cost per client change is O(ρ) relative to full retraining. Together, these procedures enable provably exact and communication-efficient model adaptation to dynamic client participation in one-shot federated learning. We empirically evaluate our framework with focus on: (1) one-shot learning performance, (2) model editing accuracy and efficiency, (3) trade-offs controlled by the stability level ρ, and (4) resilience to membership inference attacks. We also include a realistic case study based on vehicle mobility simulation. 

A. Experimental Setup 1) Datasets and Models: We use MNIST [47], SVHN [48], CIFAR-10 [49], and FEMNIST from LEAF [50]. For MNIST/SVHN/CIFAR-10, we simulate client heterogeneity using Dirichlet partitioning (α ∈ {0.1, 0.5, 0.8}; default 

α=0 .5). FEMNIST, as a FL dataset, naturally exhibits non-IID characteristics; we follow [9], sampling 10% of users and grouping into M clients. Unless otherwise specified, we use the two-layer CNN from [8] with K-FAC Fisher approximation. To evaluate F-SOSA beyond the default classi-fication setting, we additionally consider ResNet18 on CIFAR-10 and YOLOv8s on the real-world driving dataset BDD100K; detailed results are reported in the Appendix. 

2) Baselines: We compare F-SOSA with three one-shot FL baselines: FedFisher [8], which uses client-specific Fisher information for model fusion; FedLPA [9], which constructs local posteriors via layer-wise Laplace approximation; and Dense [10], which performs data-free distillation using GAN-generated samples. For dynamic-client experiments, we use a fixed-ratio implementation of F-SOSA-I/U to accommodate population changes that may extend beyond the bounded range assumed in the theoretical analysis. Specifically, we set Pt = ⌊ρM (t)⌋, where M (t) is the current active client population. This keeps the participation fraction approximately constant and adapts the aggregation size to population changes. For both unlearning and incremental learning, Retrain reruns F-SOSA on the updated client set using the same current subset size Pt. For unlearning, we additionally compare with FedEraser [14], which reconstructs the model using calibrated gradient updates; FedRR [28], which uses diagonal Fisher information with momentum correction; and FATS-CU [29], which stabilizes FedAvg for rapid unlearning. For incremental learning, we also compare with Fine-Tuning, which adapts the global model on the new client’s data using a tuned number of epochs selected from {5, 10 , . . . , 50 }. All methods are evaluated under the one-shot constraint for fair comparison. 

3) Hyperparameters: We follow the settings in [8], using learning rate η = 0.01 and default local epoch number 

K = 50 . For learning experiments, we set M = 20 clients; for model editing, we use at most 100 clients and initialize with 50 active clients, while the scalability experiments in the Appendix consider client populations from 20 to 500 . The stability level is set to ρ = 0 .5 unless otherwise specified. 

4) Evaluation Metrics: We report test accuracy, training loss, total wall-clock time, and total communication bytes, including global-model broadcasts, local-model uploads, and Fisher-information uploads. Time and communication effi-ciency measure the average accuracy gain per unit of time and communication, respectively.

C. Case Study Using Simulation of Urban Mobility (SUMO) 

We further evaluate F-SOSA-I/U using the Simulation of Urban MObility (SUMO) [51], focusing on a selected city center region in the Luxembourg scenario [52] (see Figure 5). Editing requests are triggered by vehicle movements during the 08:00 morning rush hour, simulating dynamic client join/leave events. For this case study, we map each vehicle arrival to a new client contribution and each vehicle departure to an unlearning request issued before disconnection, thereby generating a mobility-driven editing sequence. We apply F-SOSA-I/U and Retrain to the resulting request sequence across four datasets. Figure 6 reports the test accuracy after each request and the cumulative time/communication costs. F-SOSA-I/U match the accuracy of Retrain while maintaining smaller fluctuations. On SVHN, for instance, Retrain ex-hibits swings over 5%, whereas F-SOSA-I/U keep variations within 3.96% across all datasets. This robustness stems from client subsampling and Bernoulli sampling, which avoid un-necessary updates when edits do not affect the aggregation set. In terms of efficiency, F-SOSA-I/U reduce cumulative time and communication overhead by 36 .66% –53 .84% and 

21 .97% –25 .76% , respectively, where the reported communi-cation cost includes all model transfers and Fisher-information uploads. In summary, F-SOSA-I/U deliver model editing with near-optimal accuracy and significantly improved efficiency, showing their practical benefits in dynamic environments. We further replay the same mobility-driven editing sequence on a YOLOv8s object-detection model trained on BDD100K; the corresponding results are reported in the Appendix B-D. VII. C ONCLUSIONS 

We present a principled framework for exact model editing in one-shot FL, enabling both client unlearning and incre-mental learning. At its core is F-SOSA, a stable learning algorithm that supports efficient post-training edits. Together with the editing algorithms F-SOSA-U and F-SOSA-I, our method achieves statistical exactness while preserving model utility and efficiency. We provide theoretical guarantees and comprehensive experiments demonstrating its effectiveness across standard benchmarks and dynamic edge scenarios. REFERENCES [1] B. McMahan, E. Moore, D. A. Additional Experimental Details 1) Dataset and Models: Beyond the default two-layer CNN, we further evaluate F-SOSA using an ImageNet-pretrained ResNet18 on CIFAR-10 and YOLOv8s [53] on BDD100K [54]. BDD100K is a large-scale real-world driving dataset containing 100,000 40-second videos recorded at 720p and 30 FPS across diverse road, weather, and lighting conditions. It supports ten autonomous-driving tasks, with bounding-box annotations for ten traffic-related object categories. For both settings, we simulate client heterogeneity using the same Dirichlet partitioning scheme ( α ∈ { 0.1, 0.5, 0.8}; default α=0 .5). 

2) Hyperparameters: For experiments on CIFAR-10 with ResNet18 and BDD100K with YOLOv8s, we set the local learning rate to η = 0 .001 and the total number of clients to M = 20 . For CIFAR-10, we set the number of local epochs to K = 50 and the batch size to 64 ; for BDD100K, we use K = 20 local epochs, a batch size of 32 , and an input resolution of 640 ×640 . Both settings employ SGD, with momentum 0.9. For BDD100K, we additionally apply weight decay of 5 × 10 −4, a three-epoch warm-up, and cosine learning-rate decay. 

3) Metrics: For the object-detection task on BDD100K, we use mAP 50 -95 as the primary metric to evaluate the detection utility of F-SOSA and the baselines. It is computed by evaluating average precision (AP) at IoU thresholds from 0.50 to 0.95 

in increments of 0.05 and averaging the results across all thresholds and ten object categories. This metric jointly reflects object recognition and bounding-box localization accuracy, with higher values indicating better overall detection performance.

C. Scalability of F-SOSA-U and F-SOSA-I Across Client Populations 

To assess the scalability of F-SOSA-I/U with increasing edge-device density, we consider five total client populations, 

{20 , 50 , 100 , 200 , 500 }. For each population, we execute 15 sequential incremental-learning requests and 15 sequential unlearning requests. Table VI reports the mean accuracy, time-cost savings relative to full retraining, and communication cost normalized to Retrain, which together capture utility preservation and system efficiency. For incremental learning, F-SOSA-I maintains accuracy comparable to Retrain across the evaluated client scales. Even at 

500 clients, the accuracy gap between F-SOSA-I and Retrain remains within 2.46 percentage points across all four datasets. In the 50 -client case, F-SOSA-I achieves the highest accuracy on MNIST, SVHN, and CIFAR-10, reaching 94 .35% , 68 .95% ,and 59 .99% , respectively. As the system scales to 200 clients, it achieves the best performance on FEMNIST and CIFAR-10, with accuracies of 32 .33% and 46 .27% , respectively. For the largest population of 500 clients, the accuracy gap between F-SOSA-I and Retrain remains within 2.46 percentage points across all four datasets, while F-SOSA-I outperforms Fine-tuning by 2.45 –10 .90 percentage points. This utility advantage is particularly pronounced on the inherently heterogeneous FEMNIST dataset: as the client population increases from 100 to 500 , F-SOSA-I maintains an accuracy of approximately 31% , whereas Fine-tuning remains around 20% . In terms of efficiency, the normalized communication cost of F-SOSA-I never exceeds 

0.87 × that of Retrain and decreases to 0.67 × with 500 clients, corresponding to a 33% reduction. Although Fine-tuning incurs marginally lower communication overhead at some scales, these savings come at the cost of substantial accuracy degradation, particularly on MNIST, SVHN, and FEMNIST. F-SOSA-I therefore provides a more favorable utility–efficiency trade-off. Its time-cost savings remain positive across all configurations, ranging from 15 .84% to 49 .04% . Even with a population of 500 

clients, F-SOSA-I saves 23 .26% –49 .04% of the retraining time, indicating that its computational benefits persist as the edge system becomes increasingly dense. Regarding unlearning, F-SOSA-U exhibits similarly strong scalability. Across all evaluated client populations, its accuracy closely tracks or exceeds that of Retrain while consistently outperforming most unlearning baselines. The results for 100 

clients show that F-SOSA-U achieves the highest accuracy on MNIST, SVHN, and CIFAR-10. When the client population reaches 500, F-SOSA-U surpasses Retrain on SVHN, FEMNIST, and CIFAR-10 and remains within 0.89 percentage points of Retrain on MNIST. In this largest-scale regime, F-SOSA-U also outperforms the strongest competing unlearning baseline by 1.24 –4.85 percentage points across the four datasets. Its normalized communication cost remains below that of Retrain throughout the evaluated range. With 500 clients, F-SOSA-U reduces communication by 47% relative to Retrain and FATS-CU and by approximately 60% relative to FedEraser and FedRR, whose communication costs exceed 1.33 × that of Retrain. D. Real-World Driving Case Study with BDD100K and SUMO Mobility 

We further evaluate F-SOSA-I/U on a dynamic federated object-detection task by combining mobility traces generated by the Simulation of Urban MObility (SUMO) with the real-world BDD100K driving dataset. Following the mobility-trace setting described in section VI-C, the trace produces 31 sequential editing requests, comprising 16 client arrivals and 15 departures. We replay this request sequence on BDD100K using YOLOv8s and report the mAP 50 -95 after each request, together with the cumulative computation time and communication cost, in Figure 7. F-SOSA-I/U closely tracks the utility of Retrain throughout the sequence, with a maximum mAP 50 -95 difference of only 0.39 percentage points. Across the 31 editing requests, F-SOSA-I/U matches or outperforms Retrain after 19 requests and achieves a slightly higher average mAP 50 -95 of 20 .25% , compared with 20 .21% for Retrain. After the final request, its mAP 50 -95 reaches 20 .27% , exceeding the 20 .19% achieved by Retrain. F-SOSA-I/U also exhibits greater stability: its mAP 50 -95 varies within a range of 0.35 percentage points, compared with 0.55 

for Retrain, while its maximum request-to-request variation is 0.28 percentage points, versus 0.46 for Retrain. More notably, F-SOSA-I/U provides substantial efficiency gains. As shown in the figure, its communication and time curves remain essentially flat for 8 of the 31 requests. When the client involved in a request is not selected for aggregation, Bernoulli sampling avoids unnecessary computation and communication. These skipped updates save 2.74 GB of communication and 

343 .73 minutes of computation, accounting for 100% and approximately 78 .31% of the overall communication and time savings, respectively. The savings do not arise solely from skipped updates: across the remaining 23 requests that require model updates, F-SOSA-I/U saves an additional 95 .19 minutes. This additional time saving occurs because some requests require F-SOSA-I/U to update only one client with a relatively small local dataset, whereas Retrain must wait for all participating clients to complete local training and is therefore limited by the slowest client. After the complete request sequence, F-SOSA-I/U incurs 8.21 GB of cumulative communication, compared with 10 .94 GB for Retrain, corresponding to a 25 .00% reduction. Its cumulative computation time decreases from 1335 .21 to 896 .28 minutes, yielding a 32 .87% reduction. These absolute savings are particularly significant on BDD100K, since repeatedly retraining YOLOv8s and communicating its model updates over complex real-world driving data is considerably more expensive than editing lightweight classification models. Taken together, F-SOSA-I/U preserves near-retraining detection utility while substantially reducing the cost of frequent recomputation, demonstrating its practical value in dynamic and communication-constrained edge environments. 24

E. Detailed Communication-Cost Breakdown 

To provide a clear breakdown of the communication costs, Table VII reports the per-round costs for selected and unselected clients in F-SOSA with M = 20 and ρ = 0 .5. Under this configuration, ten clients are selected, while the remaining ten are unselected. The server first broadcasts the global model to all clients. Then, only the selected clients upload their local models and approximate Fisher information, represented by either Diag or K-FAC, whereas the unselected clients do not upload any information. Note that although four experiments in the table use two-layer CNNs, their architectures and parameter counts differ, resulting in different model sizes. As shown in the table, the uplink cost of the Diag Fisher information is equal to that of the 16-bit quantized local model for all model architectures. Their combined size is therefore equal to the communication size of one FP32 model. This is because 16-bit quantization is applied to both the local models and the Diag Fisher information in our implementation. After receiving the quantized information, the server reconstructs the values and performs aggregation. More specifically, each local model and its diagonal Fisher information are quantized layer by layer to 16-bit precision before aggregation. They have the same communication cost because both contain one value for each model parameter. For K-FAC, the Fisher factors are further compressed using low-rank decomposition and quantization. Therefore, the K-FAC communication cost depends on the model architecture and the dimensions of its Fisher factors, and may differ slightly from the Diag cost. The table also shows that 25
