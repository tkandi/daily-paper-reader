Current and emerging mobile networks face increasing privacy, scalability, and performance challenges as distributed learning becomes essential for advanced 6G services. These challenges are amplified by the presence of non-IID data, naturally arising in heterogeneous wireless environments, that degrade the performance of standard Federated Learning (FL) approaches. To address non-IID data across clients, we propose a Clustered Federated Learning (CFL) framework that assigns clients to specialized models during training. Data-domain clustering initializes model-based clustering, enabling identification and exploitation of client similarities without sharing raw data while reducing communication cost. Motivated by mmWave beam selection, we evaluate our methods on a wireless simulation dataset (DeepMIMO) and complement it with two non-IID benchmarks (FEMNIST, Rotated CIFAR-10) to isolate different non-IID characteristics. Our best configurations improve accuracy over IFCA by 4 .8 pp on DeepMIMO and 10 .6 pp on FEMNIST while reducing downlink data volume by 83 .1 % on FEMNIST compared to IFCA. We demonstrate that different clustering strategies suit different non-IID characteristics. These results show that our CFL methods enable resource-efficient, and high-performance distributed learning in future 6G networks. 

1 Introduction 

The evolution from 5th to 6th generation mobile networks (6G) mobile networks introduces unprecedented demands on performance, capacity, coverage, and privacy [30]. While 5G has significantly advanced mobile broadband and low-latency communications, many emerging applications—such as holographic communication, extended reality, and the Internet of Senses [17]—will require 6G mobile networks that are secure, trustworthy, and intelligent [30, 50, 54, 56]. Millimeter Wave (mmWave) frequency bands play an important role in meeting these requirements by enabling extremely high data rates through wide bandwidths [18, 43]. However, mmWave communication also introduces new challenges, including directional beam management, link instability, and increased signaling overhead [43]. In particular, selecting the best downlink (DL) beamforming vector (see Section 2.1) requires extensive search over candidate beams, resulting in high communication overhead and raising privacy concerns when data must be shared across base stations. To address privacy concerns in distributed wireless systems, Federated Learning (FL) [10, 47] has emerged as a promising approach that enables multiple clients (e.g., base stations) to collaboratively train a global model without sharing raw data. Despite its advantages, performance of standard FL methods such as Federated Averaging ( FedAvg )degrades significantly in non-IID scenarios [67], which naturally arise in heterogeneous wireless environments due to variations in user spatial distribution, behavior, geography, hardware, and traffic patterns. Such degradation limits model performance and system efficiency. Clustered Federated Learning (CFL) [55] methods such as Iterative Federated Clustering Algorithm (IFCA) [20] have emerged as promising solutions to non-IID challenges. Determining which clients should train together is the hard part. 

In this work, we propose an adaptive CFL framework that assigns clients to cluster models, overcomes limitations on high communication overhead and computational complexity, while also improving accuracy, see Fig. 1. Our framework combines data-domain cluster initialization and model-based clustering to capture heterogeneity across clients. The data-domain cluster initialization provides a cheap initialization to the model-based clustering CFL methods that refine the assignment during FL training. On DeepMIMO, our IFCA-based methods improve accuracy by 4 .8 pp with run-to-run accuracy spread reduced up to seven-fold. 2 Background 2.1 Analog beamforming and beam management 

In 5G and upcoming 6G networks, the use of mmWave frequency bands allows high throughput and low latency but introduces challenges such as increased directionality and sensitivity to blockage. Adding antenna elements increases the effective antenna gain and narrows beams, improving signal strength, but requiring precise beam management. Furthermore, since more beams are needed for coverage, beam management becomes more time-consuming [21, 43]. 

Beamforming is the technique to steer these narrow beams in a specific direction. This is done by adjusting the phase and amplitude of the transmitted signal at each antenna element in the antenna array. In analog beamforming , which is the focus of this work, a single Radio Frequency (RF) chain drives all antenna elements while the phase of the signal to each antenna element is adjusted individually by phase shifters to steer the signal in the desired direction. Recent studies [4, 5, 14, 15] have explored data-driven beam selection, leveraging uplink (UL) channel information at Sub-6GHz frequencies to infer the best mmWave DL beam. While promising, most existing works assume IID data across clients, which is unrealistic in real deployments. In practice, user and environmental conditions differ across cells, resulting in non-IID data distributions that degrade model convergence and accuracy. 2.3 Federated Learning (FL) 

FL [47] enables collaborative model training across clients without sharing raw data. A central parameter server coordinates training on many clients over several communication rounds until convergence. In FedAvg [47], training of a global model 𝑓 𝑔 (𝒙 , 𝒘 𝑔 ) is distributed and synchronous. In each communication round 𝑡 :

Step 1) Broadcast. The parameter server selects a fraction 𝐶 out of the population of 𝐾 clients as the set 𝑆 𝑡 and broadcasts the global model. 

Step 2) Local training. Each selected client 𝑘 ∈ 𝑆 𝑡 trains locally on 𝑛 𝑘 data samples (𝒙 𝑖 , 𝑦 𝑖 ), 𝑖 ∈ P 𝑘 , for 𝐸 epochs before returning an update to the param-eter server .

Step 3) Aggregate. The parameter server aggregates the received updates (typically via weighted averaging) to form a new global model 𝒘 𝑔 .

FedAvg 

This baseline algorithm, known as FedAvg , works efficiently in IID settings but performs poorly when local datasets differ significantly and show non-IID characteristics [67], see Section 2.2. In such cases, local updates diverge, leading to slow convergence and reduced model accuracy. To mitigate these effects, advanced approaches introduce personalization and clustering, grouping clients with similar data or model characteristics. However, CFL methods may introduce new costs: loss-based clustering frameworks such as IFCA [20] require clients to download and evaluate multiple global cluster models in each communication round; server-side methods [55] require server-side access to gradients, which makes them incompatible with standard privacy mechanisms such as Secure Aggregation (SA). Both are core challenges for FL in 6G [41] that this paper aims to overcome. 6 Isaksson et al. 

3 Methods 3.1 Overview 

CFL methods enable personalization based on heterogeneous client data distributions. We propose a modular CFL framework designed to operate efficiently under non-IID data. The framework consists of three components: (1) Data-domain clustering for initialization (Section 3.2); (2) Adaptive model-based clustering during federated training (Section 3.3); (3) Communication-efficient MoE (Section 3.4). These components are modular and can be used either independently or jointly. See pseudocode provided in Appen-dix A. We aim to find clusters, or subsets of clients, that benefit from training together—for example in terms of increased accuracy and reduced DL data volume—as opposed to training a single global model. Note that clients may belong to different clusters at different times during the training process as their models or data distributions evolve. In general, clustering can be done in the latent space using model parameters or gradients, using training loss, or in the data-domain on input data [53]. In some domains, clustering can be done using available metadata such as geographical distance [27] or categorical variables such as software versions and device identifiers [51]. The telecom domain is rich in such side-information—for example configuration parameters and performance indicators. As an example of clustering in the data-domain, in PACFL [61] the authors perform clustering in the data-domain using a low-rank approximation of client data via truncated Singular Value Decomposition (SVD). Data-domain clustering provides informed initial cluster assignments—at the risk of locking in clients into suboptimal clusters before model-based signals emerge. 3.2 Data-domain cluster initialization 

Our aim is to find a similarity measure between two clients that captures the statistical heterogeneity between different client data domains. In principle, this can be any available side-information [51]. 

3.2.1 Local subspace representation. In this work we define a similarity measure in the data subspaces. Inspired by PACFL [48, 61], we leverage the angle between data subspaces, see Fig. 3, spanned by the 𝑟 most significant left singular vectors of a local data matrix of each client. The local data matrix aggregates all client training set samples, one per column. In the case of DeepMIMO, a data sample is an UL channel estimate, which means that the subspace captures the dominant channel directions for each client. Base stations with similar dominant channel directions are expected to be clustered together, so that our similarity measure effectively groups base stations with similar local channel geometry. In the DeepMIMO Outdoor 1 Blockage scenario, dominant channel directions correlate with geographic UE distributions, so base stations with similar local geographic UE distributions cluster together even when they serve different areas of the map. 3.4.2 Expert selection. In standard MoE, a gating model weighs the different experts and mixes their outputs. Here we use the expert weights from a local gating model to select a single expert (global cluster model). We do this by selecting the expert with the maximum weight, without needing to evaluate the experts. This allows us to download a single expert model, reducing the per communication round DL data volume to that of standard FedAvg . The idea is that clients with similar data distributions will select the same expert in order to avoid the degradation caused by the non-IID data characteristics. The local gating models are never communicated and will remain private. 

3.4.3 Exploration and exploitation. Because only the selected expert is evaluated, each gating model learns from partial feedback by observing the loss of the selected expert alone. This frames the problem as a bandit problem where each expert is an arm that receives context 𝒙 . Without exploration, the gating model will exploit the expert it currently believes is the best. 5 Related work 

Non-IID mitigations come in many forms, such as data sharing across clients [68] which reduces distribution divergence but discloses raw data. Another form of non-IID mitigation is CFL which divides clients into clusters where the data distributions are approximately IID internally. In general, CFL can be done [53] in ( i) the latent space using model weights or gradients (server-side); ( ii ) using training loss (client-side); and ( iii ) using available data or metadata (such as local data statistics, geographical coordinates, or categorical variables such as software versions).

Server-side CFL methods use the similarity of models or gradients to cluster clients [12, 33, 42, 55, 63]. 6 Discussion and limitations 

We find that the best of our methods on each dataset reduces DL data volume while maintaining or improving accuracy over IFCA across the evaluated non-IID settings. The MoE method achieves 10 .6 pp higher accuracy on FEMNIST compared to IFCA with 83 .1 % lower DL data volume. Data-domain cluster initialization, PACFL or random, substantially reduces total DL data volume, mainly through lower per-round volume by re-evaluating all cluster models less often, with mixed effects on communication rounds. Furthermore, we find that methods that rely on feature-space similarity, such as PACFL, are working well in specific non-IID scenarios, but under-performing for DeepMIMO where the non-IID characteristics are most prevalent in the label space. This is expected. In other words, the Sub-6GHz input features are more or less IID due to the simulation environment and the similarities between the base stations—with a notable exception to the third base station, see Fig. 8. We find that IFCA-based methods perform better here due to the loss-based clustering capturing this non-IID characteristic better. 

Model capacity. In [51], the authors note that compared to FedAvg , the capacity of the expert models can be reduced. We have not investigated this effect. If the global cluster models can be made smaller, our MoE-based methods can potentially reduce the DL data volume to lower than that of FedAvg .

Number of cluster models. The number of cluster models is a hyperparameter. [24, 26] showed that IFCA-based methods are robust to setting the number of cluster models too high, with the drawback of increased communication. Our methods decrease the per communication round DL data volume, with MoE-based methods reducing per-round DL data volume to that of the baseline FedAvg .

Public datasets. There are few open public datasets in the telecom domain which can be directly used for evaluating distributed algorithms. The realistic datasets needed to properly evaluate large scale distributed algorithms and frameworks such as those proposed here are generally not available for researchers. For example, we have not made use of side-information that can be used for similarity estimation in the telecom domain, such as geographical locations, configuration parameters or performance indicators. This remains unexplored. 

Generalization. While priming our methods with an initial estimate of cluster membership does not significantly change accuracy, it does have an effect on reducing DL data volume, driven by per-round volume. When deployed in a real scenario, the system will have to continually adapt to new and changing non-IID distributions and generalize to new unseen clients [53]. These aspects are underexplored in the telecom domain. We assume that clients are stationary. PACFL [61] includes an algorithm to determine cluster membership of the new clients entering the system without changing the cluster membership of existing clients. 

Radio environment. DeepMIMO is a simplified simulation environment that may not capture real-world channel complexity. For example, in this work we have assumed a horizontal ULA, while most actual products use rectangular Uniform Rectangular Array (URA) in order to fit sufficient number of antennas in constrained spaces. Practical deployments favor vertical configurations. 24 Isaksson et al. Our beams are defined based on a beam angle, while most practical deployments use Fast Fourier Transform (FFT) beams. We argue that the difference for the purpose of this paper is small. The non-IID characteristics seen in the DeepMIMO dataset stem from three causes. (i) The environment in DeepMIMO Outdoor 1 Blockage scenario consists of a road (which is where UEs are located), buildings of different height [2, 3, 52] which reflect the beams and give rise to UE spatial distributions; ( ii ) Blockage and reflectors in front of a single antenna cause a non-uniform distribution of UEs; and ( iii ) The mirror property of the ULA antenna (Fig. 2) is used to construct two clusters of clients, as seen in Fig. 8. The non-IID characteristics stem more from UE distributions than differences in the channel characteristics between base stations, and our hypothesis is that data distributions between sites in real life will come from differences in channel characteristics to a higher degree. Furthermore, the weakest UEs often need beamforming the most, something not considered in the clustering nor evaluated in this paper. We use accuracy as a performance metric for all of our datasets, including DeepMIMO. This may not correspond to actual performance in real networks as users can be served from sidelobes of other beams with good performance. This is further examined in [15, 26]. We therefore argue that a realistic dataset would contain non-IID characteristics from a wider set and that our combined datasets show that our methods perform well in different scenarios. We include FEMNIST and Rotated CIFAR-10 to demonstrate the effectiveness of our methods across different non-IID types, but these do not validate wireless-specific claims. It is expected that MoE-based methods excel in real-world deployments with stronger feature-space heterogeneity between base stations. Real-world validation is essential. 

Number of clients. Scalability to thousands and tens of thousands of clients remains to be studied. New and larger, telecom specific benchmarks are important to reach this goal. 

6.1 Privacy 

CFL can reveal similarities between clients through cluster assignments [32, 66]. PACFL [61] uses truncated SVD which is known to reveal the dominant directions of local data distributions. Mitigations including SA [25] and DP [32, 66] are important directions of future work. 

6.2 Personalized FL 

A common method for addressing non-IID-ness in FL is using methods to limit the local drift from a global model—such as FedProx (FedProx ) [37] which adds a proximal term to penalize deviation from the global model and Stochastic Controlled Averaging algorithm (SCAFFOLD) [31] which estimates and controls the drift between local and global gradients. Such methods can be applied directly in our framework and studying their interactions is another direction of future work. 

6.3 Hyperparameters 

Hyperparameters were tuned individually per dataset using [38], see Section 3.5 and Appendix C. However, there are many hyperparameters interacting with each other in interesting ways, and their effects should be studied more closely. 7 Conclusion and future work 

We proposed a modular CFL framework designed to operate efficiently under non-IID data distributions typical of heterogeneous wireless environments. Our approach combines three complementary components:( i) data-domain cluster initialization using principal-angle similarity; ( ii ) model-based clustering during training with loss-proportional cluster selection; and ( iii ) a communication-efficient MoE formulation with exploration mechanisms. The proposed framework addresses two central challenges in FL for 6G systems: performance degradation under non-IID data and excessive communication overhead in CFL training. Specifically, integrating CFL and MoE models can improve performance under non-IID settings for mmWave beam selection [15, 26]. However, these methods suffer from high communication overhead and computational complexity, limiting their scalability in large-scale 6G systems. By combining data-domain cluster initialization with adaptive model-based clustering, we improve personalization without sharing raw data, and local gating models remain entirely on-device. Across three datasets exhibiting diverse non-IID characteristics—DeepMIMO (concept shift on labels and quantity skew), FEMNIST (feature distribution skew, label distribution skew, and quantity skew), and Rotated CIFAR-10 (feature distribution skew and concept shift on features)—the best method for each dataset matches or improves performance compared to standard IFCA and FedAvg 

baselines. No single method is superior on all datasets. In particular, MoE with PACFL improves accuracy by 10 .6 pp while reducing total DL data volume by 83 .1 % relative to IFCA on FEMNIST. IFCA ★ with PACFL improves accuracy by 4.8 pp on DeepMIMO. A key insight from our study is that different non-IID characteristics favor different clustering strategies. In our experiments, feature-space similarity is particularly effective under covariate shift (FEMNIST), whereas model-based clustering provides greater benefits under concept shift on labels (DeepMIMO). Under feature distribution skew combined with concept shift on features (Rotated CIFAR-10), no method significantly improves accuracy over IFCA, but MoE with PACFL still reduces total DL data volume by 43 .3 %. The proposed framework allows these mechanisms to be combined in a modular manner, enabling adaptation to diverse deployment scenarios. While our evaluation is conducted on a simulation dataset and established benchmarks, several directions remain for future work. These include establishing formal convergence guarantees for the MoE-based clustering mechanism, extending the framework to larger, highly dynamic client populations, extending to a continual learning scenario, and evaluating performance under real-world telecom deployment data. Further investigation into adaptive selection of the number of clusters and expert capacity reduction may yield additional communication gains. Overall, this work shows that adaptive and communication-aware clustering is a promising direction for scalable, resource-efficient federated learning in future 6G networks.
