Abstract 

The efficiency of Deep Neural Network (DNN) accelerators depends on how each tensor operation is mapped onto the hardware. A mapping specifies how the computation is tiled across the memory hierarchy, ordered, and parallelized across the processing elements. These choices form a combinatorially large search space, where effi-cient mappings are hard to find. Most violate hardware constraints, and most remaining mappings underutilize the hardware. Existing mappers either search only part of this space to keep the search tractable, excluding the most efficient mappings, or sample the full space randomly, so most mappings they evaluate are infeasible. We present IMPACT, a mapper that searches the full space. IMPACT pairs a genetic algorithm with a constraint-aware repair operator that converts each infeasible mapping into a feasible one instead of discarding it. On a flexible accelerator, IMPACT outperforms state-of-the-art mappers in EDP, latency, and energy on every DeepBench workload. On a compute-in-memory (CIM) accelerator, whose con-straints leave few mappings feasible, it reduces EDP by 66%, latency by 47%, and energy by 18% over the strongest prior mapper across five networks, and rivals hand-crafted expert mappings. Across flexible and highly constrained accelerators, IMPACT finds efficient mappings automatically. IMPACT will be released as open source.

Executing an operation therefore requires assigning the computation to the hardware: how large a tile each level holds for each dimension of the operation ( tiling factors ), how many PEs process each dimension in parallel ( spatial unrolling ), the order each level steps through its tiles ( loop order ), and which tensors each level retains ( keep/bypass ). Selecting the best mapping is a combinatorial optimization prob-lem. A spatial unrolling that occupies only part of the PE array leaves the remaining PEs idle, and tiling factors that capture little reuse force the same data to be re-fetched, inflating latency and energy [4, 11]. Efficient mappings are therefore a small fraction of the already small feasible subspace. As a result, an effective mapper must satisfy three requirements. 2 Background 

Mappings. Fig. 1b illustrates such a mapping, using a simpler 1D convolution with dimensions 𝑅 and 𝑃 on a DRAM–Global Buffer(GLB)–PE hierarchy, and marks the four decisions a mapping specifies. For every dimension and every memory level, a tiling fac-tor ( x1 ) sets the size of the tile that level holds. Within each level, the 

loop order ( x2 ) fixes the sequence in which its tiles are traversed. At the PE level, the spatial unrolling ( x3 ) selects the dimensions whose tiles execute in parallel across the array, up to its fanout. For every tensor and every level, a keep/bypass ( x4 ) decision sets whether that level buffers the tensor. The four decisions are coupled through the tiling factors. The product of a dimension’s factors across all levels must equal the dimension itself. This product constraint ties the choices of every memory level together. The set of all mappings consistent with a hardware target is the mapspace, and it grows com-binatorially with the number of dimensions and memory levels[20]. 

Imperfect factorization. Standard mappers satisfy the product constraint with tiling factors that divide each dimension exactly, a condition called perfect factorization[18, 20]. When no divisor of a dimension matches the PE count, some PEs remain idle in every iteration. Fig. 1c illustrates this for a dimension D=14 on 6 PEs. The closest divisor is 2, leaving 4 of the 6 PEs idle throughout all iter-ations. Imperfect factorization relaxes the divisibility requirement by allowing a remainder tile [10]. The main tile is sized to fill the PE array, and only the final iteration executes a smaller remainder tile. In the same example, a tile size of 6 occupies all PEs for two iterations and 2 PEs for the last, reducing idle cycles. This improves PE utilization, but enlarges the mapspace, since tile sizes are no longer restricted to divisors of 𝐷 .

Feasibility Constraints. Imperfect factorization expands the map-space with candidates that may leave loop iterations incomplete, exceed PE fanout, or overflow memory capacity [10]. Feasibility constraints identify the subset of this expanded space that is valid and executable [20]. 3 The Mapping Optimization Problem 

Problem Formulation. A hardware target 𝐻 is defined by its mem-ory hierarchy, PE array dimensions, buffer capacities, and fanout limits at each level. A workload 𝑊 is defined by its per-dimension loop bounds. A mapping 𝑀 is a tuple of tiling factors per dimension per level, loop order per level, spatial unrolling, and keep/bypass decisions per tensor per level. The feasible mapspace F ( 𝑊 , 𝐻 ) is the set of all 𝑀 that simultaneously satisfy workload coverage, spa-tial fanout, and storage capacity constraints. The mapping problem is to solve:

where 𝐶 (𝑀,𝑊 , 𝐻 ) is a cost function over energy, latency, or a weighted combination of both. The decision variables are discrete, the constraints couple them non-locally across memory levels, and 

𝐶 is non-convex over 𝑀 . Enumerating F ( 𝑊 , 𝐻 ) exhaustively is therefore intractable at realistic problem scales. 

Search Complexity. Finding an efficient mapping is hard for two compounding reasons. First, the feasibility constraints must hold simultaneously and couple decisions non-locally. A tiling factor at one level determines tile sizes at all levels below, jointly determin-ing whether storage and fanout limits are met [20]. The feasible mapspace F ( 𝑊 , 𝐻 ) therefore occupies a small, irregularly shaped fraction of the full expanded space. Second, within F ( 𝑊 , 𝐻 ), cost is highly non-uniform. Two feasible mappings can differ by orders of magnitude in energy and latency [20]. The high-quality region is consequently a small fraction of F ( 𝑊 , 𝐻 ). Exhaustive search is intractable and random sampling wastes most evaluations on infeasible or dominated candidates. Finding 𝑀 ∗, or a close approxi-mation, requires a search strategy that can navigate a large, sparsely feasible, and highly non-uniform space. 

4 Optimization Framework 4.1 Architecture and Design Rationale 

Section 3 characterized the mapping problem as a search over a large, discrete space that is sparsely feasible and non-uniform in cost. Efficient mappings are rare and scattered, and the space of-fers no gradient to follow, so IMPACT searches it globally with a genetic algorithm that explores broadly while concentrating on the strongest mappings it finds. A genetic algorithm alone cannot keep that search feasible. Loop orders are independent choices, so recombining them preserves feasibility. The tiling factors of a di-mension and the keep/bypass flags, however, are coupled through the storage capacity constraint, and any change at one level can invalidate the factorization as a whole. IMPACT therefore repairs the factors of each mapping with a dedicated local operator before it is scored. The framework has three components: 

• A genetic algorithm evolves the global structure of each mapping across generations. 

• A constraint-aware operator expresses constraint violation as a feasibility score and uses a Metropolis-style acceptance rule [8, 19] with simulated annealing [16] to repair the tiling factors of each offspring toward a feasible configuration. 

• Timeloop evaluates each mapping analytically and returns en-ergy, latency, and EDP [26]. ASPDAC ’27, January 25–28, 2027, Tokyo, Japan Stavrakakis et al. 

Fig. 2 shows the pipeline, which maps a DNN workload and a hard-ware specification to a Pareto-optimal set of mappings. IMPACT samples an initial population across the mapspace without enforc-ing validity, since the operator repairs every offspring before it is scored. Each generation applies crossover and mutation, repairs the tiling factors of the offspring, evaluates the repaired mappings with Timeloop, and selects the next generation with NSGA-II [6]. Since energy and latency trade off and no single mapping minimizes both, NSGA-II keeps a population of individuals close to the Pareto frontier. The loop repeats until the evaluation budget is spent. 

4.2 Representation and Genetic Operators 

Mapping Encoding. IMPACT encodes each candidate as a chromo-some that captures the complete mapping. A mapping is a loop nest, the same form that the analytical model evaluates, so each gene cor-responds directly to a loop bound, a loop order, or a buffering choice in the evaluated mapping. At each memory level, each workload dimension maps to a loop whose bound sets the tile size at that level, and at the PE level, selected dimensions are unrolled spatially across the array. The chromosome carries three gene classes: loop-order genes fix the nesting order at each level, factor genes set the loop bounds at each level, and keep/bypass genes select which tensors each level buffers. Each gene also records the hardware restrictions on its decision, such as a level that cannot buffer a given tensor, so the operators never violate these restrictions during search. 

Crossover. Crossover recombines and perpetuates the structural traits that high-performing mappings share. Keep/bypass and loop-order genes recombine independently under a uniform scheme, making no structural assumption about gene cross-correlation [23]. The tiling factors of a dimension are interdependent through the product constraint, so they recombine as a single unit that preserves the dimension’s factorization. 

Mutation. Mutation introduces the variation that recombination alone cannot reach, keeping the search from stalling. It handles the discrete genes directly, permuting a loop order or flipping a keep/bypass flag. The legal values of a dimension’s tiling factors, however, form a thin, irregular set that a random perturbation almost always misses, so mutation cannot explore them, and the offspring it produces are almost all infeasible. 

5 Constraint-Aware Factor Search 

After crossover and mutation, most offspring carry infeasible tiling factors. IMPACT pairs its genetic search with a repair operator that moves these factors to a nearby feasible set before the mapping is scored. The feasible set, however, is fragmented into disconnected regions, so moving only through feasible factors cannot cross be-tween them. The operator therefore uses an annealed Metropolis-style walk that steps through infeasible ground between the regions. Fig. 2 depicts the walk. Starting from the offspring’s factors, each step proposes a new factor set, scores it by its distance from feasibil-ity, and accepts or rejects it, until the walk settles on a feasible set. 

Proposal Distribution. Given the factors of an offspring after crossover and mutation, each step proposes a new factor set by scaling the current factors. IMPACT draws each proposal from a log-normal centered on the current factors and rounds to the near-est positive integer, since factors are integer-valued. Because the factors combine multiplicatively and range over orders of mag-nitude, the proposal scales them rather than adding to them; the

Acceptance. This soft score lets the operator repair factors with a Metropolis-style acceptance rule. The operator always accepts a proposal that lowers the violation, and accepts one that raises it with a probability equal to the score ratio of the proposal to the current set. Only the final set need be feasible, so the operator may accept steps through infeasible ground, crossing between the disconnected regions that a feasibility-preserving move could not. Because the feasibility score falls exponentially with violation, large moves into infeasibility are rarely accepted. The walk therefore stays near the feasible regions and reaches a feasible set even where several constraints are tight.

6 Evaluation 

The evaluation tests whether (i) IMPACT finds better mappings than prior automated mappers, and (ii) whether it sustains that quality on hardware whose constraints leave few mappings valid. We compare against Timeloop[20], which defines the mapspace and cost model, and Ruby[10], the only prior mapper that searches the imperfect-factorization space. GAMMA[15], ZigZag[18], and SALSA[14] are built on different cost models that only evaluate perfectly factorized mappings, and cannot score the mappings this work targets. The first experiment evaluates the mappers on Eyeriss, a flexible architecture on which prior mappers already perform well, and isolates the quality of the search itself. The second moves to a compute-in-memory architecture, where a deep memory hierarchy and rigid tiling rules make feasible mappings rare, and adds expert hand-crafted mappings to test whether IMPACT remains effective where constraint-unaware search breaks down. The third measures each method’s valid-mapping yield, characterizing how each search navigates the feasible region under the same time constraint.

Comparison to automated mappers. IMPACT outperforms both Timeloop and Ruby on every workload family and every objective. As shown in Figure 4, the largest reduction is in energy. IMPACT lowers it by 33% over Timeloop at the mean across workloads, while Ruby lowers it by 1%. Latency falls by 14%, and EDP by a more modest 5%. 

Effect of workload shape. IMPACT’s gains for EDP and latency concentrate on layers where perfect factorization cannot fill the PE array. Vision layers whose dimensions align with Eyeriss’s 14x12 PE array map well under Timeloop, so Ruby and IMPACT add little. On Face and DeepSpeech, filling the array requires imperfect factoriza-tion. Here Ruby reduces latency below Timeloop, and IMPACT re-duces it further. For minimum-energy mappings, IMPACT strongly outperforms both. By uniquely exploring the imperfectly factorized tilings space, it finds mappings with lower data movement. 

Source of the gains. IMPACT’s advantage on Eyeriss comes from the structure of its search. The repair operator keeps its evaluations feasible across the imperfectly factorized region, and the genetic algorithm concentrates those evaluations around the strongest mappings it finds. 6.2 Evaluation on CIM Architecture 

We next evaluate IMPACT on a compute-in-memory architecture whose dataflow is far more constrained than the Eyeriss spatial array. We use a provided CimLoop[1] architecture, based on the analog RRAM crossbar of [25], whose fixed reduction dataflow is representative of CimLoop’s CIM models. This dataflow imposes rigid tiling rules, so feasible mappings are sparse and most candi-dates are infeasible. We compare IMPACT against Timeloop and Ruby on five networks: a Vision Transformer (ViT)[7], VGG-16[22], AlexNet[17], ResNet-18[9], and MobileNetV3[12]. For this architec-ture we additionally compare against the hand-crafted mappings provided by CimLoop. Each mapper searches every layer for the same wall-clock time, following the same 32-run protocol as Sec-tion 6.1, reporting the mean across runs. Network energy and la-tency are summed across layers, while network EDP is computed as their product. All metrics are normalized to the Timeloop baseline. 

Comparison to automated mappers. IMPACT is the only auto-mated mapper that improves on Timeloop across every network and every metric. As Figure 5 shows, averaged over the five work-loads, it reduces EDP by 66%, latency by 46.6%, and energy by 18.2% relative to Timeloop. Ruby searches a larger space than Timeloop but a smaller one than IMPACT; even so, without a structured search it reaches no high-quality mappings. On this architecture it underperforms even Timeloop, inflating EDP by 4 × to 15 × and failing for several AlexNet layers. 

Comparison to hand-crafted mappings. IMPACT outperforms the hand-crafted expert mappings on most configurations, without any manual tuning. It improves on the experts in 11 of the 15 metric-workload cases. The expert mappings are tuned to minimize EDP, and they improve on IMPACT in only four cases, three in EDP and one in energy, with an average discrepancy of 4.2% and a maximum of 17% on VGG-16. IMPACT reaches the lowest latency on all five networks and lower energy on four of the five, improving on the experts by 32% and 20% on average, respectively. IMPACT therefore matches the experts on the objective they are tuned for, and outperforms them on the objectives they are not. 

Key takeaway. IMPACT’s advantage over prior mappers widens as the architecture becomes more constrained. On a flexible architec-ture, loose constraints leave many mappings valid, so every mapper finds competitive solutions and IMPACT’s margin is modest. On a constrained architecture, Timeloop is confined to perfect factor-ization and cannot fill the array, while Ruby searches an expanded space without structure and mostly evaluates invalid or inefficient candidates. IMPACT’s constraint-aware repair lets the genetic al-gorithm keep exploring and exploiting the sparse feasible region throughout the search. 6.3 Valid-Mapping Yield 

We characterize each search by its feasible-sample rate on Eyeriss. Alongside the three mappers, we measure a plain genetic algo-rithm, obtained by removing the repair operator from IMPACT. Like GAMMA, it searches without constraint awareness, and isolates the repair operator’s contribution to the yield. The yield is the frac-tion of candidates that produce a mapping satisfying all workload-coverage, spatial-fanout, and storage-capacity constraints. A can-didate is one attempt at a feasible mapping: a sampled mapping for Timeloop and Ruby, an offspring for the plain GA, and one repair walk for IMPACT, whose yield is therefore the fraction of walks that reach a feasible set rather than restart. We report it per DeepBench workload, under the same wall-clock budget as in Section 6.1. 

Valid-mapping yield. IMPACT spends its budget on feasible map-pings, while the baselines spend most of theirs on infeasible ones. As Figure 6 shows, it reaches 83%, against 10% for Timeloop, 5% for Ruby, and 5% for the plain genetic algorithm. The yields sepa-rate the search strategies. Ruby samples the imperfect-factorization space without checking feasibility, and 95% of its candidates are invalid. The plain GA reaches only 5%. Its recombination barely preserves feasible structure, so most offspring violate constraints and are discarded. A genetic algorithm without repair produces no more feasible mappings than random sampling. The repair operator lifts the yield to 83% by steering each offspring to the feasible set, and is what makes search possible in this space. 

Key Takeaway. The repair operator’s feasibility yield explains the quality gains of the previous experiments. In Sections 6.1 and 6.2, IMPACT ran under the same budget as the baselines, yet found better mappings. With its budget spent on feasible candidates rather than invalid ones, IMPACT’s search covers far more of the real mapspace in the same time, and finds the better mappings within it. 7 Discussion 

Comparison to prior work. Table 2 shows that no prior mapper handles both difficulties of mapping search: imperfect factorization and hardware constraints. Timeloop[20], ZigZag[18], GAMMA[15], and SALSA[14] all restrict search to perfectly-factorized mappings; SALSA further fixes spatial unrolling as an input. GAMMA’s ge-netic operators do not enforce dataflow constraints, confining its search to fully flexible accelerators. Ruby[10] comes closest, open-ing the imperfect-factorization space that holds the best mappings, but searches without structure or constraint awareness, so most candidates are invalid or low-quality. Unlike these, IMPACT pairs a genetic algorithm with a constraint-aware repair operator, ad-dressing both imperfect factorization and hardware constraints, and reaches the high-quality mappings the others miss. 

Practical impact. IMPACT’s ability to find high-quality mappings for a given tensor accelerator enables systematic design-space ex-ploration across the software-hardware stack. Hardware engineers can measure how a change in the architecture affects performance, without manually searching for the best mapping at each configu-ration. Compiler engineers can retarget a workload to a new accel-erator with an IMPACT-generated mapping, instead of re-tuning the mapping for every architecture by hand. 

8 Conclusion 

In this work, we present IMPACT, a framework for finding high-quality mappings on tensor accelerators, including highly con-strained ones. It uses a genetic algorithm for structured search of the imperfect-factorization space, and a repair operator that keeps the search feasible as constraints tighten. IMPACT consistently out-performs prior automated mappers, and on constrained accelerators it rivals hand-crafted expert mappings. In practice, IMPACT decou-ples automated mapper performance from how constrained the hardware is. It therefore extends automated mapping to specialized accelerators, which until now depended on expert tuning.
