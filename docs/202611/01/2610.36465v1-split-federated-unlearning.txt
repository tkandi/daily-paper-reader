Split Federated Unlearning

Abstract
Split federated learning (SFL) combines partitioned model execu-
tion with federated aggregation to support collaborative learning
across distributed Web and edge environments. However, removing
learned contributions in SFL is challenging due to cross-partition
coupling and historical influence propagation through dual-side
aggregation. To the best of our knowledge, we provide the first
systematic formulation of Split Federated Unlearning (SFU), en-
compassing client-wise and class-wise removal, counterfactual re-
training, and partition-specific update constraints. We theoretically
characterize historical influence propagation and mode-dependent
unlearning feasibility, revealing structural limitations of restricted
model updates. As an initial solution, we propose SURE, integrating
dual-importance estimation, selective parameter perturbation, and
importance-guided recovery. Extensive experiments with existing
unlearning methods adapted to SFL reveal substantial forgetting–
utility trade-offs across model architectures, data distributions, and
update modes. SURE achieves competitive agreement with mode-
matched retraining at modest additional cost, while further analyses
highlight the impact of split position and update constraints on
unlearning behavior. These findings establish SFU as a distinct
research problem and provide a foundation for unlearning in parti-
tioned federated systems.

Introduction
Personalized Web services increasingly rely on user interactions
distributed across devices and data owners [25]. Federated learn-
ing (FL) enables collaborative training without centralizing raw
data [1, 21], but training complete models on resource-constrained
clients can be costly [6]. Split federated learning (SFL) addresses
this limitation by partitioning model execution between clients and
a server while retaining federated aggregation [27]. As illustrated
in Fig. 1(a), client-side models and their corresponding server-side
replicas are jointly optimized, with each partition aggregated sepa-
rately. This architecture offers a practical approach to collaborative
learning in resource-heterogeneous Web and edge environments.
While SFL keeps user data local, it does not address the removal
of their learned contributions when users withdraw data or leave a
Web service. Deleting local records or ending participation cannot
undo their accumulated influence on the trained model. Machine

unlearning seeks to remove such influence without full retrain-
ing [3, 26]. Existing federated unlearning methods, including Fed-
Eraser [19] and FedRecovery [35], address contributions propagated
through federated aggregation, while split unlearning considers
removal under partitioned model execution [4, 10]. Neither for-
mulation directly captures SFL, where both mechanisms operate
together.
Combining split execution with dual-side aggregation introduces
distinct challenges for unlearning in SFL. Fig. 1(b) illustrates client-
wise and class-wise removal, with full retained-data retraining as
the ideal counterfactual reference and three model update modes.
As highlighted in Fig. 1(c), three challenges arise: cross-partition
coupling, as both partitions interact through exchanged activations
and gradients; historical influence propagation, as repeated dual-side
aggregation propagates contributions across clients and rounds; and
mode-dependent feasibility, as restricting updates to one partition
may limit attainable forgetting. These intertwined challenges moti-
vate a dedicated formulation of Split Federated Unlearning (SFU).
To address these challenges, we formalize SFU for client-wise
and class-wise removal, distinguishing forgetting requests from
the permitted model updates (Client-only, Server-only, and Joint).
Full retained-data retraining serves as the ideal counterfactual ref-
erence, while mode-matched retraining captures the effects of re-
stricted updates. We further characterize how forgotten-data influ-
ence propagates through coupled dual-side training and analyze
mode-dependent unlearning feasibility through local prediction-
discrepancy bounds and excess retained-data risk under a qua-
dratic approximation. These results identify structural limitations
imposed by model partitioning, independently of any particular
unlearning algorithm.
To investigate SFU in practice, we propose SURE, an initial SFU
approach combining dual-importance estimation, selective parame-
ter perturbation, and retained-data recovery. SURE identifies target-
relevant parameters while accounting for retained knowledge, ap-
plies targeted perturbations within the permitted model partitions,
and performs importance-guided recovery through the original SFL
training protocol. We systematically evaluate SURE and existing
unlearning methods adapted to SFL across different datasets, data
distributions, and model update modes. Experiments on MNIST,
CIFAR-10, and CIFAR-100 reveal substantial forgetting–utility trade-
offs among adapted baselines, while SURE achieves competitive
agreement with mode-matched retraining at modest additional cost.
Further analyses examine the effects of split position, component
design, and multiple client removals.

knowledge, we provide the first systematic formulation of Split

Related Work
Federated Unlearning. Machine unlearning aims to remove learned
data contributions without full retraining [3, 15]. In FL [2, 21], un-
learning is complicated by historical contributions accumulated
through model aggregation. Early approaches explored update
calibration and selective retraining [19, 20], alongside privacy-
preserving model recovery [35]. Subsequent work investigated
class-specific pruning, incentive-aware selection, subgraph removal,
and privacy-aware recovery [17, 18, 30, 31]. Recent methods further
address gradient conflicts, server-side unlearning, and task-vector
alignment [16, 22, 24, 33]. However, these approaches do not ex-
plicitly account for dependencies between separately aggregated
model partitions.
Split Learning and Unlearning. Split learning (SL) partitions model
execution between clients and servers through intermediate acti-
vations and gradients [29]. Recent split unlearning methods ad-
dress training dependencies through isolation-based removal [10]
and gradient-cache projection with retained-data fine-tuning [4].
Parameter-selective approaches, such as SalUn [5], further demon-
strate the role of weight saliency in targeted unlearning. Neverthe-
less, these methods do not jointly characterize split-model depen-
dencies and repeated federated aggregation.
Split Federated Learning. SFL combines partitioned execution
with federated aggregation to support resource-constrained clients [27].
Subsequent studies have improved scalability through server-side
parallelism [8] and investigated training instability under heteroge-
neous data [28]. However, such studies primarily address training
efficiency and knowledge preservation rather than deliberate re-
moval of historical contributions. Unlearning in SFL must addition-
ally account for cross-partition dependencies, dual-side aggregation,
and partition-specific update constraints.

Experimental Evaluation
We systematically evaluate SURE and adapted unlearning methods
under SFU, examining forgetting–utility trade-offs, model parti-

5.1
Experimental Setup
Datasets and configurations. We consider two unlearning sce-
narios: client-wise unlearning, which removes the contributions of
a designated client, and class-wise unlearning, which removes a
target class across clients. Experiments cover three datasets and
four dataset–model configurations: MNIST/LeNet, CIFAR-10/CNN,
CIFAR-10/ResNet-18, and CIFAR-100/ResNet-18. For client-wise
unlearning, we consider ten clients with one removal request under

data partitions, the latter assigning disjoint label subsets to clients.
For class-wise unlearning, we remove a designated class across
clients under Dirichlet-based non-IID partitions (𝛼= 0.1) [9]. Both
scenarios are examined under Client-only, Server-only, and Joint
update modes. ResNet-18 is partitioned after its first residual block,
with additional cut positions examined separately. Further details
on data partitioning, model architectures, and training configura-
tions are provided in Appendix C.1.

5.3
Impact of Split Position on Unlearning
Split position determines the trainable model partitions under dif-
ferent update modes, thereby influencing unlearning behavior. We
evaluate SURE across nine ResNet-18 split positions (Fig. 3). As the
split deepens, Client-only updates exhibit declining Test and Retain
accuracy, whereas Server-only updates show the opposite trend.
Joint updates exhibit non-monotonic behavior, while Forget accu-
racy varies across modes. To examine the representation changes,
Fig. 4 reports layer-wise Centered Kernel Alignment (CKA) [13]
relative to Original. Final-stage CKA decreases from 0.980 to 0.908
between splits 1 and 8 under Client-only updates, but increases
from 0.954 to 0.980 under Server-only updates. Additional t-SNE
visualizations in Appendix A.2 (Fig. 5 and Fig. 6) illustrate repre-
sentation differences across split positions and model partitions.
These results highlight the interplay between split placement and
update constraints, although representation changes alone do not
establish forgetting effectiveness.
5.4
Ablation Study
We evaluate seven ablation variants (Table 3): A1 removes mag-
nitude weighting; A2 fixes the first trainable layer; A3 disables
retained-importance protection (𝛾= 0); A4 applies hard layer nega-
tion; A5 uses standard retained-data FedAvg recovery; and A6/A7 re-
tain only forgotten-importance suppression or retained-importance
amplification, respectively. Full SURE achieves the lowest Avg. Gap
under Server-only (1.63 pp) and Joint (1.90 pp) updates, tying with
A2 under Client-only (0.41 pp). A1 and A4 consistently increase
the gap, while recovery variants reveal distinct forgetting–utility
trade-offs. Under Joint updates, A6 reduces Test accuracy to 72.42%,
whereas A7 increases Forget accuracy to 70.11%. In contrast, SURE
achieves 77.36% Test and 65.34% Forget accuracy, approaching Re-
train’s 64.13% Forget accuracy. These results highlight the comple-
mentary roles of importance estimation, selective perturbation, and
guided recovery. Hyperparameter sensitivity is further examined
in Appendix A.4 (Figure 7).
5.5
Efficiency Analysis
We compare communication and computation costs across three
update modes (Table 4). SURE incurs modest overhead over Fine-
tune while remaining substantially more efficient than MoDe and
FedMemPrune. On CIFAR-10/ResNet-18 under Joint updates, SURE
requires 218 GB and 1,370 TFLOPs, compared with 213 GB and 1,370
TFLOPs for Fine-tune and 449 GB and 2,890 TFLOPs for MoDe. Fe-
dRecovery achieves substantially lower costs through training-free
correction using stored historical updates, although historical stor-
age costs are excluded [35]. However, lower costs do not necessarily
imply closer agreement with Retrain.
6
Limitations
SURE provides an initial approach to SFU, with effectiveness vary-
ing across model update modes, split positions, and forgetting sce-
narios. Its recovery stage requires retained data, and formal for-
getting guarantees remain unexplored. Moreover, agreement with
mode-matched retraining under restricted updates does not neces-
sarily imply equivalence to full retraining. Future work will explore
more scalable unlearning methods, broader SFL architectures, and
stronger forgetting guarantees.

7
Conclusion
We establish Split Federated Unlearning (SFU) as a distinct research
problem, providing a unified formulation and theoretical charac-
terization of historical influence propagation and mode-dependent
unlearning feasibility. As an initial solution, SURE integrates dual-
importance estimation, selective perturbation, and guided recovery.
Extensive experiments across diverse model architectures, data
distributions, and update modes reveal the forgetting–utility chal-
lenges of existing methods adapted to SFL, while SURE achieves
competitive agreement with mode-matched retraining at modest
overhead, laying a foundation for principled unlearning in split
federated systems.

Additional Experimental Results
A.1
Client-Wise Unlearning under IID
Partitions
Under IID partitions, retained clients share the same label space, so
forgotten-sample accuracy may remain high even after retraining.
As shown in Table 5, SURE preserves competitive utility across
architectures and update modes. On CIFAR-10/ResNet-18 under
Joint updates, SURE achieves 88.01% Test accuracy, close to Retrain
(87.60%), whereas FedEraser and MoDe drop to 57.83% and 46.78%.
Nevertheless, deviations in Forget accuracy and MIA, particularly
on CIFAR-100, highlight the distinction between utility preservation
and retraining agreement.
A.2
Additional Representation Analysis
Client-Wise Representation Responses. To complement the split-
position analysis, we visualize forgotten-sample representations
at shallow (split 1) and deep (split 8) cuts on CIFAR-10/ResNet-
18. As shown in Fig. 5, forgotten samples remain largely inter-
mixed with other samples under Client-only updates at split 1,
whereas Server-only and Joint updates produce more distinct post-
unlearning neighborhoods. At split 8, Client-only updates also yield
a more concentrated target-sample distribution. These observations
illustrate how cut placement and update permissions affect repre-
sentation responses, although t-SNE separation alone does not
establish forgetting effectiveness.
Class-Wise Representation Responses. We further examine
client cut-layer and server pre-classifier representations under Joint
updates on CIFAR-10/ResNet-18. Fig. 6 shows relatively mixed class
distributions at the client partition, whereas the server partition
exhibits more structured class neighborhoods. SURE and Retrain
display different target-sample arrangements, suggesting that sim-
ilar prediction-level forgetting behavior does not necessarily im-
ply identical learned representations. These qualitative observa-
tions complement the performance results without establishing
representation-level equivalence.
A.3
Multiple Forgetting Clients
We evaluate SURE with multiple client removals on CIFAR-10/ResNet-
18 under Dirichlet non-IID partitions (𝛼= 0.1), where 𝑇–𝐹denotes
𝑇total clients and 𝐹forgetting clients. Each setting uses its corre-
sponding mode-matched Retrain reference. As shown in Table 6,
increasing the forgetting fraction from 10% (10–1) to 50% (10–5)
reduces Test accuracy and increases Avg. Gap across all update
modes. The effect is particularly pronounced under Joint updates,
where Test accuracy drops from 77.36% to 56.42% and Avg. Gap
rises from 1.90 to 4.36 pp. Comparisons among 10–1, 20–2, and 50–5
further reveal mode-dependent behavior at a fixed forgetting ratio.
These results demonstrate the challenges of maintaining utility and
retraining consistency under larger removal requests.

C.2
Baseline Adaptation to SFL
All baselines share SURE’s data partitions, model architectures,
and unlearning requests, with their operations adapted to split
computation and dual-side aggregation. Parameter modifications
are restricted to the permitted update modes, while frozen parti-
tions retain their parameters and persistent states. Recovery-based
methods follow the original SFL aggregation protocol, and history-
dependent methods use records from the training runs. FedEraser
and FedRecovery are evaluated only for client-wise unlearning.

C.3
Membership Inference Attack
We use a gradient-boosting attacker with label-aware prediction
features (probabilities, entropy, and margins). Forgotten training
samples serve as members and class-matched unseen samples as
non-members. Balanced attack data are split into disjoint training,
calibration, and evaluation sets. The attacker is fitted and calibrated
on Original, then held fixed across Retrain and unlearning methods.
ROC-AUC measures membership distinguishability, with 50% indi-
cating chance-level discrimination rather than certified forgetting.
C.4
Metric and Cost Accounting
For class-wise unlearning, Forget measures class-0 test accuracy,
while Test includes all classes. Communication includes model
transfers, cut-layer activations, and gradients, while computation is
estimated in FLOPs. Client-wise and ablation costs reflect executed
procedures, whereas class-wise costs use a fixed 40-round budget.
