Abstract 

As AI accelerator architectures become increasingly diverse, integrating different architectures into a unified system is emerging as a new heterogeneous computing paradigm. GPUs and WSEs differ substantially in compute, memory, and communication organization, creating both architec-tural complementarity and additional overheads, yet how to translate this heterogeneity into practical performance gains remains unclear. To address this gap, we propose a hierarchi-cal benchmarking methodology to characterize GPU–WSE heterogeneous execution and the conditions for exploiting complementarity, together with an analytical performance model that identifies when heterogeneity becomes a bottle-neck and when it yields system-level performance gains. 

1 Introduction 

GPUs have been remarkably successful in accelerating mod-ern AI workloads due to their massive parallelism and ma-ture software ecosystem, making them the de facto stan-dard for both training and inference[16, 25]. However as AI workloads become increasingly diverse, the limitations of general-purpose GPU architectures have become more apparent, driving the emergence of specialized accelerator architectures optimized for AI workloads. Wafer-scale accelerators represent an emerging class of specialized AI architectures that construct an entire wafer as a single computing device, rather than dividing it into mul-tiple individual chips. For example, the Wafer-Scale Engine (WSE)[17] distributes approximately 850K compute cores and 44 GB of local SRAM across a single wafer-scale chip. 

Motivation for GPU–WSE Integration: Modern AI work-loads exhibit diverse computational and memory character-istics, making it difficult for a single accelerator architecture to efficiently serve all workload. The complementary ar-chitectural characteristics of GPUs and WSEs provide an opportunity to address this challenge through heteroge-neous integration. GPUs integrate a large number of highly parallel compute units and leverage SIMT execution to effectively utilize these resources, delivering high com-putational throughput. In contrast, WSEs leverage their massive chip area to integrate large amounts of distributed SRAM, allowing larger working sets to reside entirely on-chip and transforming much of the data movement that would otherwise rely on off-chip memory into on-chip accesses. Consequently, these distinct architectures exhibit different execution characteristics across AI workloads, creating opportunities for architectural complementarity in heterogeneous systems.This opportunity is also beginning to attract industrial interest, with emerging systems exploring the integration of AI accelerators with complementary architectural characteristics. For example, AWS and Cere-bras have announced plans to integrate Trainium and WSE within a heterogeneous AI infrastructure[3]. 

Challenges in Exploiting GPU-WSE Complementarity: 

While the architectural differences between GPUs and WSEs create theoretical opportunities for complementarity, they also introduce substantial architectural asymmetry. Consequently, the feasibility boundaries of heterogeneous GPU–WSE execution have not been systematically studied. First, current AI system stacks are primarily designed for homogeneous accelerators or accelerators with similar ar-chitectures. Their parallelization strategies, system software, and interconnect mechanisms typically assume comparable hardware capabilities and execution characteristics, making them ill-suited to coordinating fundamentally different accelerators while fully exploiting their respective strengths.

Second, even though existing heterogeneous systems ac-count for hardware differences, the forms of heterogeneity they address differ from those in GPU–WSE systems. For example, heterogeneous GPU systems [8, 10, 15, 18, 19] pri-marily focus on heterogeneity across different generations of GPUs, while their underlying architectures remain largely the same. GPU–FPGA systems [1, 11] further introduce ar-chitectural heterogeneity, but FPGAs are general-purpose reconfigurable hardware whose design goals and execution characteristics differ substantially from those of specialized AI accelerators. Third, emerging heterogeneous platforms incorporating specialized AI architectures remain largely under development, and their performance characteristics remain largely unavailable to the research community. Con-sequently, existing heterogeneous system studies have not sufficiently explored heterogeneous cooperation between GPUs and specialized AI accelerators with substantially dif-ferent architectures. This leaves three fundamental questions: 1) Do the architectural differences between GPUs and WSEs create exploitable performance complementarity? 2) Under what application-level execution constraints can such comple-mentarity translate into end-to-end performance gains? 3) What system support is required to realize these gains? 

Our Approach: To answer these questions, we develop a hierarchical benchmarking methodology to systematically investigate the impact of architectural differences between GPUs and WSEs. At the application layer, we select three representative LLM execution paradigms—data parallelism, pipeline parallelism, and prefill–decode disaggregation—as case studies to analyze how architectural differences affect heterogeneous system performance. At the system layer, we investigate how compilers, communication mechanisms, and runtime systems should adapt to these architectural dif-ferences; at the hardware layer, we characterize the physical requirements for efficiently integrating these different archi-tectures. Observation: Our results show that architectural difference is not inherently beneficial: its impact depends on whether the workload can be partitioned flexibly enough to accommodate architecture-specific asymmetry, and whether heterogeneous tasks can be mapped to the architectural str- engths of different devices. We further develop an analytical performance model that captures the key effects of architectural differences on heterogeneous execution. The main contributions are summarized as follows: 

• We present a hierarchical, measurement-driven bench-marking methodology that examines how GPU–WSE architectural differences affect heterogeneous execu-tion and its feasibility boundaries across the applica-tion, system, and hardware layers. 

• We apply this methodology to three representative LLM execution paradigms and reveal how different execution constraints can amplify, mitigate, or exploit GPU–WSE architectural differences. Based on these observations, we further identify the system and hard-ware support required to accommodate such architec-tural heterogeneity. 

• We develop a lightweight analytical model that formal-izes these observations by capturing the interactions among workload decomposition, architectural asym-metry, resource utilization, and interconnect overhead. We validate the model against representative mea-sured configurations and use it to characterize feasi-bility boundaries and reason about unmeasured GPU– WSE configurations.

Compute. The A100 consists of multiple Streaming Multi-processors (SMs) and employs the SIMT execution model to schedule a large number of threads on each SM, effectively utilizing highly parallel compute units to achieve high com-putational throughput. In contrast, the WSE leverages the massive chip area enabled by wafer-scale integration to dis-tribute approximately 850K processing elements (PEs) across a single wafer. Each PE contains an independent compute core, with computation partitioned and mapped across a large number of PEs and driven by dataflow execution. Thus, GPUs achieve high arithmetic throughput through densely integrated compute units and massive thread-level paral-lelism, whereas the WSE exploits wafer-scale integration to deploy fine-grained compute units for distributed execution across the wafer. 

Memory. The A100 employs a hierarchical memory system consisting of on-chip registers, shared memory, and caches,

together with off-chip HBM, providing multiple levels with different capacities and bandwidths. In contrast, the WSE leverages its wafer-scale area to integrate a large amount of distributed SRAM. Each PE is tightly coupled with up to 48KB of local SRAM, providing approximately 44GB of on-chip memory across the wafer. Thus, GPUs primarily rely on memory hierarchy and data reuse to reduce off-chip memory accesses, whereas the WSE allows larger working sets to be distributed and retained close to the compute cores, enabling more data accesses to remain on-chip. 

Scaling. GPU typically scale by adding discrete chips, with data exchange between GPUs relying on device-level inter-connects such as NVLink. In contrast, the WSE can store model data in the external MemoryX system and stream it to the wafer on demand. For example, during training, the Weight Streaming mechanism sequentially transfers the weights of each layer from MemoryX to the wafer for exe-cution. Thus, GPUs primarily scale computational capacity through multi-device parallelism, whereas a single WSE can execute the entire model computation. 

2.2 Cross-Device Execution Strategies for LLM Workloads 

Modern LLM systems rely on cross-device execution to dis-tribute workloads and coordinate computation across multi-ple devices. During training, data parallelism (DP) [22] parti-tions the input data across devices, where each device main-tains a model replica and synchronizes gradients through all-reduce. Pipeline parallelism (PP) [13] partitions the model into stages across devices and transfers intermediate activa-tions between them. During inference, prefill–decode (PD) disaggregation [27] places the prefill and decode stages on different devices, allowing the two stages to be executed and managed separately. We use these three representative cross-device execution paradigms as case studies to study architectural heterogeneity in GPU–WSE systems.

3.2 Challenges in Exploiting GPU–WSE Architectural Complementarity 

As discussed above, while the architectural differences be-tween GPUs and WSEs create potential complementarity, they also introduce substantial architectural asymmetry, which raises three key challenges: 

Realizing architectural complementarity through heterogeneous execution. Although GPUs and WSEs pro-vide complementary hardware capabilities, whether such complementarity can be effectively exploited under hetero-geneous execution remains unclear. Existing parallelization strategies are primarily designed for homogeneous systems and typically rely on uniform workload partitioning across devices. In heterogeneous GPU–WSE systems, hardware asymmetry breaks this assumption, while the tolerance of these strategies to performance asymmetry remains unclear. Moreover, whether these strategies can effectively exploit the architectural complementarity between GPUs and WSEs is also unclear. 

> 3Hu et al.

Adapting system support to architectural asymme-try. Realizing the potential benefits of GPU–WSE comple-mentarity requires the system stack to coordinate devices with substantially different execution characteristics. Exist-ing compiler, communication, and runtime mechanisms are primarily designed for homogeneous platforms and may not effectively accommodate such asymmetry. It therefore re-mains unclear what system support is required to efficiently coordinate heterogeneous execution. 

Meeting architectural requirements for heteroge-neous platforms . Even with effective workload mapping and system support, the benefits of heterogeneous execution depend on whether the underlying hardware can efficiently support cross-device coordination. In particular, communi-cation between architecturally distinct devices may offset their local performance advantages. It remains unclear what interconnect capabilities are required for GPU–WSE comple-mentarity to translate into system-level performance gains.

4.1 Benchmarking Framework 

To address the three challenges discussed above, we develop a three-tier benchmarking framework spanning the applica-tion , system , and hardware layers, as illustrated in Fig. 2. The framework systematically explores how GPU–WSE architec-tural differences affect heterogeneous execution: the applica-tion layer investigates what characteristics of parallelization strategies determine their tolerance to architectural asym-metry and ability to exploit architectural complementarity; the system layer investigates how compiler, communication, and runtime mechanisms should adapt to architectural asym-metry; and the hardware layer examines the interconnect conditions under which the potential benefits of heteroge-neous execution can translate into end-to-end performance gains. We describe each layer in detail as follows: 

Application Layer. At this layer, we investigate what ex-ecution characteristics enable parallelization strategies to tolerate GPU–WSE performance asymmetry and exploit the complementarity between the two architectures. For hetero-geneous execution, a key distinction lies in the constraints imposed by different execution paradigms. Based on existing execution models, we focus on two representative coordi-nation paradigms: BSP-style execution follows a structured computation–communication–synchronization pattern with explicit global coordination, whereas task-based execution organizes computation as dependency-driven tasks, enabling more flexible task scheduling and resource mapping. These two paradigms represent different degrees and forms of ex-ecution constraints, providing representative contrasts for analyzing heterogeneous execution. 

System Layer. At this layer, we investigate how the exist-ing system stack should adapt to GPU–WSE architectural asymmetry to support efficient heterogeneous execution. Such architectural asymmetry introduces three key require-ments for the system stack: generating asymmetric workload mappings that account for distinct hardware capabilities, supporting asymmetric communication across architectures with different communication characteristics, and dynami-cally coordinating execution under performance asymmetry across devices. These requirements correspond to the core functionalities of the compiler, communication mechanisms, and runtime, respectively. We therefore analyze the system support required for GPU–WSE heterogeneous execution from these three aspects. 

Hardware Layer. At this layer, we examine whether the potential benefits of heterogeneous execution can trans-late into end-to-end performance gains under interconnect constraints. Heterogeneous execution inevitably introduces cross-architecture communication, and insufficient intercon-nect capability may amplify the impact of architectural asym-metry or offset architectural complementarity, potentially making heterogeneous execution less efficient than single-architecture execution. We therefore characterize the min-imum interconnect bandwidth required for effective GPU– WSE heterogeneous execution.

Workload partitioning can mitigate this imbalance by adjust-ing the amount of work assigned to each domain, but its effectiveness is constrained by the partitioning dimensions and execution constraints of each parallelization strategy.

We select DP and PP as two representative cases of BSP-style execution to study how different coordination con-straints shape the impact of architectural differences. Task-Based Execution. As illustrated in Fig. 4, task-based execution decomposes the workload into dependency-driven tasks and allows them to be independently mapped onto het-erogeneous hardware. Unlike BSP-style execution, it does not require the two hardware domains to maintain matched execution progress at global synchronization boundaries, but is primarily constrained by task dependencies and cross-domain data transfers. Unlike DP and PP, which mainly par-tition homogeneous computation in different ways, we select PD disaggregation as a representative case because prefill and decode naturally form two heterogeneous execution stages with distinct compute and memory characteristics, allowing us to study the impact of heterogeneity within the workload on heterogeneous execution. 

5 Performance Evaluation and Analysis 

In this section, we present experimental results and analysis for three parallelization strategies in heterogeneous systems. We further summarize the key insights of different execution paradigms in heterogeneous settings. 

5.1 Experimental Setup 

We evaluate GPU–WSE heterogeneous execution using com-puting resources at the Argonne Leadership Computing Facil-ity (ALCF), including measurements from a Cerebras WSE-3 cluster and an NVIDIA DGX A100 GPU cluster. We consider models with 7B, 13B, 35B, and 70B parameters, and select hardware configurations for each experiment according to the deployment and parallelization requirements of the corre-sponding workload. DP and PP are evaluated under training workloads, while PD disaggregation is evaluated under infer-ence workloads. We independently measure the execution characteristics of the GPU and WSE on real hardware and combine them using the calibrated discrete-event simulator SimAI [24]. SimAI is designed for large-scale GPU-cluster simulation and supports user-defined workload and mea-sured computation-time inputs, allowing us to incorporate the measured WSE behavior into heterogeneous execution simulation. By replaying measured configurations, we verify that the simulated results closely match the corresponding hardware measurements.

5.2.2 Layer-Wise Performance Analysis. 

Application Layer Analysis. The three execution regimes show that, architectural differences primarily manifest as architectural asymmetry rather than exploitable architectural complementarity for DP. BSP synchronization exposes this asymmetry, while flexible partitioning along the batch dimension provides a degree of freedom to absorb it as workload increases. However, heterogeneous throughput remains bounded by the aggregate standalone capacity of the GPU and WSE, indicating that workload partitioning mitigates architectural asymmetry but does not convert architectural differences into additional complementarity. 

System Layer Analysis. Although DP cannot exploit ar-chitectural complementarity to provide additional perfor-mance gains, proper workload partitioning can still enable heterogeneous execution to approach the aggregate capac-ity of the GPU and WSE, thereby utilizing otherwise idle heterogeneous hardware resources. To achieve this, the sys-tem stack must effectively accommodate architectural asym-metry. First, the compiler needs to generate asymmetric workload partitions according to the different execution ca-pabilities of the two architectures. Second, in the scaling-asymmetry regime, the relative performance of the GPU and WSE varies with workload scale, making the optimal batch partition unstable. The runtime therefore needs to dynami-cally adjust workload allocation based on observed execution behavior to maintain balanced execution across the two hard-ware domains. Finally, because large models are typically distributed across multiple devices in the GPU domain but organized within a single wafer-scale execution domain on the WSE, the two domains use different distributed represen-tations. Cross-domain synchronization therefore requires the communication layer to perform the necessary gradi-ent aggregation and redistribution to ensure correct global synchronization.

5.3.2 Layer-Wise Performance Analysis. 

Application Layer Analysis. The two PP experiments show that, under BSP-style PP execution, architectural differ-ences between GPUs and WSEs likewise primarily manifest as architectural asymmetry rather than exploitable architec-tural complementarity. Unlike DP, which can flexibly adjust the workload assigned to different hardware domains along the data dimension and use uneven data partitioning to ab-sorb performance asymmetry as much as possible, PP parti-tions the workload along the layer dimension and inherently provides less workload-partitioning freedom. This freedom is further restricted by constraints such as a unified micro-batch size, GPU memory capacity, and pipeline structure. As a result, PP has limited ability to flexibly match workloads to the performance characteristics of different architectures, making architectural asymmetry difficult to fully absorb and more likely to translate directly into a pipeline bottleneck. 

System Layer Analysis. PP is not an ideal parallelization strategy for GPU–WSE heterogeneous systems, but it can still provide performance benefits under certain configura-tions by utilizing otherwise idle heterogeneous hardware resources. Therefore, if heterogeneous PP is considered, the most important first step is to determine whether it is actu-ally beneficial. At the compiler level, architectural asymme-try may cause heterogeneous execution to perform worse than execution on a single hardware domain. The compiler therefore needs to perform a feasibility check to determine whether heterogeneous PP can provide performance gains under a given configuration or whether execution should fall back to a single hardware domain. 6 Discussion 

Using the aggregate throughput of independently running GPUs and WSEs as an additive baseline, we observe that DP can readily approach this baseline, PP struggles to reach it, and PD disaggregation can even exceed it. This demonstrates that workload characteristics are the key factor determining whether architectural differences can be effectively exploited. DP provides sufficient flexibility along the data dimension and can therefore absorb performance losses caused by archi-tectural differences by redistributing the workload quantity between the two hardware domains. PP, however, imposes numerous constraints, making it difficult to partition the workload in a way that accommodates architectural differ-ences. PD disaggregation exposes two heterogeneous tasks with different arithmetic intensities, creating opportunities to match workload characteristics with the strengths of each hardware architecture and thereby exploit architectural com-plementarity. Therefore, future GPU–WSE heterogeneous systems should be designed with the characteristics of their target applications in mind to avoid resource underutiliza-tion and performance loss. 

Takeaway 4 : Architectural heterogeneity pays off only when workload structure provides sufficient flexibility to either absorb architectural asymmetry or align hetero-geneous tasks with architecture-specific strengths. The system stack determines whether this potential comple-mentarity can be translated into end-to-end performance gains.

8 Related Work 

Heterogeneous GPU Systems. Existing studies mainly focus on heterogeneous clusters composed of different generations of GPUs within a single vendor ecosystem. In training, systems such as [21], [9], and [26] incorporate GPUs with diverse capabilities into a unified training pipeline through non-uniform partitioning of computation and model structure, to mitigate imbalances in compute and memory resources. In inference, systems such as [20] model GPU and network re-sources, and optimize model placement and request routing to improve serving performance on heterogeneous clusters. In addition, a small number of recent efforts begin to explore

9 Conclusion 

This work studies heterogeneous GPU–WSE execution across the application, system, and architecture layers. Through DP, PP, and P/D disaggregation, we show when architectural differences become performance obstacles or exploitable complementarity, governed primarily by workload partitionability and task–hardware affinity. We further develop a unified performance model to capture these effects and guide heterogeneous system design and scheduling.
