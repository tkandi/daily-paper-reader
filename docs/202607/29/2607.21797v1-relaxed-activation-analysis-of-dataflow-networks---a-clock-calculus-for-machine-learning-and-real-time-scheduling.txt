# Relaxed activation analysis of dataflow networks 

# A clock calculus for machine learning and real-time scheduling 

William Gaudelier Inria, Paris, France william.gaudelier@inria.fr Albert Cohen Google DeepMind, Paris, France albertcohen@google.com Dumitru Potop Butucaru Inria, Paris, France dumitru.potop@inria.fr 

Abstract —Previous work has shown that the simple dataflow primitives of the Lustre language allow the natural, seman-tically unambiguous, and compact representation of machine learning (ML) applications, including models featuring complex conditional execution and recurrent state. The Lustre clock calculus is responsible for the static determination of important properties such as liveness (absence of deadlocks) and static memory bounds. Yet existing clock calculi are tailored for embedded control applications. We show they do not cater for the representation of control patterns commonly found in training algorithms, resulting in cumbersome expressions and inefficient compilation. We propose a conservative extension of Lustre’s clock calculus addressing this limitation, thereby facilitating the embedding of ML models in reactive applications. 

I. I NTRODUCTION 

From embedded control theory and signal processing to data science and machine learning (ML), dataflow formalisms are used for both high-level specification and model exchange, in formalisms such as Simulink [1], Lustre [2], [3], [4], or ONNX [5]. They are also used as support of lower-level optimization and resource allocation algorithms, like in Apache Flink [6], TensorFlow graphs [7], or JAX and XLA’s HLO [8]. The dataflow paradigm is intrinsically concurrent. Within it, large subclasses of reactive and deterministic dataflow applications have been identified [9] that are amenable to efficient implementation in multiple domains. However, while determinism is desirable, it is not sufficient to ensure safe implementation in an embedded context. In particular, it is dif-ficult to ensure the absence of either deadlocks or unbounded accumulation of values (memory leaks). In TensorFlow graphs such activation errors are observable as unspecified behavior [7] or silent errors [10]. Various 

well-formed graph patterns have been proposed [11], but no general analysis exists, making the design and debugging of large graphs difficult [12] and limiting adoption. Other studies have identified classes of dataflow applications which support scalable deadlock/leak analysis. In Synchronous Data Flow (SDF) [13], the analysis is based on conservation laws ensuring that the production and consumption of dataflow messages balance each other during execution, but expressive-ness is limited. In this paper we consider the broader class of reactive, dataflow synchronous systems [14]. These found extensive use in control automation and machine learning, through formalisms such as Simulink, Lustre, or ONNX.

A. Cycles, activation, and clock calculus 

In dataflow synchronous systems, execution is organized as a sequence of execution cycles . Within each execution cycle, each dataflow node can be executed at most once, and each variable can be assigned at most one value. Thus, the 

activation of every computation and the presence of every variable in each cycle can be represented with a stream (sequence) of Booleans indexed by the indices of the cycle. Symbolic representations of these streams of Booleans, known as logical clocks , allow determining that the dataflow model cannot deadlock or accumulate data within any exe-cution cycle . To provide an intuition, consider the following Lustre equation (statement), which is syntactically correct: 

y = x + (x when c); 

It specifies that y is computed as the sum of x and the 

subsampling of x on the Boolean condition c. At cycles where 

x has a value but c is false, the second term of the addition is absent and the computation “diverges”: under dataflow semantics, the value of the left-hand-side operand x of +

must be stored into a queue, possibly leading to an infinite accumulation over execution cycles. Dataflow synchronous semantics outlaw this behavior by requiring both inputs of +

(or any function) to be present (resp. absent) in a given cycle. Detecting such errors can be done at runtime, as in TensorFlow dataflow graphs, but languages such as Lustre require, for safety and efficiency purposes, that the compiler rejects such programs by means of low-complexity static analysis. To this end, the programmer or compiler attach activation information to every dataflow variable in the form of a type; this “activation” type is known as a clock . In our example, assuming that x has a value at every cycle, it has clock “ .”, also called the base clock . Then, the expression “ x when c”will have clock “ . on c ”, meaning that it has a value on all cycles where c is true. Finally, the + operator requires all its inputs to have the same clock, which is not the case here, resulting in a type mismatch. A type system providing such an activation analysis is called a clock calculus [4]. Lustre clocks have the form “ . on c1 on c2 on . . . on cn”where ci is either xi or “ not x i” for some Boolean variable

B. Contribution 

The traditional work around is to adapt the specification itself: when a variable with clock “ . on a on f ” must be used on clock “ . on c on e”, it is first converted to a variable on clock “ .” by padding absent values with some default value. This reduces the efficiency of real-time scheduling [15] and breaks modularity. Furthermore, as we shall see in Section II-D4, the representation of backpropagation training in ML systematically raises this very problem. We thus extend the Lustre clock calculus, henceforth named 

CC-lus , to provide additional expressiveness and ordering freedom when subsampling Boolean variables, when pres-ence permits. The resulting clock calculus CC-new is aconservative extension of CC-lus : existing clock types are preserved with their semantics unchanged. New clocks in-corporate the aliasing and subsampling information to al-low the desired specification style and low-complexity type inference. The clocks defined above are respectively rep-resented as ck 1 =“. on c on (a when c)” and 

ck 2 =“. on a on (c when a)”. We have stated and proved the correctness of CC-new , and we have implemented and tested it in a prototype Lustre compiler.

II. D ATAFLOW PROGRAMMING IN LUSTRE 

A. Overall program structure 

Lustre is the prototypical dataflow synchronous language. It has been the subject of extensive formal analysis [16], with only 4 primitive statements [4] from which all others can be derived. Our analysis naturally covers only this core language. The core syntax is provided in Fig. 1. A Lustre program consists of a sequence of nodes , each defining a dataflow graph . The program in Fig. 2 features 2 nodes. The first implements a convolutional layer; the second instantiates it twice to form a simple ML model. Each node has an interface 

defining its name, inputs and outputs, followed by one or more semicolon-terminated equations (non-terminal eq ) that define the dataflow graph. Library functions such as relu or conv2d are directly accessible as long as they are pure and that they always terminate. To be activated in a cycle, a function call must receive a value on every input. When this happens, it performs its computation and produces a value on its output. The definition of a node can use other Lustre nodes through a process called instantiation . In Fig. 2, the model node instantiates node conv_layer twice. The semantics of in-stantiation is faithfully defined by inlining. A node cannot directly or indirectly instantiate itself. 

B. Stateful programs 

Fig. 2 showcased the modeling of feed-forward networks [17] (e.g. convolutional, residual) where the computations of one execution cycle are independent of those of other cycles. While such networks are preferred for low-level perceptual tasks, many higher-level activities require a temporal context [17], e.g. to determine the speed or intent of a pedestrian or car, or to remember a speed limit. Statefulness is also required, even in feed-forward networks, when the model incorporates scheduling (e.g. pipelining) [18]. Stateful behavior in deep learning typically involves a recurrent neural network (RNN) 

[17]. Recurrent layers range from the simple LSTM (Long Short-Term Memory) and GRU (Gated Recurrent Unit), to SSMs (State Space Models) such as Mamba [19], a low-complexity alternative for transformers. To represent recurrences/state Lustre uses the fby dataflow primitive. In the first cycle where equation “ y = k fby x”is active, the output y is assigned the constant k. In every subsequent cycle where the fby is active, y is assigned the value of x of the previous activation cycle. In other terms, 

fby defines a first-order recurrence relation, just like a unit delay [20] does in control theory. Using fby , the Lustre representation of the generic RNN layer of Keras is:

At each cycle, the RNN layer uses another node (traditionally called the cell layer) to compute the current output and next state from the current input and current state. The next state is propagated to the next cycle using the fby primitive. A node can contain, directly or as part of instantiated nodes, multiple fby primitives. Each one becomes a separate state element. Note that the state is internalized. Exposing it as a tuple or some other data structure is not necessary, unlike in functional ML formalisms such as JAX. This supports a more natural and modular specification style. 

C. Conditional control 

In the dataflow paradigm, computation takes pllace when-ever inputs are ready. Thus, for a function f to execute only at cycles where a condition c is true, one must make sure that 

f only receives its inputs at cycles where c is true. This is the purpose of the subsampling dataflow primitive when . The equation “ x = y when c” defines x to the value of y at cycles where c is true, and leave it absent in all other cycles. The merge primitive does the opposite operation. The equation “ x = merge c y z ” takes as input a Boolean 

c and two data inputs, the first present when c is true, and the second when c is false. At cycles where c is true, the output x takes its value from y. At cycles where c is false, x

takes its value from z.With these two primitives, we can encode the Gated Mixture of Experts (GMoE) layer [21] which enables the efficient execution of LLMs by activating expert networks on demand, as determined by a gating network. In Fig. 3, we provide a minimalist version of GMoE with two experts. Notice how x

is subsampled on conditions c1 and c2 to ensure that the two experts are conditionally executed.

Much of the related ML literature was introduced in the previous sections. We focus here on the most closely related dataflow languages and clock calculi. Dataflow reactive programming allows the natural represen-tation of large classes of ML applications and usage scenarios [23]. In particular, the large variety of ML programming constructs can be expressed using the four primitives of Lustre. Resource allocation can be managed and optimized, from the specification of target architectures and non-functional requirements [24] to static scheduling via solvers [25] or heuristics [26], [27]. This includes scenarios with complex data-dependent control, conditional execution, and pipelining. In the Lustre [2] family of dataflow synchronous languages [14], [27], [26], activation analysis is typically organized as a type system, with emphasis placed on explainability and scala-bility rather than maximal expressiveness [22], [4]. One of the most expressive extensions, known as n-synchrony [28], allows for partial desynchronization. Its ability to concretize and flatten periodic clock expressions—e.g. (001) on (01) is the same as (000001) —facilitates the sort of commutations motivating this paper; yet it is restricted to periodic control. The Signal language family [29] diverges from this trend: its activation analysis is not based on types but on a specialized solver [30]. Extensions include an SMT-solver approach for numeric clock equations [31], and a SAT-based approach [15] to capture Boolean control as closely as possible. With CC-new we maintain the low complexity and explainability of a type system while satisfying the expressive needs of both ML training and real-time resource allocation [15]. IV. T HE NEW CLOCK CALCULUS CC-new 

To allow a natural and modular representation of ML training and resource allocation we extend the Lustre clock calculus CC-lus to make it capable of determining that changes in the sampling order do not change the activation condition. The core calculus is still a Hindley-Milner polymorphic type system, but we adapt unification to include a semantic notion of clock equivalence that subsumes the syntactic clock equality of Lustre. The result is a form of equational unification , or E-unification [32, Section 3]. This extension requires non-trivial changes to both clock syntax and to the underlying type inference engine. While equational unification is undecidable in the general case [32, p.487], our extension of the clock syntax ensures that type checking and inference remain tractable, even though they are extended with a conversion rule that allows switching any clock with an equivalent one.

C. Clock inference algorithm 

Clock inference is realized using a variant of Algorithm W [33]. All expressions, equations, clocks and clock annota-tions of the program are traversed exactly once, in sequence. Starting from the base context Γ0, each step n ≥ 1 of the traversal incrementally builds a new valid substitution τn and a new context Γn = τn(Γ n−1). The context Γn ensures the satisfaction of all constraints associated with the expressions, equations, clocks and clock annotations already traversed. The traversal respects the hierarchy of the program: asubexpression is traversed before the expression, equation, or clock containing it; a clock prefix (which is a clock itself) is traversed before the full clock. For example, consider the expression e = “x when (a when b) + y when (b when a)”which is representative of the problems posed by our moti-vational example. Its subexpressions are e1 =“a when b”,

Well-formed substitutions 

In the proof of Theorem 2 we show (by induction on the rules of the calculus) that the substitutions τi produced during inference are idempotent and well-formed, as defined below. 8

Definition 6.2. A substitution σ is idempotent if and only if 

σ ◦ σ = σ holds. 

Intuitively, idempotent substitutions are those that apply all their changes in a single step, meaning a second application has no further effect. An example of a substitution that is not 

idempotent is [‘x 7 → ‘y, ‘y 7 → ‘z]. Idempotence is guaranteed during inference by ensuring that the variables appearing in the clocks of the substitution are not part of its domain. 

Definition 6.3. A substitution σ is well-formed under a context 

Γ if Γ ⊢ σ(‘x) for all ‘x ∈ dom (σ).

Notice that σ is valid under Γ if and only if σ is well-formed under σ(Γ) .The following lemma establishes that the substitutions generated by our algorithm are valid. Its proof relies on Lemma 0.9, but its significance is best understood here. 

Lemma 0.7. Let Γ be a context. If σ is idempotent and well-formed under Γ, then σ is valid under Γ.Proof. Let ‘x ∈ dom (σ), since σ is well-formed under Γ, we have Γ ⊢ σ(‘x). By Lemma 0.9, the leaves are all (I DENT )whose premise is (|d|, ck d) ∈ Γ where d ∈ subs (σ(‘x)) and 

base (ck d) = base (σ(‘x)) . By idempotency of σ we have that base (σ(‘x)) /∈ dom (σ), thus σ(ck d) = ck d. Therefore 

(|d|, ck d) ∈ σ(Γ) , and so the derivation of Γ ⊢ ‘x is also a derivation of σ(Γ) ⊢ σ(‘x).

Definition 7. We say that an expression e is typable (or

V. C ONCLUSION 

While dataflow principles are pervasive in ML languages and frameworks, the support for data-dependent control re-mains limited in practice. Symmetrically in the domain of embedded systems, the clock calculi of dataflow synchronous languages address many of the issues with data-dependent control. These calculi offer strong guarantees, such as deadlock freedom and static resource allocation. This is encourag-ing news for the progress of ML languages. Unfortunately, backpropagation training, gated mixture of experts, and other important ML algorithms are not modeled efficiently by state-of-the-art clock calculi. This paper extends the Lustre clock 11 

calculus to lift this hurdle. In particular, we allow subsampling Boolean conditions to commute whenever the presence of data allows it. The clock congruence of CC-new reorganizes the clock tree of a Lustre program [34] into a directed acyclic graph (DAG), providing a clean definition of clock intersec-tion. This enables the efficient real-time resource allocation methods showcased in [15] without the need for a SAT-based activation analysis method. This results in a radical improve-ment of both explainability—the extraction of meaningful error diagnostics—and computational complexity. The new clock calculus is a conservative extension of that of Lustre. We stated and proved its correctness. We also implemented it in a prototype compiler, powerful enough to implement complex ML applications featuring GMoE, pipelined execution, and Reinforcement Learning loops. Future work includes showcasing the language expressive-ness and efficient code generation on real-world ML applica-tions, comparing it with mainstream imperative or functional languages such as PyTorch or JAX.
