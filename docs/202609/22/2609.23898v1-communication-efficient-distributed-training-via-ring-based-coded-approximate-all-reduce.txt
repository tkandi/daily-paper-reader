# Communication-Efficient Distributed Training via Ring-Based Coded Approximate All-Reduce 

Sifat Munim and Aditya Ramamoorthy 

Abstract 

Ring All-Reduce is widely used within large-scale distributed training for exact computation of the aggregate gradient. For a system with N workers, its normalized per-worker communication is 2( N − 1) /N . In this work we present a communication-efficient Ring All-Reduce (CERAR) protocol for “approximate” gradient aggregation. CERAR partitions each local gradient into c components and crucially relies on linear encoding and decoding opera-tions. It performs L = c + N − 2 communication rounds over an N -worker ring, yielding normalized communication rate 1 + ( N − 2) /c and normalized storage 1 + N/c . We present an explicit Vandermonde-based construction, whose approximation error can be made arbitrarily close to zero with communication and storage rates approaching one with increasing c. However, this limit is achieved through ill-conditioned encoding matrices. Accordingly, we give a multiplicative perturbation construction whose error is O(ϵ), while the relevant condition numbers are O(ϵ−r⋆ ),where r⋆ = ⌈c/N ⌉ − 1. This naturally motivates a condition-number-constrained optimization formulation for trading off the competing objectives and obtaining numerically stable practical designs. We present experiments on multi-GPU clusters with low-bandwidth and high-bandwidth interconnects. Our results demonstrate clear benefits in the low-bandwidth setting, even for moderate parameter length. We expect corresponding improvements even in the high-bandwidth setting for experiments with much higher parameter lengths. 

I. I NTRODUCTION 

A typical scenario within parameter training involves a dataset D = {(xi, y i)} ˜Ni=1 of ˜N data points, where xi’s and yi’s are the features and labels, respectively. The aim is to minimize a loss function L ≜ 1˜N

In the distributed setting, portions of the dataset are distributed to different workers who compute the gradient on the data points assigned to them. At the t-th iteration, the system aims at computing the overall gradient ∇L = 1˜N

We emphasize that D can also represent a relevant subset of the entire dataset, e.g., when the cluster wants to run mini-batch stochastic gradient descent [1] on an appropriate batch. There are several classes of distributed learning architectures [2] that have been investigated in the literature that differ in how the dataset is divided into chunks and how the workers are connected to each other.

Related Work: Papers on gradient quantization [6], [7], [8], [9], [10], and sparsification [11], [12], [13] aim at reducing the communication load of distributed training by “compressing” the gradients and then communicating the compressed representations. These approaches target the communication issue along an orthogonal direction to ours. Specifically, it may be possible to apply our techniques in conjunction with them. Contributions in the area of gradient coding [14], [15], [16], [17][18] aim at making distributed training robust to node failures; however, these are in the PS + workers model, and, moreover require redundancy in the assignment of chunks to workers. Coded distributed computing [19], [20] is similar in that it trades off computational redundancy for reduced communication for exact computation. Our work is related to approximate gradient coding in the sense that we rely critically on the judicious usage of linear combining gradient chunks and aim at recovering approximate gradients. However, crucially, our setting remains the same as the basic Ring All-Reduce; we do not need redundant chunk assignments.

Our numerical experiments investigate the performance of the CERAR protocol when compared to the production-level NCCL scheme. We note here that there are a wide variety of GPU clusters and network interconnects that are commercially available. In our experiments, we focus on NVIDIA A100 PCIe, and NVIDIA A100 SXM platforms which have peak one-directional bandwidths of approximately 32 GBps and 300 GBps respectively. As our protocol reduces the time in the All-Reduce phase, it is expected that our gains will be highest in low-bandwidth scenarios. Our experiments focus on (i) the amount of time in the All-Reduce phase, (ii) training time and accuracy for a specific model/dataset for a fixed number of iterations, and (iii) evaluation of the gradient quality for the CERAR protocol. All code for recreating these experiments will be uploaded at a GitHub repository. The CERAR scheme was fit using the methods in Section II-D.

L = 26 rounds. The measurement includes the communication and local computation required by each reduction routine, while excluding input generation, buffer initialization, the initial synchronization, and result reporting. As shown in Table I, CERAR clearly outperforms the production NCCL implementation for the PCIe interconnect. On the eight-GPU A100 system, it is between 46 .0% and 47 .1% faster than NCCL. Training time: We trained a Pythia-1.4B model [22], containing approximately 1.41 billion parameters, on the TinyStories dataset [23]. Both systems used four GPUs with CERAR using c = 12 , L = 14 . Within each configuration, both methods (CERAR and NCCL) use the same model initialization, data ordering, and training hyperparameters. We use sequences of length 512, gradient checkpointing, and the AdamW optimizer. The model computation uses a mixture of BF16 and FP32, while model parameters, stored gradients, and gradient reduction use FP32. The learning rate is linearly increased to 3 × 10 −4 and subsequently decayed using a cosine schedule. The training and validation sets contain 120 million and 1 million tokens, respectively. For the PCIe experiment, the training set contains 120 million tokens, each GPU processes a batch of 32 sequences, and the learning-rate warmup lasts 200 steps. We train for 1,800 steps, processing 117.96 million tokens. For the SXM experiment, the training set contains approximately 474.89 million tokens, the per-GPU batch size is 64, and the warmup lasts 100 steps. We train for 7,630 steps, processing approximately 1 billion tokens. As shown in Table II, on the PCIe configuration, NCCL requires 3.79 hours to complete training, whereas our algorithm requires 3.14 hours. Thus, our algorithm reduces the overall training time by 17.3%, saving approximately 39 minutes. On the SXM configuration, NCCL and our algorithm require 4.578 and 4.745 hours, respectively; our algorithm takes 3.65% longer. These are comparisons within the configuration, since the training settings differ between the two configurations. The final validation losses obtained using NCCL and our algorithm are 1.4218 and 1.4213, respectively, on PCIe, and both are approximately 1.16449 on SXM. Moreover, the validation-loss trajectories in the PCIe experiment remain closely aligned throughout training. Thus, our algorithm achieves faster training on the PCIe configuration while attaining comparable final validation loss on both configurations.
